Building Event-Driven Microservices 19: Supportive Tooling

Source: Adam Bellemare, Building Event-Driven Microservices, 2nd edition (O'Reilly, 2025), chapter 19. Previous: Building Event-Driven Microservices 18: Handling Bad Data in Event Streams. Next: Building Event-Driven Microservices 20: Testing Event-Driven Microservices.

Takeaways
  • Tooling lets you manage event-driven microservices and event streams at scale; much of it must be self-service (DevOps: testing, deployments, rollbacks, scaling, debugging) and owned by the team that owns the service.
  • The author's view: choose fully managed cloud services where possible, self-hosted only when necessary.
  • Core tools: infrastructure as code, IAM, a microservice-to-team assignment system, stream creation, metadata tags, quotas, a schema registry, permissions (ACL or RBAC), schema change notifications, offset management and application reset, lag monitoring, streamlined service creation, CMS controls, multicluster replication, and dependency and topology visualisation.
  • Reduce tribal knowledge: codify ownership, schemas and metadata; ambiguity multiplies with each instance.

19.1 Scope and build versus buy

  • Covers tools you are likely to use, which you may build, install from free open source, or get within a SaaS offering. It does not recommend a product
    • it links to free open source examples
      • Many tools have command-line interfaces for administrators, but you also need self-service tools that automate common steps for DevOps
      • Your tooling will be heavily influenced by your organisation's practices, cloud providers, frameworks, languages and skills
    • there is no one right way.
  • Build versus buy
    • depends first on higher-level requirements (legal, data on-shoring rules): extremely sensitive data may force self-hosting on your own hardware in your own buildings
    • otherwise you may use fully managed services and drop all infrastructure work. Second, precedent: if you already run on a cloud provider you will almost certainly run your microservices there, reusing its deployment, logging, monitoring and access control.
  • The author's preference:
    • fully managed cloud services where possible, self-hosted only when necessary, because "a business' success is not dependent on getting good at building self-hosted platforms, but it is dependent on satisfying its customer's needs"
      • Managed services start and move faster, letting you focus on business problems
    • the total cost of ownership appears as a monthly bill that seems higher but hides no costs such as debugging and engineering time
      • As you grow you can renegotiate prices or move some of the platform to self-hosting if scale justifies the engineering investment
    • crunch your own numbers.

19.2 Infrastructure as code

  • IaC
    • manages and provisions infrastructure with code instead of manual processes: define event brokers, a schema registry, a CMS, a microservice in configuration files that automate creation, updates, scaling and deletion. Frameworks: Terraform (proprietary licence, formerly open source)
    • Pulumi (described as a Terraform fork with an MPL-2.0 licence)
    • Ansible
    • Puppet, plus the cloud providers' own tools (often compatible with open source ones).
  • Benefits:
    • automation (less manual effort and error)
    • reproducibility (identical environments for development, testing and production)
    • version control (Git history, collaboration, rollback)
    • scalable
    • cloud-provider agnostic
    • aligned with DevOps (easier hiring).
  • Cluster creation matters more as usage grows:
    • a small or medium company can often use a single event broker cluster
    • larger companies face pressure to provide several for technical and legal reasons. IaC is the best way beyond manually configured single deployments.

19.3 Identity, ownership and stream creation

  • Identity and access management (IAM):
    • manages and controls access to resources so only authorised individuals or machines reach data or systems
      • Two flavours: open source (Keycloak, Zitadel; you install, configure, manage and integrate) or proprietary integrated (AWS, GCP, Azure, Cloudflare; deeply integrated, possibly with external services) and streaming platforms such as Confluent
      • Features: identity management for users (services, applications, people), groups and roles
    • role-based access control and permission assignment
    • authentication and authorisation
    • security and encryption
    • regulatory requirements. Guided by what you already have
    • consult technical leadership
    • several IAMs often coexist (multi-cloud, large organisations).
  • Microservice-to-team assignment system:
    • attributes ownership of a service to a team. Easy to track in a small organisation but hard with microservices
    • ties to IAM (who may update, deploy, roll back, and who answers for errors)
    • enables event-stream ownership via the single writer principle (see "Event-Driven Microservice Responsibilities")
      • Start as a spreadsheet linking service identities to the owner's email or chat ID, then integrate with IAM, the broker(s) and the CMS
      • Make it a top priority
    • it becomes essential for navigating service dependencies.
  • Event-stream creation and modification:
    • teams must create and modify streams
    • microservices should automatically create their own internal streams with full control over partition count, retention policy and replication factor
      • A critical, sensitive, never-lose stream: infinite retention, high replication factor
    • a high-volume stream of unimportant updates: high partition count, low replication factor, short retention. Assign ownership to a microservice or external system when created.
  • Metadata tagging
    • (organise services and streams in one searchable place: ownership, costs, business lines)
    • Only teams with production rights to a stream may edit its tags
    • Tools: Apache Atlas, Amundsen, OpenMetadata (all Apache 2.0 licensed; others exist, some paid)
    • Useful tags:
    • Stream owner (service) for change requests and audits.
    • PII: stricter handling; restrict access unless the data's team approves.
    • Financial information: money, billing, revenue events; similar but not identical to PII.
    • Namespace: aligned with nested bounded contexts; hide streams from services outside it to reduce discovery overload.
    • Deprecation:
      • grandfathered consumers continue, new ones are blocked from subscribing, used for breaking format changes
      • notify the owner when no consumers remain, then delete safely.
    • Custom tags.

19.4 Quotas, registry and permissions

  • Quotas:
    • a maximum cap on broker throughput for a client (for example Apache Kafka quotas), such as allowing only 10% of CPU time to serve one producer or consumer group
      • They prevent self-inflicted denial of service from a chatty producer or a highly parallel consumer group starting a huge stream from the beginning, so the cluster is not saturated by one service (it is throttled)
      • Tip: set a maximum global quota of 25% for the broker as a starting point
    • allow surge-prone services up to 75%
    • consider no limit for producers handling external data sources (a throttled producer may drop data or crash).
  • Schema registry
    • (see "The Role of the Schema Registry"): enables explicit Avro, Protobuf or JSON Schema definitions (names, types, defaults, documentation)
    • Benefits: less bandwidth (no schema per event), a single point of reference, data discovery, evolution enforcement and protection against accidental changes
    • Choice follows your broker; for Kafka the Confluent Schema Registry is a free, common option supporting Avro, Protobuf and JSON.
  • Event-stream permissions:
    • ACLs
      • grant access per entity (each producer and consumer needs its own set; every change applied individually)
      • RBAC defines roles with permission sets assigned to many entities (change the role and all update), easier for large organisations
      • Apache Kafka and Apache Pulsar
        • come with only ACLs
        • RBAC is more common in cloud-vendor managed services and needs identity management integration (Confluent for its cloud and platform; StreamNative for its Pulsar service).
    • Access to a stream is granted only by the team that owns the producing service, enforced via the assignment system
      • Categories (broker-dependent): READ, WRITE, CREATE, DELETE, MODIFY, DESCRIBE.
    • Warning:
      • permissions need identification of each producer and consumer
      • enable and enforce it from day one (adding it later requires updating and reviewing every connecting service).
    • Access control protects bounded contexts:
      • a service is the only owner of CREATE, WRITE and READ on its internal and changelog streams and never couples on another's
      • By the single writer principle exactly one service has WRITE on a stream
      • Streams may be public or restricted (sensitive financial or PII).
    • Table 19-1:
      • typical permissions for a microservice: **input streams: READ
      • output streams: CREATE, WRITE (and maybe READ if used internally)
      • internal and changelog streams: CREATE, WRITE, READ.**
    • Let individuals grant and revoke access for their team's streams and services, or centralise requests with a security review for sensitive data (guided by tags)
      • Keep grants and revocations as their own event stream: a durable, immutable access record for auditing.
    • Orphans:
      • cross-reference permissions with existing streams and services: a stream with no consumers may be eligible for deprecation and deletion
      • a service writing to a stream nobody consumes may itself be deletable.
  • Schema creation and modification notifications:
    • with many services it is hard to tell other teams a schema they depend on will evolve. A notification system alerts consumers before their input schemas evolve
    • ACLs and RBAC (the permissions graph) show which service consumes which stream and which team owns it
      • Tip: use Git webhooks to alert when a schema file changes in a pull request
      • It avoids breaking changes and flags changes that expose sensitive information or bloat events
    • hook up automatic checks (for example PII scans).

19.5 Offsets, reset, lag, creation, CMS

  • Consumer offset management: normally the offset advances as processing completes, but manual changes are needed for:
    • Application reset: resetting the offset to reprocess after a logic change (usually the beginning).
    • Application reset: advancing the offset to the latest when old data is not needed.
    • Application recovery: specifying the offset, such as a point in time for multicluster failover (for example N minutes before the crash so no replicated events are missed).
    • Only the owning team or an administrator may modify a group's offsets.
  • State management and application reset:
    • resetting a stateful application's state is common when its implementation changes. The tool must: delete internal and changelog streams
    • delete state store materialisations (if applicable)
    • reset consumer group offsets
      • to the beginning for each input stream or a user-chosen point
      • Where the platform supports it, also reset external state stores (for example Amazon DynamoDB or Google Bigtable), reducing operational overhead and removing stale or erroneous data
    • unsupported services need manual resets. It should be self-serve but never let a team delete another's streams and state: use the assignment system (owner or admin only).
  • Consumer offset lag monitoring:
    • consumer lag
      • is one of the best scaling indicators
      • Lag is the difference in event count between the most recent event and the last processed event for a consumer group
      • Warning: verify lag is not caused by service errors (a service throwing errors and not progressing will not be helped by scaling)
      • A basic threshold: if lag exceeds N events for M minutes, double the processors and rebalance
    • scale back down when resolved
      • Systems such as Burrow (Kafka) consider lag history (with high volume lag is 0 only for a split second, so a periodic measure always looks lagging; deviation from historical norms shows falling behind or catching up)
      • Use hysteresis (a tolerance threshold) to avoid scaling up and down endlessly
    • modern clouds (AWS CloudWatch, Google Cloud Operations) can accommodate it.
  • Streamline the microservice creation process:
    • automate a typical process: create a repository
    • integrate with the CI pipeline ("Continuous Integration, Delivery, and Deployment Systems")
    • configure webhooks and dependencies
    • assign ownership via the assignment system
    • register for permissions on input streams
    • create output streams and apply ownership permissions
    • offer a template or code generator for the skeleton
      • It saves time over many repetitions and keeps templates current
      • Warning: otherwise people copy and paste an existing service into a new repository and edit settings, which is error-prone and causes permission errors, malconfigured services and repetitive break-fix.
  • Container management controls:
    • Kubernetes or a cloud equivalent (see "Managing Containers and Virtual Machines"). Expose so teams provide their own DevOps: environment variables
    • which cluster (testing, integration, production)
    • CPU, memory and disk
    • autoscaling by CPU, memory, disk or lag
    • manual scaling. The business decides which controls go to developers versus an operations team
    • Kubernetes RBAC gives fine-grained permissions
    • the Kubernetes Dashboard UI is part of the official project.

19.6 Multicluster deployments and event-stream replication

  • Reasons for several broker, CMS or service clusters:
    • data locality laws
    • automatic failover and disaster recovery
    • reduced data transfer costs
    • workload isolation. The subject is too broad to cover (on premises, cloud, hybrid)
    • consult documentation for your broker, CMS and state stores
      • Within scope: running multiple clusters in different regions needs programmatic cluster management (IaC) and standardised creation and maintenance to avoid repetitive break-fix.
  • Event-stream replication
    • reasons: a hub-and-spoke model with smaller regional clusters feeding aggregates to a central one
    • replicating core business entities to cut cross-region access costs
    • a replica of production data for testing services before deploying to the real thing.
  • Questions for a replication tool:
    • does it automatically replicate new streams and deletions
    • does it replicate exactly (same offsets, partitions, timestamps) or approximately
    • does it replicate schemas, permissions and consumer group offsets
    • what is the latency
    • can it scale?
  • Exact replication is hard, especially for failover:
    • many tools use the broker's producer and consumer interface and not a deep file replication, so data is copied but offsets, ordering and partitioning are not preserved
      • Apache Kafka MirrorMaker 2 (a marked improvement over version 1) keeps a mapping between source and destination offsets to translate offsets when consumers migrate
      • Proprietary tools are simpler and more precise but vendor-bound: Confluent Cluster Linking gives a perfect byte-to-byte replica (offsets, partitions, consumer groups)
    • Amazon MSK Replicator is similar to MirrorMaker 2 with offset mapping
    • both only for customers of the respective service.

19.7 Dependency tracking and topology visualisation

  • Knowing which microservices read and write which streams helps run an event-driven organisation
    • Self-reporting is voluntary: some teams forget or opt out, leaving gaps and incomplete topologies
    • Instead derive dependencies from the permissions structure (ACLs and RBAC): a service cannot operate without registering permissions, and any permission change updates the topology automatically.
  • Uses:
    • Determine data lineage:
      • trace each ancestor service and stream of an event
      • replaying the permissions and assignment event streams gives the topology at a past time (useful for auditing old data).
    • Overlay team boundaries: show which teams are responsible for which services.
    • Discover data sources: see which streams exist and their producers and consumers.
    • Measure interconnectedness and complexity: count a team's internal connections versus cross-boundary connections (fewer external is generally better, though a simple count is a basic metric that still reveals relative interdependence).
    • Map business requirements to microservices:
      • state each service's business requirements alongside its code (README or metadata store), mapped to teams
      • a business owner asks "Does this implementation structure align with the goals and priorities of this team?".
  • Figure 19-1:
    • 25 microservices and four teams, each arrow a stream produced and consumed
    • team 2 owns two services (2 and 7) outside its main bounded context with dependencies on teams 1, 3 and 4, enlarging its "surface area"
      • Table 19-2: team 1: 1 incoming and 3 outgoing streams, 5 services
    • team 2: 8 incoming, 2 outgoing, 3 incoming and 2 outgoing team connections, 8 services
    • team 3: 3 and 3, 6 services
    • team 4: 1 and 5, 8 services
      • Reassigning service 7 to team 1 or 3, service 2 to team 4 (and maybe service 1 to team 4) gives Figure 19-2 and Table 19-3: team 2 drops to 4 incoming and 0 outgoing streams and 6 services
    • cross-team incoming and outgoing stream counts fall with a net decrease of three connections among teams.
  • Caveat:
    • fewer cross-boundary connections reduce complexity but are no guarantee
    • consider head count, expertise, implementation and history
      • A team that only sources and organises external data into events may legitimately have many connections
      • Ask qualitative questions: does service 2's function align with team 2's or team 4's goals
    • service 7 with team 1's? Team goals change with business needs, so keep assignments aligned.

Summary points

  • As service counts grow, no one can know how everything works:
    • keep explicit track of service and stream ownership, schemas and metadata to track change over time
    • reduce tribal knowledge and codify properties, since ambiguity is amplified with each instance.
  • Autonomy and control over a team's services matter: under DevOps principles manage your own services with minimal administrative overhead.
Terms to carry forward
  • IaC; IAM; ACL versus RBAC; single writer permissions; metadata tags.
  • Quota (25% global); offset reset; lag and hysteresis; MirrorMaker 2, Cluster Linking.
