Building Event-Driven Microservices 22: Conclusion

Source: Adam Bellemare, Building Event-Driven Microservices, 2nd edition (O'Reilly, 2025), chapter 22. Previous: Building Event-Driven Microservices 21: Deploying Event-Driven Microservices.

Takeaways
  • Event streams give well-defined, low-latency data to whichever services need it, as many times as needed; microservices let you pick the best technology per problem. Together they give resilient, decoupled services that communicate durably and asynchronously.
  • The data communication structure decouples the ownership and production of data from its access and consumption.
  • Align services on bounded contexts, pay the microservice tax incrementally, design events deliberately (state, delta, hybrid), use schemas, liberate data and make the event broker the single source of truth.
  • Pick the implementation (lightweight, heavyweight, BPC, FaaS, streaming SQL) to fit; test with composed events and temporary environments; deploy with owner autonomy while weighing SLAs, state rebuilds and reconsumption.

22.1 Recap

The data communication structure

  • It gives access to important business data across the company without ad hoc solutions. Event brokers let services reach any data written to a stream, organise it strictly, propagate updates in near real time and operate at very large scale
    • the broker is agnostic to business logic and simply stores events resiliently and distributes them to subscribers.
  • Producers get readily available, low-latency, decoupled access for all consumers and no longer provide custom query endpoints, focusing on their primary use cases
    • Consumers use their own logic, processing power and storage.
  • A mature structure decouples ownership and production of data from its access and consumption: applications no longer do double duty (internal logic plus synchronisation and outside access)
    • A failed instance no longer makes data inaccessible, only delays new data until the producer returns.

Business domains and bounded contexts

  • A business has a domain, broken into subdomains
    • solutions are described by bounded contexts that identify boundaries (inputs, outputs, events, requirements, processes, data models) of the subdomain
    • Align microservices with a bounded context: a small context may use one service, a larger one several
    • The result is decoupled services updated independently, meeting changing business needs while preserving team autonomy.

Tooling, infrastructure and the microservice tax

  • Event-driven microservices need investment in systems and tools to operate at scale: the microservice tax
    • The event broker is at the heart, providing the fundamental communication
    • Each new service needs its own repository, deployment pipeline, monitoring, logging and processing and storage resources
      • a custom process per service is costly, while letting teams do these steps themselves pays off.
  • The tax includes:
    • the event broker and streams
    • schema registry and metadata management
    • access control management
    • the container management system with resource management
    • continuous integration, delivery and deployment
    • monitoring and logging. Paying it is not all-or-nothing: start with a broker or CMS and add the rest as needed.

Event design

  • State events:
    • an entity's state at a point in time with all related fields
    • may hold only the current state or earlier versions too (before/after)
    • use a primary key to enable copartitioning and compaction
    • support event-carried state transfer.
  • Delta events:
    • the change from one state to another, usually only changed fields so the consumer composes state
    • not compactable (storage grows)
    • support event sourcing, most used for state changes within a bounded context, not between them.
  • Hybrid events: state plus the deltas that led to it: a compromise at the cost of production and consumption complexity, storage and network.
  • Normalisation:
    • relational databases handle joins on data at rest and benefit from normalised schemas (third or fourth normal form)
    • in streams joins are more complex and expensive, so a denormalised approach helps. Trade-offs: larger events, more storage and network, harder for the producer
    • consumers avoid expensive stream joins and can use a wider range of frameworks.

Schematised events

  • Schemas play a critical role in communicating meaning:
    • strongly typed events make producers and consumers face the realities of data instead of hoping someone else figures it out
    • Producers must emit according to the schema and consumers code against it, reducing misinterpretation and bad data and providing a contract for future changes.
  • Schema evolution lets producers add fields while consumers that do not care keep older schemas, cutting the frequency and risk of nonessential changes; the rest upgrade independently.
  • Field-level encryption
    • (only authorised consumers decrypt specific fields) and value constraints (a positive integer age, alphabetical names). Code generation creates classes or structures so problems surface early
    • the schema registry is a searchable map of data to streams for data discovery.

Data liberation and the single source of truth

  • Populate streams with business-critical data
    • liberating it from services and data stores takes time but is essential for decoupling and moving toward an event-driven architecture. Tip: with data in streams, services are built by composition: a new service subscribes to the streams of interest through the broker instead of connecting to every service that would provide the data.
  • Start with data most commonly used and critical to the organisation's next major goals
    • Weigh each extraction method's impact on the existing service against the risks of stale data, lack of schemas and exposed internal data models.

Microservices

  • Small, purpose-built services solving specific business problems
    • most sustainable when aligned with business-focused bounded contexts, isolating them from changes elsewhere except where business changes require it. Many technologies work (lightweight, heavyweight, BPC, FaaS, streaming SQL), with internal versus external state and containers (most common) versus VMs (less common) to decide.
  • Not all microservices need be micro:
    • several larger services are reasonable, especially without paying the microservice tax in part or full
    • "There is no award for building the most microservices, nor is there an award for having just a single massive service."
  • Fine-grained, decoupled new services get easier if you:
    • publish commonly used and important business entities and events to the broker
    • use the broker as the single source of truth driving microservices
    • make it easy to set up, use and manage your own microservices.

Microservice implementation options

  • Lightweight frameworks:
    • the greatest out-of-the-box functionality: streams materialised to tables and maintained indefinitely
    • joins (including on foreign keys) across streams and tables
    • hot replicas, durable storage and changelogs for resiliency and scalability.
  • Heavyweight frameworks:
    • similar functionality but less processing independence (a separate dedicated cluster)
    • new cluster options such as Kubernetes integration are growing
    • often already used in mid-sized and larger organisations for big-data analysis.
  • Basic producer/consumer (BPC):
    • flexible across languages and runtimes but limited to basic consumption and production
    • deterministic behaviour is difficult (no built-in event scheduling)
    • complex operations need your own custom libraries or simpler use cases that tolerate nondeterministic processing.
  • Functions as a service (FaaS):
    • similar to BPC but with more streamlined management and scaling
    • simple stateless and stateful processing and orchestration for complex workflows (orchestrator functions are not available from all providers).

Testing

  • Event-driven microservices suit unit and integration testing: compose events for a wide range of cases (order, timestamps, partitions, contents across input streams), structured by the event schemas including constraints and edge cases.
  • Local testing
    • covers unit and integration tests, using dynamically created brokers, schema registries and other dependencies: a broker in the same executable as the test (numerous JVM solutions) or in a container beside the application
    • full control lets you simulate load, race conditions, outages and failures.
  • Production integration testing:
    • dynamically create a temporary broker, populate it with copies of production streams and events (barring information-security concerns) and run the service: a smoke test before deploying, plus performance tests of a single instance and horizontal scaling
    • then discard the environment.

Deploying

  • Owners must be able to deploy and roll back quickly and easily: autonomy removes bottlenecks of an infrastructure team solely responsible for deployments. CI, delivery and deployment pipelines provide a streamlined, customisable process that reduces manual steps and scales to other services
    • the CMS may add help with deployments and rollbacks.
  • Deployment must consider SLAs, state rebuilds and reconsumption of input streams. SLAs are not only downtime but the impact on all downstream consumers and the health of the broker: a rebuilding service may place considerable load on the broker, force downstream consumers to scale up and "process millions or billions of events in short order"
    • quotas mitigate but a rebuilding service may stay inconsistent too long for downstream needs.
  • Trade-offs
    • between SLAs, downstream impact, broker impact and monitoring and alerting: for example blue-green needs two consumer groups to be considered for monitoring and alerting, including lag-based autoscaling
    • An alternative to blue-green is a thin, always-on serving layer handling synchronous requests while the backend event processor is swapped out and reprocesses in its own time: it may serve stale data for a while but needs no tooling augmentations or complex swaps and may still meet dependent SLAs.

22.2 Final words

  • Event-driven microservices are powerful, flexible, resilient and scalable: purpose-built services aligned with business goals using any frameworks, languages, stores and tooling.
  • Success needs a rethink of what data is and how services access and use it: gone are the days of all organisational data in one database and one monolithic service
    • Event streams provide the data communication structure many organisations lack: publishing important data makes it widely available to any authorised service, system, team or person, relieving services of serving other bounded contexts' query needs.
  • Services compose their own data models from streams, giving flexibility for business units to use whatever data they need
    • Even organisations with just a handful of services can benefit, paving the way for new services decoupled from old requirements and implementations.
  • Closing: the data communication structure extends the power of an organisation's data to any service or team that needs it, eliminates access boundaries, and reduces unnecessary complexity in production and distribution, "a solid foundation for you to build the services your organization needs to find ultimate business success".
Terms to carry forward
  • Data communication structure; bounded context; microservice tax; single source of truth.
  • State, delta, hybrid events; schema evolution; data liberation.
  • Lightweight, heavyweight, BPC, FaaS; blue-green alternative (thin serving layer).
