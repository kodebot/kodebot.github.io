Building Microservices 1: What Are Microservices?

Source: Sam Newman, Building Microservices, 2nd ed. (O'Reilly, 2021), ch. 1. Previous: Building Microservices 0: Preface. Next: Building Microservices 2: How to Model Microservices.

Takeaways
  • Microservices are independently releasable services modelled around a business domain. A service is a black box: other services see only its network endpoints; language and data store stay hidden (information hiding).
  • The one idea to keep: independent deployability. Deploy and release a single microservice without deploying anything else; many good things follow.
  • Services own their own state: avoid shared databases. Size matters least; focus on how many services you can handle and where to draw boundaries.
  • Architecture follows organisation (Conway's law): slice by business domain, not by tier, so one team owns an end-to-end change.
  • Monoliths (single-process, modular, distributed) are a valid, sensible default; adopt microservices only for a reason, incrementally ("turn a dial, not a switch").

1.1 Microservices at a glance

  • Microservices are independently releasable services modelled around a business domain. One might be inventory, another order management, another shipping
    • together an ecommerce system.
  • They are a type of service-oriented architecture (SOA), but opinionated about where boundaries go, with independent deployability as the key. They are technology agnostic.
  • From outside, a service is a black box hosting business functionality on network endpoints (a queue, a REST API) over whatever protocol fits
    • Consumers use the endpoints; implementation details are hidden
    • So shared databases are avoided in most cases: each service encapsulates its own database where needed.

  • Information hiding
    • (David Parnas, 1971, "Information Distribution Aspects of Design Methodology") means hiding as much as possible inside a component and exposing as little as possible
    • It separates what changes easily from what is hard to change
    • Hidden implementation can change freely as long as the interface does not break backward compatibility, so changes inside a service should not affect upstream consumers
    • Stable boundaries give looser coupling and stronger cohesion.
  • Hexagonal Architecture
    • (Alistair Cockburn) keeps internal implementation separate from external interfaces, so the same functionality can be reached over different interface types
    • Newman draws microservices as hexagons, as a homage and to tell them from "normal" services.
Are SOA and microservices different?
  • SOA: multiple services (usually separate OS processes) collaborate via network calls; it emerged against large monoliths and aims at reuse and replaceability.
  • SOA is "a sensible idea", but there was no consensus on doing it well. Many problems blamed on SOA were really protocols (SOAP), vendor middleware, no guidance on granularity, or poor places to split. Newman suggests vendors co-opted the movement to sell products.
  • Counter-example he has seen: teams made services smaller but kept everything coupled to a database and deployed it together: service oriented, not microservices.
  • Microservices are a specific approach to SOA, as Extreme Programming or Scrum are specific approaches to Agile.

1.2 Key concepts

Independent deployability

  • Make a change to a microservice, deploy and release it without deploying any other microservice. It must be how you manage deployments by default, a discipline.
  • Tip from the book: if you take one thing from the book, embrace independent deployability: get into the habit of deploying one microservice to production without deploying anything else.
  • It needs loose coupling (change one service without changing others) and explicit, well-defined, stable contracts. Sharing databases makes it especially hard.
  • It acts as a forcing function: to achieve it you must get many other things right, each with its own benefits. It also guides how you find boundaries.

Modelled around a business domain

  • Use domain-driven design to structure code like the real-world domain, and the same idea to define service boundaries
    • That makes it easier to roll out new functionality and recombine services.
  • A feature touching several services is expensive: coordinate work across teams and manage deployment order. So make cross-service changes infrequent.
  • A three-tier layered architecture puts each layer behind a service boundary grouped by technology
    • real changes usually span presentation, application and data. Microservices prioritise cohesion of business functionality over cohesion of technical functionality: services are end-to-end slices.

Owning their own state

  • A service needing another's data asks that service
    • This lets each decide what is shared and what is hidden, separating freely changeable internals from the infrequently changed external contract
    • It resembles encapsulation in OO programming, itself information hiding.
  • Tip from the book: don't share databases unless you really need to; it is "one of the worst things you can do" for independent deployability.
  • Services are slices that, where appropriate, encapsulate UI, business logic and data; hiding the database also reduces coupling.

Size

  • "How big?" is the most common question and, in Newman's view, among the least interesting.
  • Lines of code are meaningless (25 lines of Java may be 10 of Clojure).
  • James Lewis
    • (Thoughtworks): a microservice "should be as big as my head", read as: as big as your head, small enough to be understood
    • experienced teams can manage larger codebases.
  • Chris Richardson (Microservice Patterns): the goal is "as small an interface as possible", which fits information hiding, though the original term was not about interface size.
  • Size is contextual: a 100,000-line system is easy for a 15-year veteran and too big for a newcomer; a company with 10 services answers differently from one with hundreds.
  • Focus instead on:
    • how many microservices you can handle, since complexity rises and you need new skills (hence incremental migration)
    • how to define boundaries so it does not become a horribly coupled mess

Flexibility

  • James Lewis: "microservices buy you options". They cost something; decide whether the options are worth it (organisational, technical, scale, robustness).
  • Adopting microservices is less like flipping a switch, more like turning a dial: more services give more flexibility and more pain, so turn it gradually and stop if needed.

Alignment of architecture and organisation

  • MusicCorp
    • (a fictional composite company selling CDs online) has a web UI, a monolithic backend and a database, owned by separate teams
    • Adding "favourite genre" needs changes in all three, coordinated and deployed in order.
  • Conway's law
    • (Melvin Conway, "How Do Committees Invent?"): organisations produce designs that copy their communication structures
    • Three tiers is Conway's law in action: IT used to group people by competency (DBAs, Java developers, frontend developers).
  • Forces changed:
    • poly-skilled teams to reduce hand-offs and silos, and faster delivery
    • A layered architecture has high cohesion of related technology but low cohesion of business functionality.
  • Alternative:
    • break organisation and architecture along vertical business lines. A Customer microservice owned by a profile team holds a thin slice of UI, logic and data
    • the genre list comes from a Catalog microservice
    • a new Recommendation microservice can read the favourite genre in a later release.
  • A stream-aligned team (Skelton and Pais, Team Topologies) is aligned to a single, valuable stream of work and can build and deliver value quickly, safely and independently, without hand-offs (chapters 14 and 15).

[!note] "Fake" companies MusicCorp is a composite of many organisations (not one real engagement). FoodCo, AdvertCo and PaymentCo are real companies with names changed and extraneous detail omitted; FinanceCo also appears.

1.3 The monolith

Here a monolith is a unit of deployment: when all functionality must be deployed together. Three forms:

Form Description
Single-process monolith All code in one process (maybe several instances); often really a simple distributed system because of the database and clients. Most real systems are more complex (two coupled monoliths, vendor software). Can make sense for smaller organisations (David Heinemeier Hansson, "The Majestic Monolith").
Modular monolith A single process divided into modules worked on independently but deployed together. Modules date back to 1970s structured programming. Shopify is a cited success. Weakness: the database often is not decomposed, so later pulling it apart is hard; some teams split the database along module lines.
Distributed monolith Multiple services that must be deployed together. It has the disadvantages of a distributed system and a single-process monolith without enough upsides of either. It arises when too little attention went to information hiding and cohesion of business functionality, so changes ripple across boundaries. (Quote: Leslie Lamport on a distributed system being one where a computer you did not know existed can break yours.)

Delivery contention and the advantages of monoliths

  • Delivery contention:
    • many people in one place get in each other's way (editing the same code, different release timing, unclear ownership and decisions)
    • Studies show confused ownership hurts (Microsoft Research: "Don't Touch My Code!", Bird et al.)
    • A monolith does not guarantee it and microservices do not prevent it, but microservices give concrete ownership boundaries.
  • Single-process and modular monoliths have real advantages:
    • simpler deployment topology avoids distributed-system pitfalls
    • simpler developer workflow, monitoring, troubleshooting and end-to-end testing
    • simpler code reuse (in a distributed system you must copy code, extract libraries or push it into a service)
  • "Monolith" has come to mean "legacy", which is a problem. A monolith is a valid choice and the sensible default: Newman looks for a reason to use microservices, not a reason not to.

1.4 Enabling technology

Do not adopt lots of new technology at first. As you grow, spot problems and then find tools.

  • Log aggregation and distributed tracing
    • Log aggregation is a prerequisite for microservices: a central place to analyse logs and alert.
    • Correlation IDs tie together a chain of calls triggered by one user action.
    • Tracing tools:
      • Jaeger (tracing)
      • Lightstep and Honeycomb (explore a running system; Figure 1-9 shows a Honeycomb trace of where time goes across services). Newman likes Humio for logs
      • cloud vendors' simple services may be enough to start.
  • Containers and Kubernetes
    • Containers isolate each service instance (one cannot eat all the CPU) more lightly than virtualisation, with faster spin-up and lower cost
      • Kubernetes orchestrates them across machines (chapter 8).
    • Do not rush:
      • hard to justify with few services ("You don't need a Kubernetes cluster when you have three services!")
      • If you do, have someone else run the cluster, such as a managed cloud service.
  • Streaming:
    • for sharing data between services and moving from batch to real-time. Apache Kafka is the de facto choice (message permanence, compaction, scale)
    • KSQLDB and Apache Flink for stream processing
    • Debezium streams data from existing datasources over Kafka (chapter 4).
  • Public cloud and serverless:
    • Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure and AWS offer managed services (databases, Kubernetes, brokers, file systems), offloading work. Serverless products hide the machines
    • FaaS lets you deploy code and let the platform spin up instances on demand (chapter 8).

1.5 Advantages of microservices

Many are benefits of any distributed system, but microservices achieve them more because they are opinionated about boundaries, combining information hiding and domain-driven design with distribution.

Advantage What it means
Technology heterogeneity Pick the right tool per service (for example a graph database for social interactions, a document store for posts). Try new technology on a low-risk service. Some constrain choices (Netflix and Twitter mostly use the JVM). Hidden implementation eases upgrades per service.
Robustness Service boundaries are bulkheads: a failure need not cascade. But you must understand new failure sources (networks and machines fail); some teams ended up less robust after migrating.
Scaling Scale only the services that need it (Gilt: monolithic Rails app from 2007, overwhelmed by 2009, now over 450 microservices). On-demand provisioning ties scaling to cost savings (chapter 13).
Ease of deployment A one-line change need not redeploy a million-line app; deploy a single service, isolate problems, roll back quickly. Reason Amazon and Netflix use these architectures.
Organisational alignment Smaller teams on smaller codebases are more productive; ownership can change as the organisation does.
Composability Open "seams" so functionality can be recombined for web, native, mobile web, tablet or wearable channels, instead of one coarse-grained seam.

1.6 Microservice pain points

Most are problems of distributed systems and would show up in a distributed monolith too; "the bulk of this book is about dealing with the pain, suffering, and horror of owning a microservice architecture".

  • Developer experience:
    • many services strain a laptop (four or five JVM services, not twenty). "Developing in the cloud" is disliked because feedback cycles suffer
    • better to limit which parts a developer works on (which conflicts with collective ownership).
  • Technology overload:
    • a danger of "technology fetishism". Heterogeneity is an option, not a requirement. Understand data consistency, latency and service modelling first
    • add technology as needed.
  • Cost: short-term costs rise (more processes, machines, network, storage, licences) and change slows you down while the team learns. Microservices suit growth, not cost cutting.
  • Reporting:
    • a monolith offers one schema to join over (maybe on a read replica)
    • microservices scatter data across schemas. Options: streaming for real-time reporting, or publishing data into central reporting databases or data lakes.
  • Monitoring and troubleshooting:
    • a monolith is mostly all up or all down
    • with hundreds of processes, is one stuck at 100% CPU worth waking someone at 3 a.m.? (Recommended: Cindy Sridharan, Distributed Systems Observability; chapter 10.)
  • Security: more data flows over networks, so protect data in transit and endpoints (chapter 11).
  • Testing:
    • broader tests give more confidence but are harder to set up and debug
    • end-to-end tests across many processes give diminishing returns and false negatives from environment issues. This drives contract-driven testing, testing in production and progressive delivery (parallel runs, canary releases) (chapters 8 and 9).
  • Latency: work once local is now serialised, sent and deserialised over networks. Migrate incrementally and measure (Jaeger can help); sometimes slower is fine if fast enough.
  • Data consistency:
    • database transactions no longer span services
    • distributed transactions are highly problematic. Use sagas (chapter 6) and eventual consistency: a big change in thinking, another reason to decompose cautiously.

1.7 Should I use microservices?

They are "an architectural approach, not the architectural approach"; context decides.

Whom they might not work for:

  • New products and startups.
    • Boundaries need a stable domain, and domains change fast while finding product-market fit (Uber began with limos; Flickr spun out of a multiplayer game attempt)
    • Coordinating across boundaries is expensive
    • Fewer people and the "microservice tax" (time spent deploying and managing them) hurt small teams; a five-person team losing one person to it is costly
    • Moving later is easier.
  • Software your customers deploy and manage. They will not take "just put these 20 pods on your Kubernetes cluster" well.

Where they work well:

  • More developers on one system without getting in each other's way. A five-person startup finds them a drag; a hundred-person scale-up properly aligned finds growth easier.
  • SaaS applications: 24-7 operation, independent releases, scaling services up or down for cost-effective load.
  • Cloud platforms: match each service to a suitable offering (functions, managed VM, PaaS); FaaS can cut operational overhead for suitable workloads.
  • New customer channels: unlocking functionality hidden in existing systems.
  • Above all, flexibility as the system evolves, at a cost.

Summary points

  • Microservices give flexibility in technology, robustness, scaling and team organisation, but bring significant complexity; justify them by the problem.
  • Many organisations, especially large ones, show how effective they are when the core concepts are understood.
  • Next: how to define boundaries (structured programming and domain-driven design).
Terms to carry forward
  • Independent deployability, information hiding, black box.
  • Conway's law, stream-aligned team, vertical slice.
  • Single-process, modular and distributed monolith; delivery contention.
  • Log aggregation, correlation ID, distributed tracing.
  • Bulkhead, microservice tax, saga, eventual consistency.
