Building Microservices 2: How to Model Microservices

Source: Sam Newman, Building Microservices, 2nd ed. (O'Reilly, 2021), ch. 2. Previous: Building Microservices 1: What Are Microservices?. Next: Building Microservices 3: Splitting the Monolith.

Takeaways
  • A good boundary maximises independent change and deployment. Microservices are just modular decomposition with a network between modules, so structured-programming prior art applies: information hiding, cohesion, coupling.
  • Cohesion: "the code that changes together, stays together." Coupling: a change to one service should not force a change to another. Constantine's law: a structure is stable if cohesion is strong and coupling is low.
  • Coupling types from loose to tight: domain, pass-through, common, content. Avoid content coupling; temporal coupling (both services up at once) is a related concern.
  • Domain-driven design gives the main tools: ubiquitous language, aggregates (one aggregate in one microservice), bounded contexts. Event storming surfaces the domain model collaboratively.
  • Domain and organisation are Newman's default boundary drivers; volatility, data and technology can also drive splits. Layered (horizontal) splits across teams are an anti-pattern.

2.1 Introducing MusicCorp

A fictional online retailer, formerly brick-and-mortar, used for examples throughout (composite; see Building Microservices 1: What Are Microservices?). Its bet: make changes as easily as possible, so "microservices for the win".

2.2 What makes a good microservice boundary?

  • Services must be changed, deployed and released independently; the ability to change one in isolation is vital.
  • Microservices are another form of modular decomposition, with network interaction and its challenges, so lean on prior art in modular software. Three key concepts: information hiding
    • cohesion
    • coupling.

Information hiding

  • David Parnas
    • developed it to find the best module boundaries (1971, "On the Criteria to Be Used in Decomposing Systems into Modules")
    • Hide as many details as possible behind the boundary
    • Benefits modules should give:
    • Improved development time: independent development allows parallel work and reduces the cost of adding developers.
    • Comprehensibility: each module can be understood in isolation, so the whole is easier to understand.
    • Flexibility: modules change independently and can be recombined for new functionality.
  • Having modules does not guarantee these outcomes; it depends on how boundaries are formed
    • Parnas: "The connections between modules are the assumptions which the modules make about each other." Fewer assumptions mean fewer connections, so a developer who knows how a module is used can change it without breaking callers
    • With microservices you can also deploy the change alone
    • (Adrian Colyer has summarised Parnas's papers in terms of microservices.)

Cohesion

  • "The code that changes together, stays together" (the book could not find the original source).
  • We optimise for ease of changing business functionality, so group related behaviour so a change lands in one place and ships as soon as possible
    • Scattered behaviour means many services released together, slower and riskier
    • Related functionality spread across the system means weak cohesion; we want strong cohesion.

Coupling

  • Loosely coupled services mean a change to one does not require a change to another
    • that is the whole point. A classic mistake is an integration style that tightly binds services, so internal changes force consumer changes.
  • A loosely coupled service knows as little as it needs about its collaborators
    • Limit the number of different types of calls: chatty communication can lead to tight coupling as well as performance problems.

The interplay

  • Related functionality spread out means changes ripple across boundaries (tighter coupling)
    • Constantine's law: "A structure is stable if cohesion is strong and coupling is low." (Newman earlier attributed this to Larry Constantine; the text now credits Albert Endres and Dieter Rombach, A Handbook of Software and Systems Engineering, 2003.)
  • Stability of boundaries matters: a contract that keeps changing in backward-incompatible ways forces consumers to change.
  • Cohesion
    • is the relationship between things inside a boundary; coupling is the relationship across a boundary
    • There is no absolute best organisation, only trade-offs to balance for your context
    • Parts of a system may change so much that stability is impossible (Snap CI example in chapter 3).

2.3 Types of coupling

Not all coupling is bad; some is unavoidable. The goal is to reduce it. The book synthesises a model from structured design (Yourdon and Constantine, Structured Design, 1976; Meilir Page-Jones, The Practical Guide to Structured Systems Design, 1980; both out of print).

Domain coupling

  • One microservice needs another's functionality. Example: Order Processor calls Warehouse to reserve stock and Payment to take payment; Warehouse and Payment do not interact.
  • Largely unavoidable and a loose form
    • But one service depending on many downstream services can mean too much logic centralised, and it becomes worse as more complex data flows
    • Share only what you must and send the minimum data.

Temporal coupling (a note)

  • In distributed systems it means one service needs another to do something at the same time for the operation to complete. A synchronous HTTP call from Order Processor to Warehouse needs Warehouse up at that moment
    • if not, the operation fails and the caller blocks, risking resource contention.
  • Not always bad, but it gets harder to scale as interactions multiply. One way to avoid it: asynchronous communication, such as a message broker.

Pass-through coupling

  • A service passes data on purely because a downstream service needs it (Page-Jones called it "tramp coupling")
    • Among the most problematic implementation couplings: the caller must know about the downstream service and how it works.
  • Example:
    • Order Processor sends a Shipping Manifest (address and shipping type) to Warehouse, which passes it to Shipping
    • If Shipping changes the format, Warehouse and Order Processor change.
  • Fixes:
    1. Bypass the intermediary: Order Processor calls Shipping directly. But it increases domain coupling, and now it must reserve stock with Warehouse, dispatch via Shipping, then update stock, pushing logic into Order Processor.
    2. Hide the manifest requirement: Warehouse takes the information in its own contract and builds the manifest locally. Shipping's contract changes become invisible to Order Processor.
    3. Treat it as an opaque blob: Order Processor still sends the manifest via Warehouse, but Warehouse does not look inside it. A format change touches Order Processor and Shipping only.
  • Even with option 2, adding a Customs Declaration for international shipping still needs all three to change if it is a required parameter
    • if optional, Shipping can deploy alone. You gain control over when and how changes roll out and avoid a lockstep rollout.

Common coupling

  • Two or more services use a common set of data: a shared database, shared memory or a shared filesystem. A change to the data's structure hits several services at once.
  • Benign case: multiple services reading static, read-only reference data (countries) from a shared database; Newman is relaxed about it.
  • Problem case:
    • Order Processor and Warehouse both read and write a shared Order table's Status column (Order Processor sets PLACED, PAID, COMPLETED; Warehouse sets PICKING, SHIPPED)
    • Can one change break the other's view?
  • A finite state machine forbids invalid transitions (PLACED to PAID allowed; PLACED straight to PICKING not)
    • But if two services share the responsibility, how do they agree what is allowed? (Sagas, chapter 6.)
  • Solution:
    • let one microservice manage the order state. The Order service is the source of truth
    • Warehouse and Order Processor send requests, which the Order service may reject (for example PLACED to SHIPPED).
Treat a request to a microservice as something the downstream service can reject if it is invalid.

  • Anti-pattern: an Order service that is only a thin wrapper over database CRUD (like an object with private fields and public getters and setters): behaviour has leaked to consumers, so cohesion is weak and you are back to coordinating state transitions across services.
  • Shared resources also cause resource contention:
    • a shared database can be overloaded by one expensive query
    • So common coupling is sometimes fine, but often not, and speaks to weak cohesion.

Content coupling

  • An upstream service reaches into the internals of a downstream service and changes its state, most commonly writing directly to another service's database
    • Unlike common coupling (you know it is a shared, external dependency), ownership becomes unclear.
  • Example:
    • Order Processor delegates state change and the valid-transition decision to the Order service, but Warehouse updates the order table directly, bypassing its checks
    • At best duplicated logic, at worst inconsistent rules and orders in odd states
    • The table's internal structure is exposed, so changing the Order service is risky.
  • The database effectively becomes part of the external contract with no way to say what is shared and what is hidden: "information hiding has gone out of the window"
    • Some call it pathological coupling.
  • Fix: Warehouse sends requests to the Order service. Avoid content coupling.

2.4 Just enough domain-driven design

The main mechanism for finding boundaries is the domain itself, using domain-driven design (DDD) (Eric Evans, Domain-Driven Design, 2004). Three concepts matter here:

Concept Definition
Ubiquitous language A common language, used in code and in describing the domain, to aid communication
Aggregate A collection of objects managed as a single entity, typically a real-world concept
Bounded context An explicit boundary within a business domain that provides functionality to the wider system but hides complexity

Ubiquitous language

Use the same terms in code as the users do.

  • Counter-example (a large bank, corporate liquidity):
    • the product owner talked about "haircuts" and "end-of-day sweeps", but the code used a generic "IBM banking model" where a loan, a share purchase and a credit card were all an "arrangement"
    • The code was shorn of domain understanding, analysts kept re-explaining, and work was spent translating
    • Working the real-world language into the code made stories easier to understand and implement.

Aggregate

  • Think of an aggregate as a representation of a real domain concept (Order, Invoice, Stock Item), with state, identity and a life cycle that can be implemented as a state machine
    • An Order aggregate may contain line items that only have meaning inside it.
  • Treat aggregates as self-contained units:
    • state-transition code lives with the state
    • One aggregate is managed by one microservice, though a microservice may manage several aggregates.
  • Other services either request a change or react to events from the aggregate's owner. The aggregate can say no; ideally illegal transitions are impossible.
  • Aggregates relate to each other (Customer to Orders and Wishlists)
    • Within one service this can be a foreign key; across services you need another way
    • Storing a raw ID (a Finance ledger with a CustID column) leaves the relationship implicit, visible only in the code.
  • Better: store a URI (explicit and, in REST, dereferenceable), or a pseudo-URI scheme such as SoundCloud's soundcloud:tracks:123 (Phil Calçado, "Pattern: Using Pseudo-URIs with Microservices"), which is human-readable and can support lookup code.
  • Aggregate design is subjective; start from the mental model of the system's users, and treat implementation as secondary.

Bounded context

  • Typically a larger organisational boundary with explicit responsibilities
    • It hides implementation detail (the types of forklift truck in the warehouse are nobody else's concern) and contains one or more aggregates, some exposed outside, some hidden
    • Context relationships become inter-service dependencies when mapped to services.
  • MusicCorp's warehouse and finance departments are two bounded contexts, each with an explicit interface (inventory reports, pay slips) and private detail (forklifts, calculators).
  • Hidden models:
    • the warehouse's picker and shelves are internal
    • finance's general ledger entries are internal
      • Shared models: finance needs stock information to value the company, so Stock Item appears in both
      • The warehouse's internal Stock Item holds shelf locations
    • the shared representation holds only a count, and it may be worth naming it differently (Stock Count; finance may call it an asset).
  • The same real-world thing can mean different things in different contexts, and each stores different information (finance: value; warehouse: location)
    • You may still link both to a global concept of the item and look up shared data (name, supplier) with a reference.

Mapping aggregates and bounded contexts to microservices

  • Both are units of cohesion with well-defined interfaces, so both work as service boundaries. Start with services covering whole bounded contexts (fewer services)
    • later split further, remembering an aggregate should not be split across microservices.
  • Turtles all the way down:
    • bounded contexts nest
    • Warehouse could decompose into order fulfilment, inventory management and goods receiving
    • Think coarse first, then subdivide along nested contexts when you need the benefit.
  • You can hide a later split:
    • Warehouse split into Inventory (Stock Items) and Shipping (Shipments) can still present one coarse-grained Warehouse API
    • It is an implementation decision, another form of information hiding.
  • Nesting also simplifies testing: consumers stub the coarse API only, and you can run all warehouse-context services together in a larger-scoped test while stubbing other collaborators (chapter 9).

Event storming

Developed by Alberto Brandolini: a collaborative exercise to surface a domain model, bringing technical and non-technical stakeholders together instead of an architect modelling alone, so the model is shared and joined-up. The result can drive event-driven or request-response systems.

Logistics:

  • Everyone in one room: users, subject matter experts, product owners (hard to schedule, especially in a pandemic).
  • Brandolini suggests removing chairs; Newman (bad back) agrees on big space and notes it may not suit everyone. Pin large rolls of brown paper on walls.
  • Sticky notes in different colours for different concepts.

Process:

  1. Domain events (orange): facts, such as "Order Placed" or "Payment Received". Newman's joke: events are the most numerous and orange notes are hard to get.
  2. Commands (blue): human decisions that cause events; reveals the system boundary and key human actors. Do not let the current implementation warp the picture.
  3. Aggregates (yellow): the noun in an event ("Order") suggests an aggregate; "Placed" part of its life cycle. Cluster commands and events around it; see how aggregates relate.
  4. Bounded contexts: group aggregates, which most commonly follow the organisation's structure.
  • Further reading: Brandolini's EventStorming (Leanpub, in progress at publication).

2.5 The case for DDD for microservices

  1. Bounded contexts are explicitly about hiding information, so following DDD gives you information hiding (vital for stable boundaries) whether you notice or not.
  2. A ubiquitous language gives a shared vocabulary for APIs and event formats and helps decide how far to standardise language within contexts.
  3. Changes are usually changes to business capabilities; decomposing along bounded contexts isolates changes to one service and lets you deploy quickly.
  4. DDD puts the business domain at the heart of the software, building domain expertise, empathy for users and communication; it fits stream-aligned teams and helps tear down silos between IT and the business.

2.6 Alternatives to business-domain boundaries

Newman often uses several methods with DDD.

Volatility

  • Volatility-based decomposition
    • extracts the frequently changing parts into their own services
    • Sound when the driver is fast time to market, but not as the only way: if the problem is scaling, it will not help much.
  • The same thinking appears in Gartner's bimodal IT ("Mode 1" systems of record, slow; "Mode 2" systems of innovation, fast)
    • Newman dislikes it: oversimplified, fixed view of the world (MusicCorp went from a web page listing stock, ordering by phone, to online ordering requiring a digitised warehouse), a place to dump hard-to-change things, and changes in innovation systems often need changes in systems of record
    • "Organizations adopting bimodal IT do end up having two speeds—slow and slower."

Data

  • Data can drive decomposition, for example limiting which services handle PII to reduce breach risk and simplify oversight and GDPR.
  • PaymentCo
    • (a real payment company, name changed) handles full credit card data at a volume needing PCI Level 1 (quarterly external assessment)
    • To limit audit scope it split the system into a green zone (never sees card data, exempt from a full PCI audit) and a red zone (handles card data, in scope), with a gateway diverting calls
    • Card data must never flow into the green zone, in either direction, or the separation breaks (chapter 11).

Technology

  • A need for a different runtime model (for example Rust for performance) forces a boundary
    • different databases can coexist in one service, different runtimes cannot. As a general decomposition driver it yields the classic three-tier architecture, often a poor outcome.

Organisational

  • Conway's law
    • again: how you organise drives your architecture
    • A boundary whose ownership cuts across teams rarely works well (shared ownership is fraught, chapter 15)
    • Consider existing structure, and perhaps change it to support the architecture you want.
  • If the organisation changes, a service may need splitting if two teams now own parts of it
    • often only the owner changes. Example: moving supplier ordering from warehousing to a dedicated forecasting team is easy if a Supplier Ordering microservice exists, harder if it was embedded in a larger system.
  • Anti-pattern story (California client):
    • a public global app was split for a new team in Brazil as a front half (stateless website) and a back half (an RPC interface over a data store): in effect a repository layer made into a service
    • Both changed together, calls were low-level, brittle and chatty, needing RPC batching: "onion architecture" that "made me cry"
    • A better split: each team owns a vertical slice (related front end plus data access).

Layering inside versus outside

Newman is not a fan of horizontally layered architecture, but layering is fine inside a microservice. The problem is when layers become the mechanism for drawing service and ownership boundaries.

Mixing models and exceptions

  • Do not be dogmatic: follow information hiding and the coupling/cohesion interplay and you avoid the worst pitfalls of any mechanism (and likely end up domain-oriented).
  • Mechanisms interact:
    • volatility-based extraction that crosses organisational boundaries brings delivery contention
    • a domain-based Warehouse may still need splitting if part must be C++ and part Kotlin.
  • Newman's own default:
    • organisational and domain-driven boundaries
    • Which factors dominate depends on your problem
    • "If someone says 'The only way to do this is X!' they are likely just selling you more dogma."

Summary points

  • A good boundary gives low coupling and strong cohesion; understanding the domain helps find those seams, and aligning services to bounded contexts preserves them.
