Building Microservices 16: The Evolutionary Architect

Source: Sam Newman, Building Microservices, 2nd ed. (O'Reilly, 2021), ch. 16. Previous: Building Microservices 15: Organizational Structures. Next: Building Microservices 17: Afterword, Bringing It All Together.

Takeaways
  • Microservices bring many decisions, so the architect's role must change: not an ivory-tower plan-drawer but a town planner who sets zones and focuses on what happens between them.
  • Architecture is a social construct and "what happens, not what is planned"; architects must work with teams, make systems habitable, and spend routine time coding with them.
  • Guide with strategic goals, principles and practices (and fitness functions), a "good citizen" microservice standard, a paved road (exemplars, templates, platform), and light governance run as a group.
  • Manage technical debt and exceptions consciously. Core responsibilities: vision, empathy, collaboration, adaptability, autonomy, governance.

16.1 What's in a name?

  • Architects are responsible for a joined-up technical vision. For one team the role is often the technical lead
    • elsewhere it spans programmes or organisations. It is criticised more than most roles, and poorly understood.
  • The industry is young (about 75 years of computers)
    • We borrow "engineer" (the term software engineering, per Newman, was coined by Margaret Hamilton in the 1960s, as an aspiration) and "architect", but without the rigour, qualifications or thousands of years of knowledge (UK architects need at least seven years' study; a friend became accountable for advice the day he qualified)
    • He thus finds many IT certifications worthless, since we know little about what "good" looks like.
  • The harm:
    • the image of an architect drawing detailed plans for others to follow, ignoring an unknowable future and implementation difficulty
    • But change is far cheaper in software ("you can't unpour concrete, but you can change code").

16.2 What is software architecture?

  • Ralph Johnson's
    • email (extreme programming list, shared by Martin Fowler, "Who Needs an Architect?"): "Architecture is about the important stuff. Whatever that is." In context, he continued: architecture is the shared understanding of expert developers of how the system is divided into components and how they interact through interfaces
    • it is a social construct (group consensus about what is important).
  • Newman:
    • architecture is the shape of the system
    • it happens by design or accident. A dedicated architect sees the whole system and the forces on it (logical and physical, shape and developer experience).
  • Fowler's other line: "things that people perceive as hard to change". True, but architecture is also about creating space to allow change.

Making change possible

  • Mies van der Rohe's Seagram Building:
    • non-structural outer walls around a steel frame
    • services (lifts, stairs, air conditioning, water, electrics) in a central concrete core built first
    • no interior structural walls, giving "universal space" reconfigurable for different uses. The design also evolved during construction.

An evolutionary vision: the town planner

  • Erik Doernenburg (Thoughtworks) suggested thinking of the architect as a town planner (as in SimCity): define zones with local decision rights under constraints, and spend more time on how people and utilities move between zones
    • Direct control of everything is futile; set direction in broad strokes and get specific only in limited cases
    • Plan to allow change rather than over-specify.

16.3 Defining system boundaries

  • Zones are microservice boundaries or coarse groups of them
    • Worry less inside a zone and more between: how services talk and how overall health is monitored
    • This creates "universal space".
  • Example:
    • Recommendations uses Promotions and Sales
    • you may change the inside of any as long as the expected interactions hold. A marketing zone owned by one team can change freely as long as it is compatible with the wider system
    • existing team structures help define zones (organisation is hard to change). This ties to the team API (chapter 15): information hiding at team level.
  • Within a zone a team may pick a different stack or data store, though hiring and moving people suffers with ten stacks, and many data stores strain operating expertise: Netflix mostly standardised on Cassandra despite imperfect fits, valuing tooling and expertise
    • Between services, mixed REST, gRPC and Java RMI make integration a nightmare: "be worried about what happens between the boxes, and be liberal in what happens inside."

16.4 A social construct

  • "No plan survives contact with the enemy" (von Moltke, paraphrased)
    • Architecture is what happens, not what is planned. An architect who is not embedded with the builders cannot communicate the vision or see where it fails: "you're not an architect, you're a dreamer".
  • Grady Booch (2020):
    • the architecture begins as vision and ends as "a reflection of the billions upon billions of small and large, intentional and accidental design decisions made along the way"
    • So it is a team effort.
  • Comcast's Architecture Guild (Jon Moore, InfoQ 2019) modelled on the IETF: autonomous groups agree on technical approaches; formal, but suits its size.

Habitability

  • From Frank Buschmann
    • Richard Gabriel (Patterns of Software, 1996): habitability is the quality of code that lets later programmers "understand its construction and intentions and to change it comfortably and confidently"
    • It extends to the technologies and working practices
    • Developers curse tools chosen by people who never use them, so make decisions collaboratively.
  • So architects must spend time with the teams, ideally coding (pairing, perhaps ensembles, mindful of how their presence changes the dynamic), on normal tasks
    • Make it routine: for four teams, half a day with each every four weeks.

16.5 A principled approach

Decisions are trade-offs; framing helps via goals, principles and practices.

Level Description Example
Strategic goals Where the company is going; often not technical "Expand into Southeast Asia"; "Let the customer achieve as much as possible using self-service"
Principles Rules aligning actions to goals; fewer than 10 (to remember, avoid overlap and contradiction) Teams control the full life cycle and ship independently; the system must be portable for data sovereignty
Practices How principles are carried out; detailed, technology specific, change more often All log data centralised; HTTP/REST is the standard; deploy each microservice into isolated AWS accounts
  • Heroku's Twelve Factors mixes principles and constraints (very hard to change)
    • listing them together invites challenging constraints. One person's principle is another's practice
    • larger organisations may have different practices (a .NET team, a Java team) under common principles
      • Evan Bottcher's one-page diagram of goals, principles and practices (practices change; principles stay)
      • Back practices with documentation and working code
    • the platform makes them easy.

Guiding an evolutionary architecture: fitness functions

  • From Building Evolutionary Architectures (Ford, Parsons, Kua, 2017): a fitness function measures whether an important property stays within bounds, so changes are investigated
    • Example: a service must respond in 100 ms or less, measured in a performance environment or in production
    • Others: coupling, cycle time
    • They work best with close collaboration and do not replace speaking to people.

Architecture in a stream-aligned organisation

  • A complex stream-aligned team may have its own architect (blurred with the tech lead)
    • often architects span teams. Most duties are enabling: communicate vision, understand emerging challenges, adapt the vision, connect people.
  • Newman's model:
    • an architecture enabling team of one or two dedicated architects plus technical leads from each delivery team, giving buy-in and information flow
    • The architect is responsible for the group working.
  • When the group disagrees with you:
    • usually go with the group (it is often wiser, and overriding disempowers). Like teaching a child to ride a bike: let them wobble, but step in when they are about to veer into traffic or a duck pond
    • overruling costs standing.
  • Divergence:
    • if a team throws new data into a shared database for speed while the group is moving away from central data, talk to the product owner
    • it may be conscious technical debt, or plans may change
    • escalate in the worst case. At REA, product owners were made accountable for technical aspects too (security, performance), which was non-trivial but worthwhile.

Building a team

  • Technical leaders must help people grow
    • Microservices create ownership opportunities (own one service before more responsibility), which also lightens the architect's load
    • "Great software comes from great people."

16.6 The required standard: a good citizen microservice

Decide how much variability to allow. Ben Christensen (Facebook): "a cohesive system made of many small parts with autonomous life cycles but all coming together."

Attribute Why
Monitoring Coherent cross-service health views; emit health metrics and logs the same way, centrally; keep the technology inside opaque
Interfaces One standard is good, two not bad, twenty not good; also REST conventions (verbs or nouns, pagination, versioning)
Architectural safety Shield from bad downstream calls (mandate circuit breakers); play by the rules on response codes (no 2XX for errors, do not confuse 4XX and 5XX) or safety measures fail

16.7 Governance and the paved road

  • COBIT's definition:
    • governance "ensures that enterprise objectives are achieved by evaluating stakeholder needs, conditions and options
    • setting direction through prioritization and decision making
    • and monitoring performance, compliance and progress"
      • In short: agree how things should be done, make sure people know, make sure it happens
      • Technical governance is the architect's job: check what is built matches the vision, and evolve the vision.
  • It should be a group activity with mostly people doing the work, tracking technical risks and revisiting principles
    • Make it easy to do the right thing (paved road): the architect shapes the platform requirements and is a key stakeholder for the platform team.
  • Exemplars: real running microservices that get things right (not artificial "perfect" ones), so people can imitate them; usage keeps the principles honest.
  • Tailored microservice template:
    • a framework (Spring Boot for the JVM) standardised with the organisation's libraries (circuit breakers, JWT authentication) and a build pipeline
    • usually a platform team task per supported language.
    • Caution:
      • mandated frameworks can destroy morale and become monsters
      • ideally optional, easy to use, and open to contributions (internal open source)
        • Beware coupling from shared code (chapter 5): one organisation copies the template into each service
      • others treat it as a shared binary dependency, with care about DRY.
    • At scale:
      • Netflix (JVM) and Monzo (Go) standardise stacks to speed up new services
      • More stacks mean a template for each, subtly constraining language choice
      • Netflix uses sidecar services to run its JVM libraries for fault tolerance
      • Service meshes can take on common behaviour, easing the templates.

Technical debt

  • Cutting corners to ship has a long-term cost, like financial debt, and a changed vision creates debt too
    • The architect keeps a view of the level and where to act: gentle guidance and team tracking, or a regularly reviewed debt log in more structured organisations.

Exception handling

  • Log deviations
    • enough exceptions may justify changing the rule (from "always MySQL" to "MySQL, unless large growth in volume, then Cassandra")
      • Where teams have high trust and autonomy, principles are light and exceptions rarely tracked
      • Newman values microservices for autonomy
    • if your organisation heavily restricts developers, microservices may not suit.

Summary points: core responsibilities

Responsibility Meaning
Vision A clear, communicated technical vision for customers and organisation
Empathy Understand the impact of decisions on customers and colleagues
Collaboration Engage peers to define, refine and execute the vision
Adaptability Change the vision as needed
Autonomy Balance standardisation and team autonomy
Governance Ensure the system fits the vision and make the right thing easy
  • It is "a constant balancing act"; the worst response to forces for change is to become rigid.
  • Reading: Ford, Parsons and Kua, Building Evolutionary Architectures; Gregor Hohpe, The Software Architect Elevator (O'Reilly, 2020).
Terms to carry forward
  • Software architecture as shared understanding, town planner, zones, habitability.
  • Strategic goals, principles, practices, constraints, fitness function.
  • Good citizen microservice, exemplar, tailored template, paved road.
  • Governance, technical debt, exception log.
