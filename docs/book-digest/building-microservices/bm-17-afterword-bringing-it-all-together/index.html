Building Microservices 17: Afterword, Bringing It All Together

Source: Sam Newman, Building Microservices, 2nd ed. (O'Reilly, 2021), Afterword. Previous: Building Microservices 16: The Evolutionary Architect. Next: Building Microservices: contents.

Takeaways
  • Microservices are a service-oriented style built on independent deployability, enabled by information hiding (including a hidden database) and aligned to the business domain with DDD.
  • Start from a single-process monolith; move incrementally, with a clear goal, never a big-bang rewrite.
  • Choose per topic: events and requests, sagas, one build per service, isolated deployment, fewer end-to-end tests, observability, zero trust, stability patterns, easy scaling axes first, micro frontends and BFFs, stream-aligned teams, and collaborative architecture.
  • "Embrace change": keep decisions small and evolve the system continually.

17.1 Summary of advice by chapter

Topic Advice
What are microservices? (ch. 1 and 2) A type of SOA focused on independent deployability: change, deploy and release one service without others changing. Needs stable, backward-compatible interfaces and information hiding (expose the bare minimum). Hide the database and avoid several services sharing one. They fit DDD, giving business-aligned boundaries and teams
Moving to microservices (ch. 3) They add complexity, so justify them. A simple monolith is a sensible start. Know your goal (scale or autonomy changes what you split). Many problems show only in production, so prefer incremental, evolutionary decomposition: extract one service, deploy and learn. Detail in his Monolith to Microservices
Communication (ch. 4 and 5) Request-response (sync: response to the same instance; async: possibly a different instance) or event-driven (statements of fact; always asynchronous). A service can use both (a Shipping service with a REST API and a topic). Events are looser but harder to follow and need brokers (prefer managed). Mix is normal
Workflow (ch. 6) Model processes with sagas, not distributed transactions. Orchestrated (simple, central, contention across teams) for one team; choreographed (more complex, less coupled) across several. Newman loves choreography but notes his many mistakes
Build (ch. 7) Own build and CI pipeline per microservice, triggerable independently, whether mono- or multirepo (he dislikes monorepos: ownership and build complexity)
Deployment (ch. 8) Isolate instances with their own ring-fenced resources; containers are effective. Kubernetes for many services across machines (managed if possible; not for a few services). FaaS is a likely future (a whole microservice as one function is fine). Separate deployment from release (canary releases, parallel runs)
Testing (ch. 9) Automate; end-to-end tests are costly and flaky, worse across teams. Reduce them in favour of consumer-driven contracts, schema compatibility checks and testing in production
Monitoring and observability (ch. 10) Monitoring is an activity; observability (understand from external outputs) is the outcome. Use tools like Lightstep and Honeycomb to ask unexpected questions. With scale there is always an error somewhere: use parallel runs and synthetic transactions to find user-affecting problems, not 3 a.m. alerts for one bad machine
Security (ch. 11) More defence in depth but a larger attack surface; protect data in transit; automate patching, certificates and secrets; JWTs decentralise authorisation (guarding against the confused deputy); adopt a zero-trust mindset
Resiliency (ch. 12) Robustness, rebound, graceful extensibility, sustained adaptability. Microservices help robustness and rebound; resilience is largely team and organisational culture. Robustness is a choice: bulkheads, circuit breakers, timeouts
Scaling (ch. 13) Four axes (vertical, horizontal duplication, data partitioning, functional decomposition): do the easy stuff first, and mix (partition by customer, then scale each horizontally)
User interfaces (ch. 14) Do not leave a monolithic UI. Use micro frontends (for example with React) for stream-aligned teams; a BFF for server-side aggregation and filtering (or GraphQL)
Organisation (ch. 15) Stream-aligned teams with enabling teams, self-service, and a platform as a paved road: optional, so it stays easy to use
Architecture (ch. 16) Architecture is continually changing; an ivory-tower architect either blocks the architecture or is ignored. Whether called principal engineer, technical product owner or architect, the role is to support teams, connect people, spot patterns and spend time embedded

17.2 Further reading

  • The two most influential books on this edition: Accelerate (Forsgren, Humble, Kim) and Team Topologies (Skelton and Pais): "the two most useful books on software development written in the last ten years", essential "whether you are into microservices or not".
  • Companion: Newman's Monolith to Microservices for breaking apart existing systems.

17.3 Looking forward

  • Newman expects technology to keep easing microservices
    • keen on second- and third-generation FaaS
    • Kubernetes more widespread yet hidden behind developer-friendly layers ("Kubernetes has won, but in a way that most application developers shouldn't have to worry about")
    • interested in Wasm, and suspects unikernels may return.
  • Microservices have "gone mainstream", partly because others do it: he expects horror stories and an eventual industry backlash (opinion, no source)
    • Critical thinking "isn't very sexy or marketable", and selling technology pays more than selling ideas.

17.4 Final words

  • More options mean more decisions, and some will be wrong:
    • make each decision small in scope, adopt evolutionary architecture (a system that bends and flexes), and avoid big-bang rewrites in favour of a series of changes
    • Think "a journey, not a destination": go incrementally, piece by piece
    • "Change is inevitable
    • Embrace it."

Terms to carry forward
  • Independent deployability, information hiding, DDD.
  • Incremental decomposition, evolutionary architecture.
  • Stream-aligned team, paved road, observability.
