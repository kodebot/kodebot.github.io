Clean Architecture 1: What Is Design and Architecture?

Source: Robert C. Martin, Clean Architecture: A Craftsman's Guide to Software Structure and Design (Pearson, 2017), chapter 1. Own-words digest with short attributed quotes. Next: Clean Architecture 2: A Tale of Two Values.

Takeaways
  • Design and architecture are the same thing. There is no clear line between the high-level structure and the low-level details: they are one continuum of decisions.
  • The goal of software architecture is to minimise the human resources required to build and maintain the required system. Design quality is measured by the effort needed to meet customer needs.
  • A mess has a signature: more developers, flat output, soaring cost per line. The only way to go fast is to go well; "clean it up later" never happens, and a rewrite repeats the mess.

1.1 Design and architecture are one

  • Many people use "architecture" for the high level, divorced from detail, and "design" for lower-level structure and decisions
    • Martin says that usage is nonsensical and asserts "there is no difference between them
    • None at all".
  • Example:
    • the architect of Martin's new home
    • The architecture is the shape, outward appearance, elevations and layout of rooms
    • The same plans also show where every outlet, light switch and light goes, which switches control which lights, the furnace, water heater and sump pump, and how walls, roofs and foundations are built
    • Those low-level details support the high-level decisions and are part of the whole design.
  • Software works the same way:
    • low-level details and high-level structure form a continuous fabric that defines the system's shape
    • you cannot have one without the other
    • there is a continuum of decisions from the highest to the lowest levels.

1.2 The goal

  • The goal of software design is Martin's "utopian description": "The goal of software architecture is to minimize the human resources required to build and maintain the required system."
  • The measure of design quality is the effort required to meet the customer's needs
    • If that effort is low, and stays low throughout the lifetime of the system, the design is good
    • If it grows with each release, the design is bad.

1.3 Case study: the signature of a mess

  • Real data from a real company that wishes to remain anonymous (figures reproduced in the book with permission from a slide presentation by Jason Gorman):
    • Engineering staff grows steadily (Figure 1.1): apparently a sign of success.
    • Productivity, measured by lines of code, approaches an asymptote (Figure 1.2) even though every release has ever more developers.
    • Cost per line of code rises sharply (Figure 1.3): code was 40 times more expensive to produce in release 8 than release 1.
  • This is "the signature of a mess":
    • systems thrown together in a hurry, headcount as the only driver of output, little thought for code cleanliness or design structure
    • From the developers' side (Figure 1.4): they start near 100% productivity and it declines each release, approaching zero by about the fourth release
    • Everyone is working hard and nobody has reduced effort, but all effort now goes to managing the mess
      • their job becomes moving the mess from place to place to add one more meagre feature.
  • The executive view (Figure 1.5): monthly development payroll was a few hundred thousand dollars for release 1 and a few hundred thousand more for release 2, and $20 million by release 8 and climbing
    • Compared with lines of code per release (Figure 1.2), the initial few hundred thousand bought a lot of functionality while the final $20 million bought almost nothing
    • Any CFO would see immediate action is needed
    • These trends are not sustainable and can drive a profitable company into a stall or collapse.

1.4 What went wrong: the hare's overconfidence

  • Martin retells Aesop's Tortoise and the Hare (nearly 2,600 years old) with morals such as "Slow and steady wins the race", "The race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong" and "The more haste, the less speed"
    • Modern developers do not sleep but a part of their brain does: the part that knows clean, well-designed code matters.
  • They buy a familiar lie:
    • "We can clean it up later; we just have to get to market first!" Things never get cleaned up, because market pressure never abates: reaching the market first just means a horde of competitors on your tail
    • Developers never switch modes, the mess builds and productivity approaches zero asymptotically
    • The creeping mess "never sleeps and never relents" and, left alone, can reduce productivity to zero in a matter of months.
  • The bigger lie:
    • that messy code makes you fast in the short term and only slows you in the long term
    • It also assumes you can switch from making messes to cleaning them up later
    • The fact: making messes is always slower than staying clean, on any time scale.
  • Evidence (Figure 1.6): Jason Gorman's experiment.
    • Over six days he finished a simple program converting integers to Roman numerals each day, complete when predefined acceptance tests passed
    • each day took a little under 30 minutes
      • He used test-driven development (TDD) on days 1, 3 and 5 and not on the other three
      • Findings: a learning curve (later days faster than earlier ones)
    • TDD days about 10% faster than non-TDD days
    • and even the slowest TDD day was faster than the fastest non-TDD day.
  • The simple truth: "The only way to go fast, is to go well."
  • This is the answer to the executive's dilemma:
    • get developers to stop thinking like the overconfident hare and take responsibility for the mess they made
    • Starting over from scratch with a redesign is "just the Hare talking again": the same overconfidence that caused the mess will drive the redesign into the same mess as the original.

1.5 Conclusion

  • The best option is for the development organisation to recognise and avoid its own overconfidence and take the quality of its software architecture seriously
    • That requires knowing what good software architecture is and which attributes of system architecture minimise effort and maximise productivity: the subject of the book, to help developers build systems with long profitable lifetimes.

Summary points

  • Architecture and design are one continuum; the goal is less human effort to build and maintain.
  • The signature of a mess (headcount up, output flat, cost per line up 40 times in the case study) comes from overconfidence and "clean it up later".
  • Going well is going fast; rewriting from scratch does not escape the cause.
Terms to carry forward
  • Architecture = design (a continuum).
  • Goal: minimise human resources to build and maintain.
  • Signature of a mess; TDD; "the only way to go fast is to go well".
