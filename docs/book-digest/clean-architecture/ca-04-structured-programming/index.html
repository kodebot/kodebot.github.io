Clean Architecture 4: Structured Programming

Source: Robert C. Martin, Clean Architecture: A Craftsman's Guide to Software Structure and Design (Pearson, 2017), chapter 4. Own-words digest with short attributed quotes. Previous: Clean Architecture 3: Paradigm Overview. Next: Clean Architecture 5: Object-Oriented Programming.

Takeaways
  • Edsger Dijkstra wanted to prove programs correct like theorems. Along the way he found that unrestrained goto blocks recursive decomposition, while sequence, selection and iteration (the minimal set from which all programs can be built, per Böhm and Jacopini) allow it: structured programming.
  • Formal proofs of whole programs never materialised, but science offers another route: statements are falsifiable, not provable. "Testing shows the presence, not the absence, of bugs."
  • Structured programming's lasting value is falsifiable units: small provable functions that tests try to show wrong. Architects apply the same idea to modules, components and services (testability).

4.1 Dijkstra and the problem of proof

  • Background:
    • Dijkstra was born in Rotterdam in 1930 and survived the bombing and German occupation; in 1948 he finished school with top marks in maths, physics, chemistry and biology
    • In March 1952, at 21, he became the Netherlands' first programmer, at the Mathematical Center of Amsterdam
    • By 1955 he judged programming's intellectual challenge greater than theoretical physics's and chose it as a career
    • When he married in 1957 the Dutch authorities would not accept "programmer" as a profession, so he gave "theoretical physicist"
    • His boss, Adriaan van Wijngaarden, said he might be one of the people to discover the disciplines that would turn software into a science
    • He began in the era of vacuum tubes: huge, fragile, slow, unreliable machines, programs in binary or crude assembly, paper tape or punched cards, and an edit/compile/test loop of hours if not days.
  • The problem:
    • programming is hard and programmers do it badly
    • a program of any complexity has too many details for one brain, and overlooking one detail gives a program that seems to work but fails in surprising ways.
  • His solution: mathematical proof.
    • A Euclidean hierarchy of postulates, theorems, corollaries and lemmas
    • programmers would use proven structures and tie them together with code they would prove correct themselves. First he had to show how to write basic proofs of simple algorithms, which was challenging.
  • The discovery about goto:
    • certain uses prevent modules being decomposed recursively into smaller units, blocking the divide-and-conquer approach proofs need
    • Other uses did not: those "good" uses correspond to selection (if/then/else) and iteration (do/while)
    • Modules using only those can be recursively subdivided into provable units.
  • These structures plus sequential execution were special:
    • two years earlier Böhm and Jacopini had proved that all programs can be constructed from sequence, selection and iteration
    • The control structures that make a module provable are the same minimum set from which all programs are built: structured programming was born.
  • How he proved each:
    • Sequence: by enumeration, tracing the sequence's inputs to its outputs like any normal mathematical proof.
    • Selection: reapply enumeration to each path; if all paths give appropriate results the proof is solid.
    • Iteration:
      • induction: prove the case for 1 by enumeration
      • prove that if N is correct then N + 1 is correct, by enumeration
      • prove the starting and ending criteria by enumeration.
  • Such proofs were laborious and complex but proofs, making a Euclidean hierarchy of theorems seem reachable.

4.2 A harmful proclamation

  • In 1968 Dijkstra wrote a letter to the editor of CACM, published in the March issue, titled "Go To Statement Considered Harmful", outlining his position on the three control structures
    • "The programming world caught fire": with no Internet, people wrote letters to editors of many journals, some intensely negative, others strongly supportive
    • The battle lasted about a decade, then petered out because Dijkstra had won: as languages evolved the goto statement moved ever rearward until it all but disappeared (LISP never had one).
  • "Nowadays we are all structured programmers, though not necessarily by choice": our languages do not give us undisciplined direct transfer of control. Named breaks in Java and exceptions are not utterly unrestricted jumps like Fortran or COBOL
    • even languages that keep goto often restrict the target to within the current function.

4.3 Functional decomposition

  • Structured programming lets modules be recursively decomposed into provable units, so large problems can be functionally decomposed into high-level functions, each into lower-level ones, "ad infinitum", each expressed with the restricted control structures.
  • On that base structured analysis and structured design became popular in the late 1970s and through the 1980s, promoted by Ed Yourdon, Larry Constantine, Tom DeMarco and Meilir Page-Jones: break large systems into modules and components and then into tiny provable functions.

4.4 No formal proofs

  • But the proofs never came
    • The Euclidean hierarchy of theorems was never built, and programmers at large never saw the benefit of laboriously proving every little function correct
    • Dijkstra's dream "faded and died"; few programmers believe formal proofs are the way to high-quality software
    • Formal Euclidean proofs are not the only strategy, though: another successful one is the scientific method.

4.5 Science to the rescue

  • Science differs from mathematics:
    • scientific theories and laws cannot be proven correct
    • Martin cannot prove Newton's second law F = ma or the law of gravity F = Gm1m2/r²
      • he can demonstrate them and measure them correct to many decimal places, but no amount of experiment rules out some experiment showing them wrong
    • They are falsifiable but not provable.
  • Yet "we bet our lives on these laws every day":
    • getting into a car bets on F = ma
    • every step bets on the gravity law. Science works by proving statements false, not true
    • those we cannot prove false after much effort are deemed true enough for our purposes.
  • Not all statements are provable:
    • "This is a lie" is neither true nor false. In summary: **mathematics is the discipline of proving provable statements true
    • science is the discipline of proving provable statements false.**

4.6 Tests

  • Dijkstra:
    • "Testing shows the presence, not the absence, of bugs." A test can prove a program incorrect but never correct
    • after sufficient testing effort we can only deem a program correct enough for our purposes.
  • The implication:
    • software development is not a mathematical endeavour, even though it manipulates mathematical constructs
    • software is like a science: we show correctness by failing to prove incorrectness despite our best efforts.
  • Such disproofs only apply to provable programs:
    • one that is not provable (for example through unrestrained goto) cannot be deemed correct however many tests are applied. Structured programming forces us to recursively decompose a program into small provable functions
    • tests then try to prove those functions incorrect, and if they fail we deem them correct enough.

4.7 Conclusion

  • The value of structured programming today is the ability to create falsifiable units of programming: why modern languages typically lack unrestrained goto, and why, at the architectural level, functional decomposition remains one of our best practices.
  • At every level, from the smallest function to the largest component, software is like a science and is driven by falsifiability
    • Architects strive to define modules, components and services that are easily falsifiable (testable), using restrictive disciplines similar to structured programming at a much higher level: the subject of the chapters ahead.

Summary points

  • Structured programming = sequence, selection, iteration; no unrestricted jumps; enabling recursive decomposition into provable units.
  • Formal proof failed in practice; falsification by tests succeeded: tests show bugs, never their absence.
  • Architecture inherits this: build testable, falsifiable modules, components and services.
Terms to carry forward
  • Structured programming: sequence, selection, iteration; goto considered harmful (1968).
  • Falsifiability; "testing shows the presence, not the absence, of bugs".
  • Functional decomposition; provable unit.
