Clean Architecture 19: Policy and Level

Source: Robert C. Martin, Clean Architecture: A Craftsman's Guide to Software Structure and Design (Pearson, 2017), chapter 19. Own-words digest. Previous: Clean Architecture 18: Boundary Anatomy. Next: Clean Architecture 20: Business Rules.

Takeaways
  • A computer program is a statement of policy: the detailed description of how inputs become outputs. Real systems hold many smaller policies (business rules, report formatting, input validation).
  • Group policies by how they change: same reasons and times means same level, same component; different means different levels, different components.
  • Level is the distance from the inputs and outputs: the farther away, the higher the level. Low-level components depend on high-level ones; source dependencies are coupled to level, not to data flow.
  • Higher-level policies change less often and for more important reasons; lower-level ones change often and urgently for less important reasons. Lower-level components are plugins to higher-level ones.

19.1 Policies and components

  • "Software systems are statements of policy." A program is "a detailed description of the policy by which inputs are transformed into outputs"
    • In nontrivial systems that policy breaks into many smaller statements: how particular business rules are calculated, how reports are formatted, how input data is validated.
  • Part of the art of architecture
    • is carefully separating those policies and regrouping them by the way they change. Policies that change for the same reasons and at the same times are at the same level and belong in the same component
    • those that change for different reasons or at different times are at different levels and belong in different components.
  • Often the regrouped components form a directed acyclic graph: nodes are components holding same-level policies, directed edges the dependencies between components at different levels
    • Those dependencies are source code, compile-time dependencies (Java import, C# using, Ruby require: what the compiler needs).
  • In a good architecture the direction of dependencies is based on the level of the components: "In every case, low-level components are designed so that they depend on high-level components."

19.2 Level

  • Strictly, level is "the distance from the inputs and outputs": the farther a policy is from both the system's inputs and outputs, the higher its level
    • the policies that manage input and output are the lowest-level policies.
  • Figure 19.1:
    • a simple encryption program reads characters from an input device, translates them using a table and writes them to an output device
    • Data flows are curved solid arrows; the properly designed source dependencies are straight dashed lines
    • Translate is the highest-level component because it is farthest from the inputs and outputs (footnote: Meilir Page-Jones called it the "Central Transform" in The Practical Guide to Structured Systems Design, 2nd ed., Yourdon Press, 1988).
  • Note: data flows and source dependencies do not always point the same way: "We want source code dependencies to be decoupled from data flow and coupled to level."
  • A wrong architecture is easy to write:
function encrypt() {
    while (true)
        writeChar(translate(readChar()));
}
  • It is wrong because the high-level encrypt function depends on the lower-level readChar and writeChar.
  • A better architecture (Figure 19.2):
    • a class diagram with a dashed border around the Encrypt class and the CharWriter and CharReader interfaces
      • all dependencies crossing that border point inward, making this unit the highest-level element
    • ConsoleReader and ConsoleWriter are classes that are low level because they are close to inputs and outputs
    • The structure decouples the high-level encryption policy from the lower-level IO policies, making encryption usable in many contexts, and IO changes are unlikely to affect it.

19.3 Why levels change at different rates

  • Policies are grouped by how they change (SRP and CCP)
    • Higher-level
      • policies (farthest from IO) tend to change less frequently and for more important reasons
      • lower-level policies (closest to IO) change frequently and with more urgency but for less important reasons
    • Even in the trivial program the IO devices are far more likely to change than the encryption algorithm, and if the algorithm does change it is likely for a more substantive reason.
  • Keeping these separate with all source dependencies pointing toward the higher-level policies reduces the impact of change: "Trivial but urgent changes at the lowest levels of the system have little or no impact on the higher, more important, levels."
  • Another view:
    • lower-level components should be plugins to the higher-level components (Figure 19.3): the Encryption component knows nothing of the IODevices component
    • IODevices depends on Encryption.

19.4 Conclusion

  • The discussion mixed the SRP, OCP, CCP, DIP, SDP and SAP: Martin invites the reader to look back and identify where each principle was used and why.

Summary points

  • A program is policy; architecture groups policies by how they change and layers them by level (distance from IO).
  • Source dependencies follow level (low to high), not data flow.
  • Lower-level components are plugins; this isolates important, stable policy from urgent, trivial change.
Terms to carry forward
  • Policy; level (distance from inputs and outputs); Central Transform.
  • Dependencies coupled to level, decoupled from data flow.
  • Lower-level components are plugins.
