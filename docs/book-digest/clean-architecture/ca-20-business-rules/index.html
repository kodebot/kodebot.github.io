Clean Architecture 20: Business Rules

Source: Robert C. Martin, Clean Architecture: A Craftsman's Guide to Software Structure and Design (Pearson, 2017), chapter 20. Own-words digest. Previous: Clean Architecture 19: Policy and Level. Next: Clean Architecture 21: Screaming Architecture.

Takeaways
  • Business rules make or save the business money. Critical Business Rules would exist even without automation; they and their Critical Business Data form an Entity.
  • Use cases hold application-specific rules: how an automated system is used, controlling "the dance of the Entities". They say nothing about the user interface.
  • Use cases depend on Entities; Entities know nothing of use cases: Entities are higher level (farther from IO).
  • Use cases exchange simple request and response models with no framework or Entity dependencies. Business rules are "the family jewels": keep them pristine, the most independent and reusable code.

20.1 Critical Business Rules and Entities

  • To divide an application into business rules and plugins we must understand what business rules are, and there are several kinds
    • Strictly, business rules are rules or procedures that make or save the business money
      • very strictly, they would do so whether or not implemented on a computer, even executed manually
    • Example: a bank charging N% interest on a loan makes money regardless of whether a program or a clerk with an abacus calculates it.
  • These are Critical Business Rules:
    • critical to the business itself and existing even with no system to automate them
    • They usually need data: a loan needs a balance, an interest rate and a payment schedule: Critical Business Data, data that exists even if the system is not automated.
  • Critical rules and critical data are inextricably bound, a good candidate for an object, called an Entity (Ivar Jacobson's name; footnote: I. Jacobson et al., Object Oriented Software Engineering, Addison-Wesley, 1992).
  • Entity:
    • an object in the system that embodies a small set of critical business rules operating on Critical Business Data
    • It either contains the data or has easy access to it; its interface consists of the functions implementing the critical rules
    • Figure 20.1: a Loan class in UML with three pieces of critical data and three related critical rules at its interface.
  • Such a class gathers the software implementing a concept critical to the business and separates it from every other concern
    • It "stands alone as a representative of the business", unsullied by databases, user interfaces or third-party frameworks
    • it could serve the business in any system regardless of presentation, data storage or machine arrangement
      • "The Entity is pure business and nothing else." Do not worry about the word "class": you need no OO language
    • you only need to bind the critical data and rules together in a single, separate software module.

20.2 Use cases

  • Not all business rules are as pure. Some make or save money by defining and constraining how an automated system operates
    • they would not be used in a manual environment, making sense only as part of an automated system.
  • Example:
    • loan officers use an application to create a new loan
    • The bank may forbid payment estimates until contact information is gathered and validated and the credit score is 500 or higher, so the system will not go to the payment estimation screen until the contact screen is complete and verified and the credit score exceeds the cutoff.
  • That is a use case:
    • a description of the way an automated system is used, specifying the input from the user, the output returned and the processing steps producing it
    • It describes application-specific business rules as opposed to the Critical Business Rules in Entities
    • Figure 20.2: an example use case whose last line refers to the Customer entity, which holds the critical rules governing the bank's relationship with customers.
  • Use cases contain the rules specifying how and when the Critical Business Rules in the Entities are invoked: "Use cases control the dance of the Entities."
  • A use case does not describe the user interface other than informally specifying the data in and out: from it you cannot tell if the application is on the web, a thick client, a console or a pure service. "Use cases do not describe how the system appears to the user": they describe the application-specific rules governing interaction between users and Entities
    • how data gets in and out is irrelevant.
  • A use case is an object, with functions implementing the application-specific rules and data elements: input data, output data and references to the Entities it uses.
  • Entities know nothing of the use cases that control them:
    • another case of DIP: high-level concepts (Entities) know nothing of lower-level ones (use cases), and the lower-level use cases know about the higher-level Entities
      • Why are Entities high level? Use cases are specific to a single application, closer to the inputs and outputs of that system
    • Entities are generalisations usable in many applications, farther from IO. **Use cases depend on Entities
    • Entities do not depend on use cases.**

20.3 Request and response models

  • Use cases expect input and produce output, but a well-formed use case has no inkling how data is communicated to the user or any other component: "We certainly don't want the code within the use case class to know about HTML or SQL!"
  • It accepts simple request data structures and returns simple response data structures
    • These depend on nothing: they do not derive from framework interfaces like HttpRequest and HttpResponse, know nothing of the web and share none of the UI's trappings
    • This lack of dependencies is critical: if the models are not independent, the use cases depending on them are indirectly bound to whatever dependencies the models carry.
  • Avoid having these structures refer to Entity objects,
    • tempting because they share so much data. The two serve very different purposes and will change for very different reasons, so tying them together violates CCP and SRP
    • the result is "lots of tramp data, and lots of conditionals in your code".

20.4 Conclusion

  • Business rules are the reason a software system exists:
    • the core functionality, the code that makes or saves money, "the family jewels"
    • They should stay pristine, unsullied by baser concerns such as UI or database
    • Ideally the business rules are the heart of the system, with lesser concerns plugged in to them, and the most independent and reusable code in the system.

Summary points

  • Critical rules plus critical data make an Entity; application-specific rules make a use case.
  • Use cases depend on Entities; both are independent of UI, database and frameworks.
  • Keep request and response models plain and separate from Entities.
Terms to carry forward
  • Critical Business Rule, Critical Business Data, Entity.
  • Use case; request and response models; tramp data.
  • Use cases depend on Entities (higher level).
