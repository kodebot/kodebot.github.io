Clean Architecture 27: Services, Great and Small

Source: Robert C. Martin, Clean Architecture: A Craftsman's Guide to Software Structure and Design (Pearson, 2017), chapter 27. Own-words digest. Previous: Clean Architecture 26: The Main Component. Next: Clean Architecture 28: The Test Boundary.

Takeaways
  • Services are not, by themselves, an architecture. The architecture is the boundaries separating high-level policy from low-level detail following the Dependency Rule; services that merely separate behaviours are "little more than expensive function calls".
  • Two popular benefits are only partly true: decoupling (services still share data and resources) and independent development and deployment (coupling forces coordination).
  • Cross-cutting concerns (the Kitty problem) hit functional decompositions into services hard; an object-oriented, component-based design absorbs them via polymorphic extension.
  • Architectural boundaries run through services, dividing them into components, not between them.

27.1 Service architecture?

  • Service-oriented and micro-service "architectures" are popular because services seem strongly decoupled (only partly true) and appear to support independent development and deployment (only partly true).
  • Is using services an architecture? "This is patently untrue." The architecture of a system is defined by boundaries that separate high-level policy from low-level detail and follow the Dependency Rule
    • Services that simply separate application behaviours are little more than expensive function calls and not necessarily architecturally significant.
  • That does not mean services lack value:
    • there are often substantial benefits to services that separate functionality across processes and platforms, whether or not they obey the Dependency Rule
    • but "services, in and of themselves, do not define an architecture".
  • Analogy:
    • in a monolithic or component-based system, the architecture is defined by the function calls that cross architectural boundaries and follow the Dependency Rule
    • many other functions simply separate behaviours and are not architecturally significant
      • "Services are, after all, just function calls across process and/or platform boundaries." Some are architecturally significant, some are not
    • this chapter cares about the former.

27.2 Service benefits?

  • The heading's question mark signals a challenge to "the current popular orthodoxy".
  • The decoupling fallacy:
    • the supposed benefit is strong decoupling: each service runs in a different process, even processor, so they cannot access each other's variables, and interfaces must be well defined
    • There is "some truth, but not very much"
    • Services are decoupled at the level of variables, but can still be coupled by shared resources in a processor or on the network, and are strongly coupled by the data they share: if a new field is added to a record passed between services, every service operating on it must change, and they must agree on its interpretation: coupled to the record and so indirectly to each other
    • As for well-defined interfaces:
      • that is "no less true for functions"
      • service interfaces are no more formal, rigorous or well defined than function interfaces: "something of an illusion".
  • The fallacy of independent development and deployment:
    • the belief is that dedicated teams can write, maintain and operate a service (a dev-ops strategy) and that dozens, hundreds or thousands of independent services and teams can build large enterprise systems. "Some truth, but only some": first
    • history shows large enterprise systems can be built from monoliths and component-based systems too; second, because of the decoupling fallacy
    • services cannot always be independently developed, deployed and operated: to the extent they are coupled by data or behaviour, development, deployment and operation must be coordinated.

27.3 The Kitty problem

  • The taxi aggregator again:
    • it knows many taxi providers in a city and lets customers order rides, choosing by pickup time, cost, luxury and driver experience
    • To be scalable the architects built lots of micro-services and split developers into many small teams, each responsible for a correspondingly small number of services (footnote: so the number of micro-services will be roughly the number of programmers).
  • Figure 27.1: TaxiUI serves customers on mobile devices
    • TaxiFinder examines the inventories of the various TaxiSuppliers and deposits candidate taxis in a short-term data record attached to the user
    • TaxiSelector takes the user's criteria and chooses a taxi, handing it to TaxiDispatcher, which orders it.
  • After more than a year in operation, marketing announces a kitten delivery service: users can order kittens to their homes or businesses
    • the company sets up several kitten collection points
    • a nearby taxi collects a kitten and delivers it. One supplier has agreed to take part
    • others may or may not. Some drivers may be allergic to cats and must never be chosen
    • some customers too, so a vehicle used for kitten delivery within the last 3 days must not be selected for customers declaring allergies.
  • "How many of those services will have to change to implement this feature? All of them." Development and deployment must be very carefully coordinated: the services are all coupled and cannot be independently developed, deployed and maintained.
  • This is the problem of cross-cutting concerns, faced by every system, service oriented or not: functional decompositions like Figure 27.1 are "very vulnerable to new features that cut across all those functional behaviors".

27.4 Objects to the rescue

  • In a component-based architecture, careful use of the SOLID principles would have yielded a set of classes polymorphically extended to handle new features (Figure 27.2)
    • The classes roughly correspond to the services, but note the boundaries and that dependencies follow the Dependency Rule.
  • Much of the original services' logic stays in the base classes of the object model
    • the ride-specific logic is extracted into a Rides component, and the new kitten feature goes in a Kittens component. Both override the abstract base classes in the original components using a pattern such as Template Method or Strategy, follow the Dependency Rule, and have their classes created by factories under the control of the UI.
  • Implementing Kitty then changes only TaxiUI
    • a new jar, Gem or DLL is added and dynamically loaded at runtime: the Kitty feature is decoupled, and independently developable and deployable.

27.5 Component-based services

  • Can services do the same? "Yes! Services do not need to be little monoliths." Design them with the SOLID principles and a component structure so new components can be added without changing existing ones.
  • Think of a service in Java as a set of abstract classes in one or more jar files, and each new feature or extension as another jar with classes extending them
    • Deploying a feature is then adding jars to the services' load paths, not redeploying the services: adding features conforms to the Open-Closed Principle
    • Figure 27.3: the services remain, but each has its own internal component design, with new features as derived classes in their own components.

27.6 Cross-cutting concerns

  • "Architectural boundaries do not fall between services. Rather, those boundaries run through the services, dividing them into components." To handle the cross-cutting concerns every significant system faces, services need internal component architectures that follow the Dependency Rule (Figure 27.4): the services do not define the system's architectural boundaries
    • the components within them do.

27.7 Conclusion

  • Services are useful for scalability and develop-ability, but "not, in and of themselves, architecturally significant elements"
    • A system's architecture is defined by the boundaries drawn within it and the dependencies crossing them, not by the physical mechanisms of communication and execution.
  • A service might be a single component fully surrounded by an architectural boundary, or several components separated by boundaries
    • in rare cases (footnote: "We hope they are rare. Unfortunately, experience suggests otherwise.") clients and services are so coupled they have no architectural significance whatever.

Summary points

  • Services are deployment and communication mechanisms; architecture is the Dependency Rule's boundaries.
  • Data coupling and cross-cutting features defeat the decoupling and independence claims.
  • Give services internal component structure (OCP, polymorphic extension) so features can be added without changing existing parts.
Terms to carry forward
  • Decoupling fallacy; independent development and deployment fallacy.
  • Kitty problem; cross-cutting concern.
  • Component-based service; boundaries run through services.
