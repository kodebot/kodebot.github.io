Clean Architecture 36: Appendix A, Architecture Archaeology

Source: Robert C. Martin, Clean Architecture: A Craftsman's Guide to Software Structure and Design (Pearson, 2017), Appendix A. Autobiographical; own-words digest, much shorter than the source. Previous: Clean Architecture 35: Afterword. Back to Clean Architecture: contents.

Takeaways
  • A 45-year tour of Martin's projects since 1970, mined for architectural lessons, many learned by getting them wrong.
  • Boundaries pay off: a supervisor that hid devices, a SAC/COLT split over a slow link, and vectorised EPROMs (a literal plugin architecture).
  • Smearing details everywhere hurts: modem bit-patterns, embedded SQL, and framework-shaped databases each became expensive to replace.
  • Over-architecture and false reuse also fail: ROSE, and a first framework that was not reusable until built alongside several users.

A.1 Union accounting system (late 1960s to 1971)

  • ASC Tabulating
    • contracted with Teamsters Local 705 to provide an accounting system, first on a GE Datanet 30: a room-sized, discrete-transistor machine with 16K × 18 bits of core (cycle about 7 microseconds), 7-track tapes and a 20 MB disk with 36-inch platters (seek about half a second to a second) that sounded like a jet engine
    • No operating system or file system, only an assembler: they wrote their own disk driver, choosing track, platter and sector
    • About a dozen data-entry clerks used CRT terminals at 30 characters per second on leased lines with 300-baud modems, printing on ASR35 teletypes
    • The system handled Agents, Employers and Members records plus dues posting and ledger changes, written in assembler by a consultant who fitted it into 16K.
  • In 1971, aged 18, Martin and two friends were hired to replace it with a Varian 620/f minicomputer (32K × 16 core, about 1 microsecond cycle; IBM-2314-style disks, 30 MB on 14-inch platters)
    • They built an overlay system (applications swapped between disk and a memory block) and a preemptive supervisor, plus every driver, in 8 or 9 months of too many 80-hour weeks.
  • It had two boundaries
    • Character output, dependency normal: applications passed strings to the supervisor and never knew a 30-cps terminal was involved
    • Starting applications, dependency inverted: the supervisor started every application by jumping to the same address in the overlay area, a polymorphic interface leaving it ignorant of the applications.

A.2 Laser Trim (1973)

  • At Teradyne Applied Systems (Chicago): lasers trimmed resistors on ceramic substrates to within about a tenth of a percent, on an M365 (an enhanced PDP-8) with a tape-cartridge loop (no disk, no rewind, about 1 foot per second), an uppercase CRT, an ED-402 block editor and a 30-minute compile with roughly a 1 in 10 chance of a tape error (so they wrote two tapes).
  • Architecture:
    • a shared Master Operating Program (MOP), forked by each division and merged by hand from marked-up listings
    • a utility layer tightly coupled to the MOP
    • and an isolation layer presenting a virtual machine for customers' trim jobs in a DSL
      • Not an attempt at machine independence
      • "The boundaries in this application were soft at best" and couplings were everywhere, "typical of software in the early 1970s".

A.3 Aluminium die-cast monitoring (mid-1970s)

  • At Outboard Marine Corporation, an IBM System/7 counted and timed cycles of die-cast machines and sent statistics to a 370 running CICS-COBOL
    • All code was assembler, interrupt driven, with no OS or libraries
    • Martin disliked the culture and was fired ("the only programming job I was ever fired from")
    • Its one lesson: the set program interrupt (SPI) instruction, what Java calls Thread.yield().

A.4 4-TEL (from 1976)

  • 4-TEL
    • tested every telephone line in a service area nightly and reported lines needing repair. Central office line testers (COLTs) held the dialing and measurement hardware at each central office (up to 10,000 lines each)
    • one service area computer (SAC) dialled them at 300 baud.
  • The split:
    • at first the COLTs did everything and testers watched characters trickle over 30 cps
    • Moving analysis and reports to the SAC and leaving dialing and measurement in the COLTs made screens fast and shrank COLT memory
    • The boundary was very clean: short packets of a simple DSL (DIAL XXXX, MEASURE).
  • Replacing the M365
    • by an 8085 board set (32K RAM, three ROM boards of Intel 2708 EPROMs, hand-translated, 30K of code) meant 30 chips burned from one binary
    • Any code change shifted every address, so every site visit replaced all 30 chips (mislabelled, bent pins, spares carried).
  • Vectorization
    • (three months): 32 source files under 1K each, each with an ORG address and a fixed 40-byte table of at most 20 subroutine addresses
    • a RAM area of 32 vector tables loaded at boot
    • every call indirect through the RAM vectors
      • Now one or two chips could be reburned
      • "We had made the chips independently deployable
      • We had invented polymorphic dispatch
      • We had invented objects." It was a literal plugin architecture: plug a chip in and its menu appeared
    • faulty routines could even be patched by dial-up by repointing a vector to hex-typed code in spare RAM.
  • The SAC (M365, 1976, about 60,000 lines of assembler, a home-grown non-preemptive task switcher called MPS) had no isolation of device or UI logic from business rules: modem bit-control and terminal formatting were smeared throughout, with constant race conditions.
    • Dispatch determination:
      • code deciding which craft (central office, cable or drop) to send was written by a brilliant poor communicator ("three weeks of staring at the ceiling and two days of code pouring out") and nobody could change it without breaking it
      • management locked it: "officially rigid". It taught the value of clean code.
    • The modem hack:
      • a new in-house modem with an entirely different control structure arrived despite pleas for compatibility, and old and new had to coexist
      • Martin's team patched the one bus-write subroutine to translate old bit sequences to new, "the worst hack imaginable"
      • It taught isolating hardware from business rules and abstracting interfaces.
    • The Grand Redesign in the Sky:
      • a "Tiger Team" rewrite in C on UNIX and an 80286 machine ("Deep Thought") failed repeatedly
      • a redesign cannot catch a large staff maintaining the old system. In Europe, UK developers forked the SAC code
      • three reintegration attempts failed.

A.5 C, BOSS, pCCU, DLU/DRU and VRS (1980s)

  • C and BOSS:
    • Martin lobbied for a PDP-11/60 (two 25 MB disk packs: "Fifty megabytes!"), a cross-compiler pipeline to the 8085 and C (from Kernighan and Ritchie, with a Whitesmiths compiler)
    • BOSS (Basic Operating System and Scheduler) gave non-preemptive tasks switched by a polling loop around block(eventCheckFunction);.
  • pCCU and the schedule trap:
    • digital switches meant dialing (central office) and measurement (local distribution) needed separating, the CCU/CMU architecture ("we could have saved ourselves a fortune" recognising that boundary earlier). The team postponed it, then a customer needed it in a month
    • a tiny customer with one office and two distribution points let a simple pCCU (first C and BOSS product shipped) be written in about a week.
  • DLU/DRU
    • (Display Local and Remote Units): multiplexing terminals over a 9600-bps conditioned modem link
    • Martin's DLU used a dataflow, queue-based assembly line; Mike Carew's DRU used one task per terminal
    • Both worked: "software architectures can be wildly different, yet equally effective".
  • VRS
    • (Voice Response System) let cable craftsmen operate fault location by touch tone
    • The team picked UNIFY and embedded SQL, "smeared throughout the body of that code" with vendor quirks
    • When UNIFY was cancelled, three months of effort to move to another vendor failed; they paid a third party ever-rising maintenance
    • Lesson: databases are details to isolate, and avoid strong coupling to third-party software.

A.6 Electronic Receptionist and Craft Dispatch System (1983 to 1985)

  • The Electronic Receptionist (ER):
    • callers spelled a name in touch tones
    • ER dialled the person's listed numbers in turn and otherwise recorded a message: the first voice mail system, patented by Teradyne
      • Built on the 80286 board under MP/M-86, with assembler voice boards sharing memory
      • Architecture "would today be called service oriented": a listener per line and handler processes passing state through disk files
    • not a clean plugin architecture, but real boundaries and independent deployment. Marketing failed
    • the patent application was dropped and a competitor's was granted.
  • Craft Dispatch System (CDS):
    • ER repurposed to read trouble tickets to repairmen
    • Martin pushed it to micro-services: every state transition started a new process, with the state machine externalised in a text file (changing flow with no code change: OCP, hot-swappable, "an effective BPEL")
    • Disk files were too slow, so they invented 3DBB (Three-Dimensional Black Board), shared memory accessed by name that held only strings (no pointers across MP/M partitions), and FLD (Field Labeled Data), binary-tree name/value data convertible to strings: "XML ..
    • in 1985"
    • "There is nothing new under the Sun."

A.7 Clear Communications, Uncle Bob and ROSE (1988 to 1990s)

  • At the startup Clear Communications (T1 line quality monitoring, on Sun and X-Windows), they coded for 70 to 80 hours a week without architecture, including a 3000-line C function called gi() (Graphic Interpreter) and a full seven-layer communications stack, and after three years had not sold enough.
  • Meanwhile Martin had set up a uucp link (email and Netnews) and, with Sun's C++ compiler, read Stroustrup, Wirfs-Brock, Coad, Goldberg, Coplien and especially Grady Booch
    • he debated C++ and OO on Usenet for two years: "It was in one of those debates that the foundations of the SOLID principles were laid." A colleague, Billy Vogel, nicknamed him "Uncle Bob".
  • A recruiter then offered a job at Rational (Booch's company) building a CASE tool: ROSE, joined in 1990, drawing Booch diagrams
    • It had real layers (GUI to representation to manipulation rules to database) with strictly enforced dependencies, pointing with flow of control, not toward policy, which "aided the eventual demise"
    • An object-oriented database (objects appearing in memory "like magic") proved "a big, slow, intrusive, expensive third-party framework", and over-architecture with layer overheads cut productivity
      • after two tepid releases it was replaced by a small team's "cute little application"
    • Lesson: architecture must be flexible enough to match the size of the problem
    • He then wrote articles for C++ Report and his first book, Designing Object-Oriented C++ Applications Using the Booch Method, and kept the "Uncle Bob" brand.

A.8 Architects Registry Exam (early 1990s)

  • Consulting for ETS (under contract to NCARB), automating the architects' registration exam: 18 vignettes, each a CAD-like GUI app plus a scoring app, 36 applications sharing mechanisms
    • Martin and Jim Newkirk promised a reusable framework so later apps would "pop out every few weeks" ("you should be face-palming")
    • Vignette Grande took a year: 45,000 lines of framework and 6000 of application code
    • The framework did not fit the next vignettes.
  • They restarted with four vignettes at once, another year and another 45,000-line framework, vignettes of 3000 to 6000 lines
    • GUI vignettes were plugins to the framework (policy in the framework)
      • scoring plugged the other way (policy in the vignette): both consistent with the Dependency Rule, though statically linked C++
    • Later vignettes popped out on schedule
    • Lesson: "You can't make a reusable framework until you first make a usable framework." Reusable frameworks must be built in concert with several reusing applications.

A.9 Conclusion

  • A partial, autobiographical history, stopping in the early 1990s (a later book would cover the late 1990s); "I hope you were able to learn some things along the way."

Summary points

  • Hide devices, databases and vendors behind boundaries; hacks multiply otherwise.
  • Plugins and polymorphic dispatch arose from real deployment pain (the 30 chips).
  • Match architecture to the problem size, and build reusable frameworks from several concrete users.
Terms to carry forward
  • Dependency normal versus dependency inverted boundary.
  • Vectorization (RAM vector tables as polymorphism).
  • Externalised state machine; 3DBB; FLD.
  • Over-architecture; "usable before reusable".
