DDD Quickly 1: Preface, introduction and what DDD is

Source: Abel Avram (DDD summary), with preface by Floyd Marinescu (managing editor), Domain Driven Design Quickly (InfoQ / C4Media, 2006, free online edition, ISBN 978-1-4116-0925-9), preface, introduction, "What Is Domain-Driven Design" (pp. iv-13). Free edition at http://www.infoq.com/minibooks/domain-driven-design-quickly (printed copy at http://www.lulu.com/content/325231). Production credits in the book: "DDD Summary by Abel Avram; Managing Editor Floyd Marinescu"; special thanks to Eric Evans. This is a summary of Eric Evans, Domain-Driven Design: Tackling Complexity in the Heart of Software (Addison-Wesley, 2004, ISBN 0-321-12521-5), plus Jimmy Nilsson's Applying DDD and discussion forums. Next: DDD Quickly 2: The Ubiquitous Language.

[!abstract] The one-minute version Software exists to serve a real-world domain (banking, air traffic). So you start from the domain, learn it from the domain experts, and build a model: a selective, rigorously organised abstraction of that knowledge. Software and domain experts build and refine the model together, in both directions, instead of passing requirements one way (waterfall) or avoiding design (naive agile).

Preface (Floyd Marinescu, co-founder and chief editor of InfoQ.com)

  • He first heard of DDD and met Eric Evans at a small gathering of architects at a mountain summit organised by Bruce Eckel in summer 2005
    • Attendees he names: Martin Fowler, Rod Johnson, Cameron Purdy, Randy Stafford, Gregor Hohpe.
  • His claim:
    • the enterprise and web community was "tainted by years of hype"
    • in Java, good domain modelling was lost in the hype of EJB and the container/component models of 1999 to 2004
    • technology shifts were moving back to traditional object orientation but lacked a vision for object orientation at enterprise scale
      • DDD supplies that vision
      • (The author's opinion, not independently verified.)
  • Why this book: very few outside a small group of senior architects knew DDD, so InfoQ commissioned a short, quick-to-read, free summary plus a cheap pocket print version.
  • It introduces no new concepts; it is a crash course, no substitute for Evans' examples and case studies or Nilsson's hands-on ones.

Introduction

  • Software is an instrument to help with the complexity of life, always tied to a domain it manages (e.g. software coordinating thousands of airplanes in the air)
    • It cannot be decoupled from that sphere of reality.
  • Software design is an art: principles and techniques exist, but no exact path from a real-world need to the code module. Every product carries the personal touch of its designers.
  • This book focuses on domain-driven design, which emerged over about two decades and crystallised in the last few years
    • Evans wrote down much of the accumulated knowledge
    • A discussion group existed at http://groups.yahoo.com/group/domaindrivendesign.
  • It is an introduction meant to "whet your appetite", not a detailed treatment.

What is domain-driven design

Start with the domain

  • Software usually automates real-world processes or solves real business problems; that is its domain. Software originates from and is deeply related to it.
  • Car-manufacturing metaphor:
    • workers who specialise in parts see a car as a huge collection of parts to fit together
    • A good car starts with a vision, careful specifications and months or years of design, including models tested under conditions, with the design changed by the results
    • Software is similar: you cannot just sit down and type code for anything complex.
  • You cannot build banking software without understanding banking.
    • Who knows banking? Not the architect (just a customer), not the analyst (knows how to analyse, given the ingredients), not the developer
    • The bankers: they know the details, catches, issues and rules
    • So start from the domain.
  • Software must fit the domain harmoniously, or it introduces strain, malfunction, damage and chaos
    • The best way: make the software a reflection of the domain: it incorporates the core concepts and elements and realises their relationships precisely
    • Someone with no banking knowledge should learn a lot by reading the domain-model code
    • Software rooted deeply in the domain reacts well to change over time.

The model

  • A domain is part of the world; you cannot pour it into the keyboard. You build an abstraction: a model, a blueprint in your mind, incomplete at first and clearer with work.
  • Eric Evans' definition (quoted):
    • a domain model "is not a particular diagram
    • it is the idea that the diagram is intended to convey. It is not just the knowledge in a domain expert's head
    • it is a rigorously organized and selective abstraction of that knowledge. A diagram can represent and communicate a model, as can carefully written code, as can an English sentence."
  • The mind cannot hold a whole domain, so you organise, divide, group into logical modules, and leave some parts out
    • What to keep and what to drop is part of design
    • Example: banking software tracks the customer's address, not the eye colour; other cases are less obvious.
  • The model must come out of your head: communicate it with domain experts, fellow designers and developers precisely, completely and without ambiguity, through diagrams, use cases, drawings, writing, and a shared language (detailed in the next chapter).

Software design vs code design

  • Software design
    • is the architecture of a house (the big picture)
    • code design is where the painting hangs on a wall. Code-design mistakes are cheap to fix
    • software-design errors are costly (moving a painting vs tearing down a wall). Both matter: apply design patterns when needed and good coding techniques for clean, maintainable code.

Waterfall, agile, and DDD

  • Waterfall:
    • business experts give requirements to analysts, who produce a model and pass it to developers
    • One-way flow of knowledge with no feedback from analysts to business experts or from developers to analysts
    • Used with some success, but flawed.
  • Agile (e.g. Extreme Programming):
    • a movement against waterfall, since a complete model upfront is very hard to produce and requirements change
    • also fights "analysis paralysis". It resists upfront design in favour of implementation flexibility, iteration, continuous stakeholder participation and refactoring.
  • Agile's own problems:
    • "simplicity" means different things to different people
    • continuous refactoring without solid design principles produces code hard to understand or change
    • fear of over-engineering becomes fear of deep, well-thought-out design.
  • DDD combines design and development practice: good design accelerates development, and feedback from development improves the design.

Building domain knowledge: the air-traffic example

Project: a flight monitoring system (a subset of air traffic control) that tracks every flight over an area, checks that it follows its route, and detects possible collisions. Air traffic controllers are the domain specialists but are not system designers; they cannot hand over a complete description. You must extract the essentials and generalise.

The model evolves through conversation:

  1. First agreement: each aircraft has a departure and a destination.
  2. The controller says each plane has a flight plan describing the whole trip; then you hear the word route. The route is what planes follow in the air, so associate the aircraft with a Route, which in turn has departure and destination.
  3. The route is made of consecutive segments passing through predetermined fixes. Now departure and destination are just two of the fixes. (The controller may not see it so, but it is a useful abstraction.)
  4. A fix looked like a 3D point; the controller actually sees the route as the projection on the Earth's surface, so a fix is a 2D point (latitude and longitude).

What is happening: you and the experts exchange knowledge; they dig essential concepts out of the domain, unpolished and disorganised at first. By asking the right questions and processing the answers you sketch a domain model, "neither complete nor correct, but the start you need".

  • Discussions are long
    • you might build an early prototype, find problems, and change the model. Communication is two-way: feedback improves the model and the understanding of the domain.
  • Experts organise their knowledge in a way that is not always best for a software system
    • the analytical mind of the designer unearths key concepts and gives structure to future discussions.
  • The model is the place where the two areas of expertise meet. The process is time-consuming and should be, because the software must blend perfectly with the domain.
