Fundamentals of Software Architecture 1: Introduction

Source: Mark Richards and Neal Ford, Fundamentals of Software Architecture, 2nd ed. (O'Reilly, 2025), ch. 1. Previous: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 0: Preface. Next: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 2: Architectural Thinking.

Takeaways
  • Software architecture has four dimensions: an architecture style, the characteristics it must support, the logical components that implement behaviour, and the decisions that justify it all.
  • Three laws: everything is a trade-off; why is more important than how; most decisions are not binary but sit on a spectrum.
  • An architect has eight expectations, from making decisions to navigating politics. Half the job is leadership and people skills.
  • All architectures are products of their context: microservices were unaffordable in 2002 and are practical now thanks to open source and DevOps.
  • The book runs in three parts: Foundations, Architecture Styles, Techniques and Soft Skills.

1.1 Who this is for and why context matters

  • Readers: developers heading for the next career step, project managers, and "accidental architects" who make architecture decisions without the title.
  • The authors argue architecture is a role that is hard for generative AI to replace: it means evaluating trade-offs in complex, changing contexts, with incomplete information.

Architecture can only be understood in context.

  • Late-20th-century goals centred on using shared infrastructure efficiently, because operating systems, application servers and database servers were all commercial and expensive.
  • In 2002, a microservices-style design would have meant telling operations: "50 Windows licenses, another 30 application-server licenses, and at least 50 database server licenses".
  • Such architectures are possible today only because of open source and the engineering practices of the DevOps revolution.

1.2 Defining software architecture

The definition has four dimensions, drawn in the book as one frame (Figure 1-1): the system's structure is the heavy frame, and the other four elements sit around it.

The four dimensions side by side: The system's structure (the heavy black lines in the book's figure) is what these dimensions support.

Dimension What it defines
Architecture characteristics ("-ilities") The system's capabilities and the criteria for its success: what the system should do
Logical components The system's behaviour: the domains, entities and workflows of the application
Architecture style The starting point: the easiest implementation path for a given set of requirements
Architecture decisions The rules for how the system should be constructed

The order in which architects analyse them:

  1. Characteristics and logical components first (each has later chapters).
  2. With those known, choose an architecture style as the starting point.
  3. Decisions record the constraints and justify the rest (Chapter 21).

Decisions form the constraints of the system and direct teams on what is and is not allowed. Example: only the Business and Services layers of a layered architecture may access the database, so the Presentation layer cannot call it directly (Figure 1-5 calls the bottom layer the Persistence layer).

1.3 Laws of software architecture

The authors hoped to find 10 or 15 universal "laws". They found two in the first edition and one more in the second. Chapter 27 returns to them.

First Law: everything in software architecture is a trade-off.

  • Nothing sits on a clean spectrum; each decision weighs many variables whose values depend on circumstances.
  • If you think you found something that is not a trade-off, you likely have not identified the trade-off yet.
  • Corollary 1: you cannot do trade-off analysis once and be done with it.
  • Corollary 2: there is no "One Big Trade-off Jamboree" to fix defaults, because every situation needs the trade-offs re-evaluated.
    • Example from the authors: a team defaulting to choreography only in distributed workflows found it works sometimes and is "a spectacular disaster" at other times (see the later section on choreography and orchestration).

Second Law: why is more important than how.

  • An experienced architect shown an unfamiliar architecture can understand how it works, but may struggle with why earlier decisions were made.
  • The why includes the trade-offs considered, which is the context of "this decision versus another".

Third Law: most architecture decisions are not binary but exist on a spectrum between extremes.

1.4 Expectations of an architect

The role ranges from expert programmer to setting a company's strategic technical direction, so the authors list what is expected rather than defining it. Eight core expectations apply whatever the title:

Make architecture decisions

  • The architect defines the decisions and design principles that guide technology choices for a team, a department or the enterprise.
  • Guide is the key word: guide choices, do not specify them.
    • Choosing React.js is a technical decision; telling teams to use a reactive-based framework (Angular, Elm, React.js, Vue and so on) is the architectural one.
  • The test: does the decision guide teams to the right technical choice, or make it for them?
  • Sometimes a specific technology must be named to preserve a characteristic such as scalability, performance or availability. Chapter 21 covers the balance.

Continually analyse the architecture

  • Architecture vitality assesses how viable an architecture defined three or more years ago is today, given changes in business and technology.
  • Without this, most architectures suffer structural decay: coding or design changes that damage the required characteristics, such as performance, availability and scalability.
  • Testing and release environments matter too: fast code changes give no agility if testing takes weeks and release takes months.

Keep current with trends

  • Architect decisions tend to be long-lasting and hard to change, so understanding trends keeps them relevant.
  • Examples from the authors: cloud-based storage and deployment, and generative AI at the time of writing.
  • Chapter 2 gives techniques and resources.

Ensure compliance with decisions

  • Continually verify that teams follow the decisions and principles that were defined, documented and communicated.
  • Example:
    • the architect bars the Presentation layer from the database so database changes do not affect it
    • A UI developer disagrees and gives it direct access "for performance reasons"
    • Without compliance checks the violation stands and the required characteristics are lost.
  • Chapter 6 covers automated fitness functions and other tools.

Understand diverse technologies

  • Not an expert in every framework, but familiar with a variety, since most environments are heterogeneous.
  • Favour technical breadth over depth: know the pros and cons of 10 caching products rather than be an expert in one.
  • Stretch outside your comfort zone to get experience in several languages, platforms and technologies.

Know the business domain

  • The business domain is the problem, goals and requirements the architecture serves.
  • Example: an architect at a bank who does not know terms like average directional index, aleatory contracts, rates rally or nonpriority debt cannot talk to stakeholders and loses credibility.
  • The most successful architects combine broad hands-on technical knowledge with strong knowledge of one domain, and can talk to C-level executives in their language.

Possess interpersonal skills

  • Includes teamwork, facilitation and leadership.
  • Gerald Weinberg's line, as quoted: "No matter what they tell you, it's always a people problem."
  • Leadership skills are "at least half" of what makes an effective architect.
  • Strong leaders stand out in a market flooded with architects; excellent technologists who cannot lead, mentor or communicate "have difficulties holding down a job".

Understand and navigate politics

Two scenarios show why:

Scenario Who must approve Why
A developer picks a design pattern to simplify convoluted code Nobody Code structure, class design and pattern choice are part of programming
An architect splits a CRM database into application silos, each database reachable only from its owning application Almost everyone challenges it Other systems now need remote calls for CRM data; product owners, project managers and stakeholders face extra cost and effort
  • The silo decision gives better control over customer data, security and schema changes.
  • Former developers are used to deciding without review; as architects they must justify and fight for almost every decision.
  • Chapter 25 is devoted to negotiation.

1.5 Roadmap

  • Part I, Foundations:
    • the two key elements of architectural structure, architectural characteristics and logical components. Different techniques analyse each
    • together they let the architect choose a style.
  • Part II, Architecture Styles:
    • a catalogue of named topologies
    • It shows structural and communication differences and compares styles across data topologies, teams and physical architectures.
  • Part III, Techniques and Soft Skills:
    • the soft skills (those involving people, not technology)
    • They are often the hardest for new architects because the usual route into the role rewards technical, not interpersonal, brilliance.

Summary points

  • Architecture = style + characteristics + logical components + decisions, supported by the system's structure.
  • Three laws: trade-offs everywhere, why over how, decisions on a spectrum.
  • Eight expectations; guide choices rather than dictate them; leadership is at least half the job.
  • Architectures reflect their era's costs and practices.
Terms to carry forward
  • Accidental architect: someone who makes architecture decisions without the title.
  • Architecture characteristics: the "-ilities"; the capabilities and success criteria of a system.
  • Logical components: the building blocks that structure a system's behaviour (domains, entities, workflows).
  • Architecture style: a named topology used as the starting point.
  • Architecture decision: a rule for how the system is constructed.
  • Architecture vitality: how viable an old architecture still is given business and technology change.
  • Structural decay: loss of required characteristics through accumulated code or design changes.
  • Business domain: the problem, goals and requirements the system serves.
  • Fitness function: an automated check that verifies compliance with an architecture decision (Chapter 6).
