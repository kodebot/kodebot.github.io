Fundamentals of Software Architecture 2: Architectural Thinking

Source: Mark Richards and Neal Ford, Fundamentals of Software Architecture, 2nd ed. (O'Reilly, 2025), ch. 2. Previous: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 1: Introduction. Next: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 3: Modularity.

Takeaways
  • Architecture is about structure, design about appearance; most decisions sit on a spectrum, judged by how strategic, how costly to change and how significant the trade-offs are.
  • Architects need technical breadth more than depth: the knowledge pyramid shows why, and the 20-minute rule and a personal technology radar build it.
  • Thinking architecturally means analysing trade-offs, not just benefits: the auction example shows topics beat queues on extensibility but lose on security, contracts and monitoring.
  • Business drivers translate into architecture characteristics (Chapters 4 to 7).
  • Architects should still code, while avoiding the Bottleneck Trap.

2.1 Architecture versus design

A house gives the analogy. Floors, roof shape and bedroom count define its structure (architecture); carpet, wall colour and lamps are its design.

  • Choosing microservices defines the system's structure, so it is architecture.
  • The look and feel of a UI screen is design.
  • Many decisions (splitting a service, picking a UI framework) fall between the two, so three criteria help locate them.

Strategic versus tactical

The more strategic a decision, the more architectural. Strategic decisions are long-term; tactical ones are short-term and usually independent of other decisions. Three questions help:

Question More design (tactical) More architecture (strategic)
How much thought and planning? A couple of minutes Weeks of planning
How many people are involved? Alone or with a colleague Many meetings with many stakeholders
Long-term vision or short-term action? Likely to change soon Will last a very long time

The authors call these questions "a bit subjective" but still useful.

Level of effort

  • Martin Fowler, in "Who Needs an Architect?", writes that architecture is "the stuff that's hard to change".
  • Monolithic layered architecture to microservices: significant effort, so architecture.
  • Rearranging fields on a screen: minimal effort, so design.

Significance of trade-offs

  • The more significant the trade-offs, the more architectural the decision.
  • Microservices give better scalability, agility, elasticity and fault tolerance, but are highly complex, very expensive, poor on data consistency, and slower because of service coupling
    • These are significant, so the decision is architectural.
  • Even design decisions have trade-offs: splitting a class file improves maintainability and readability at the cost of more classes. These are minor, so it is design.

2.2 Technical breadth

Developers need depth: deep knowledge of a language, platform, framework or product. Architects need breadth: a little about a lot.

The knowledge pyramid holds all technical knowledge in three levels:

  1. Stuff you know: the technologies you use daily and are good at (a Java programmer knowing Java). Smallest level, since nobody is expert at everything.
  2. Stuff you know you don't know: things you have heard of but cannot use (most people know Clojure is a Lisp-based language but cannot write it). Bigger, because familiarity is easier than expertise.
  3. Stuff you don't know you don't know: the perfect solution that exists but you do not know it does. The largest level.

The goal: move things from the third level into the second, and, when expertise becomes necessary, from the second to the top.

  • Early in a career, growing the top means gaining valuable expertise, but it must be maintained: a Ruby on Rails expert who ignores it for a year or two loses the edge
    • The top part is technical depth.
  • For an architect the important parts are the top and middle. How far the middle penetrates the bottom is their technical breadth.
  • It is better to know that five solutions exist for a problem than to be expert in one. The wise course is to sacrifice some hard-won expertise to broaden the portfolio

Figures 2-3 to 2-5 show how the pyramid changes: developers keep their expertise, depth and breadth are defined, and the architect's breadth grows while depth shrinks.

  • some areas remain, others "usefully atrophy".

Two dysfunctions come from this shift in perspective:

  • An architect tries to keep expertise in many areas, succeeds in none and works themselves ragged.
  • Stale expertise:
    • the mistaken sense that outdated information is still cutting edge
    • It shows in large companies where founding developers moved into leadership but still decide with "ancient criteria".

Frozen Caveman antipattern

  • Andrew Koenig defines an antipattern as something that seems a good idea at first but leads into trouble.
  • The Frozen Caveman architect reverts to a pet irrational concern on every architecture.
  • Example from Neal Ford's colleague: a centralised architecture where client architects kept asking "But what if we lose Italy?", because years earlier a freak communication problem cut headquarters off from its Italian stores.
  • It typically affects architects burned by a past decision or event. Risk assessment should be realistic, separating genuine from perceived technical risk.

The 20-minute rule

  • Spend at least 20 minutes a day learning something new or digging into a topic: InfoQ, DZone Refcardz, the Thoughtworks Technology Radar, looking up unfamiliar buzzwords, or reading a book.

  • Looking up a buzzword promotes it from "don't know you don't know" to "know you don't know".
  • Lunchtime and evenings rarely work: lunch gets eaten by catch-up work, evenings by social and family plans.
  • The authors recommend first thing in the morning, after the coffee or tea and before email: once you check email "your morning is over and your day has started".

Developing a personal radar

One author, as CTO of a small training company in the 1990s, saw the platform (Clipper, a rapid-application development tool for DOS applications on dBASE files) vanish when the business market moved to Windows. A coworker lamented the "enormous body of now-useless Clipper knowledge". The lesson: ignore technology's march at your peril.

  • Heavy investment in one technology creates a memetic bubble, an echo chamber with no warning before it collapses, especially if a vendor created it.
  • The remedy is a technology radar: a living document assessing the risks and rewards of existing and nascent technologies.

The Thoughtworks Technology Radar. The Technology Advisory Board (TAB), a group of senior technology leaders that assists the CTO on technology directions, began producing it (now biannual). It answers the question "How do you keep up with technology?": speakers all have some form of internal radar.

The four quadrants:

  • Tools: from IDEs to enterprise-grade integration tools.
  • Languages and frameworks: typically open source.
  • Techniques: processes, engineering practices and advice.
  • Platforms: databases, cloud vendors, operating systems.

The four rings, outer to inner, with the personal-use meaning the authors suggest:

Ring Thoughtworks meaning Personal use
Hold Originally "hold off for now"; now "don't start anything new with this technology" (fine on existing projects) Technologies and techniques to avoid, and habits to break (for example low-value forum gossip)
Assess Worth exploring (spikes, research, conference sessions) to see the effect on the organisation A staging area for promising technologies you have not yet assessed
Trial Worth pursuing; understand how to build it; pilot a low-risk project Active research, such as spike experiments inside a larger code base, to include in trade-off analysis
Adopt The industry should adopt it What you are most excited about and your best practices
  • Most technologists pick technologies ad hoc ("what's cool or what their employers are using"), which is dangerous to a career.
  • A radar helps balance opposing criteria: a "cooler" technology may be harder to get a job in; an established one has a big market but less interesting work.
  • Treat the technology portfolio like a financial portfolio: diversify. Mix widely demanded skills with gambits such as generative AI or embedded IoT devices.
  • The exercise matters more than the outcome:
    • it forces time to think about this
    • Thoughtworks released Build Your Own Radar, which turns a Google spreadsheet into a radar visualisation.

2.3 Analysing trade-offs

"Architecture is the stuff you can't Google or ask an LLM about." (Mark Richards, as quoted in the book)

  • The answer to every architecture question is "It depends": on deployment environment, business drivers, company culture, budgets, time frames, developer skills and more.
  • "There are no right or wrong answers in architecture, only trade-offs." (Neal Ford, as quoted)

Worked example: an item auction. The Bid Producer service takes a bid from the bidder and sends the amount to Bid Capture, Bid Tracking and Bid Analytics. Asynchronously, it could use queues (point-to-point) or a topic (publish and subscribe).

The obvious win for the topic is extensibility:

  • The producer needs one connection, not three.
  • Adding a Bid History service (a history of each bidder's bids) needs no change to existing services or infrastructure: it subscribes to the topic.
  • With queues, Bid History needs a new queue and the producer must be modified to add a connection.
  • The producer is also less coupled with a topic: it does not know how the bids are used or by whom.

Rich Hickey is quoted: "Programmers know the benefits of everything and the trade-offs of nothing. Architects need to understand both." So the negatives of the topic matter:

  • Data access and security:
    • anyone can access the bids on a topic ("very easy to wiretap a topic"). A queue is read only by its consumer
    • a rogue listener would stop the real service receiving bids, which triggers an immediate loss-of-data notification.
  • Homogeneous contracts only:
    • every subscriber accepts the same data contract
    • If Bid History needs the current asking price, the shared contract changes and affects all services
    • With queues each consumer has its own channel and contract.
  • No monitoring of message counts,
    • so no autoscaling on a topic
    • Queues can be monitored individually and each consumer scaled independently
    • This one is technology specific: AMQP supports programmatic load balancing and monitoring because an exchange (what the producer sends to) is separate from a queue (what the consumer listens to).
Topic advantages Topic disadvantages
Architectural extensibility Data access and data security concerns
Service decoupling No heterogeneous contracts
Monitoring and programmatic scalability

Which is better? It depends. The architect asks "Which is more important: extensibility or security?" and the answer follows the business drivers and environment.

2.4 Understanding business drivers

  • Translate the business drivers for success (scalability, performance, availability and so on) into architecture characteristics.
  • It needs some business-domain knowledge and healthy, collaborative relationships with key stakeholders.
  • Four chapters cover it:

    • Chapter 6: measuring them

2.5 Balancing architecture and hands-on coding

The authors firmly believe every architect should code and keep some technical depth, though it is hard to do.

The Bottleneck Trap antipattern: the architect owns code on the system's critical path (usually underlying framework code or complicated parts). They are not a full-time developer, so they split time with diagrams and meetings and become a bottleneck.

The way out is to delegate the critical parts and code a minor piece of business functionality (a service or UI screen) one to three iterations down the road. Three effects:

  1. The architect gets hands-on experience writing production code without blocking the team.
  2. Critical-path and framework code is spread to the team, giving them ownership and understanding of the harder parts.
  3. Most important: the architect writes the same business code as the team, so they feel the team's pain points in processes, procedures and the development environment (and hopefully fix them).

If the architect cannot code with the team, stay hands-on by:

  • Frequent proofs of concept.
    • Validate a decision with working code
    • Example: stuck between two caching products, build a working example in each and compare scalability, performance and fault tolerance.
    • Write production-quality POC code, because "throwaway" code often lands in the repository and becomes the reference example, and sloppy POCs build bad habits.
  • Tackle technical debt, freeing the team for critical user stories. It is low priority, so missing an iteration is not the end of the world.
  • Fix bugs. Not glamorous, but it reveals weaknesses in the code base and possibly the architecture.
  • Automate. Build command-line tools and analysers for repetitive tasks: source validators for standards that lint tests miss, automated checklists, repetitive refactorings.
    • Architectural analysis and fitness functions (for example ArchUnit on the Java platform) automate compliance (Chapter 6).
  • Do code reviews. They keep the architect involved in the code, check architectural compliance and reveal mentoring opportunities.

2.6 There is more

Architectural thinking also includes understanding the system's overall structure (Chapter 3), translating business concerns into characteristics (Chapters 4 to 7), and seeing the system through its logical components (Chapter 8).

Summary points

  • Architecture is structure, design is appearance; judge a decision by strategy, effort and trade-off significance.
  • Architects need breadth over depth; build it with 20 minutes a day and a personal radar.
  • Always analyse the downsides as well as the benefits: "it depends".
  • Translate business drivers into characteristics.
  • Keep coding, but avoid the Bottleneck Trap.
Terms to carry forward
  • Strategic versus tactical: long-term and multi-stakeholder (architecture) versus short-term and independent (design).
  • Technical depth and breadth: depth is how well you know a topic; breadth is how many topics you know.
  • Knowledge pyramid: what you know, what you know you don't know, what you don't know you don't know.
  • Frozen Caveman antipattern: an architect fixated on a pet concern from past bad experience.
  • 20-minute rule: 20 minutes of learning a day, first thing in the morning.
  • Technology radar: quadrants (tools, languages and frameworks, techniques, platforms) and rings (Hold, Assess, Trial, Adopt).
  • Memetic bubble: an echo chamber around a technology you are heavily invested in.
  • Bottleneck Trap: an architect owning critical-path code and slowing the team.
  • Topic versus queue: publish and subscribe versus point to point messaging.
