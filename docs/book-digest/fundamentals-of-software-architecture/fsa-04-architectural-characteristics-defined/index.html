Fundamentals of Software Architecture 4: Architectural Characteristics Defined

Source: Mark Richards and Neal Ford, Fundamentals of Software Architecture, 2nd ed. (O'Reilly, 2025), ch. 4. Previous: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 3: Modularity. Next: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 5: Identifying Architectural Characteristics.

Takeaways
  • A software solution is the problem domain (what it does) plus architectural characteristics (the capabilities it needs); defining the second is part of what separates architecture from coding and design.
  • A requirement is an architectural characteristic only if it meets three criteria: it specifies a nondomain design consideration, influences some structural aspect of the design, and is critical or important to success.
  • Characteristics are explicit (in requirements) or implicit (assumed, such as availability, reliability and security). Architects must uncover the implicit ones.
  • They fall into operational, structural, cloud and cross-cutting groups, but no universal list exists and many terms are ambiguous.
  • Support as few as possible: each one adds complexity and they trade off against each other. "Never strive for the best architecture; aim for the least worst architecture."

4.1 Structural design and the problem domain

Structural design has two main activities: architectural characteristics analysis (this chapter) and logical component design (Chapter 8). They can run in either order or in parallel, and meet at a critical join point.

  • The company's requirements for the system form the problem domain (or just the domain).
  • Chapter 1 defined characteristics as aspects of a system that are independent of the problem domain and important to its success.
  • Architects collaborate on defining the domain, but also define and analyse everything the software must do that is not directly domain functionality.

The book's figure shows seven concerns side by side: auditability, performance, security, requirements, data, legality and scalability. Requirements and data belong to the domain; the rest are characteristics.

The longevity of the term "non-functional requirements"
  • Many organisations use non-functional requirements, coined to distinguish characteristics from functional requirements.
  • The authors dislike it: it is self-denigrating, so how do you convince teams to pay attention to something "non-functional"?
  • Another popular term, quality attributes, implies after-the-fact quality assessment rather than design.
  • They prefer architectural characteristics: concerns critical to the architecture's success without discounting their importance. In Head First Software Architecture (O'Reilly, 2024) they call them the system's capabilities and the domain the system's behaviour.
  • The old term appeared in software engineering literature in the late 1970s, alongside function point analysis, an estimation technique that decomposes requirements into "function points" (units of work). It gave "a veneer of certainty" but was plagued by subjectivity and is no longer in use.
  • One insight remained: much of the effort of building a system goes to its capabilities, not its requirements. They called these non-function points, hence "non-functional requirements".

4.2 What makes a characteristic architectural

Three criteria, drawn in the book as a triangle:

The triangle is deliberate: each element supports the others, which together support the system's design. The fulcrum shows how characteristics interact, which is why architects say "trade-off" so much.

It specifies a nondomain design consideration

  • Domain design covers the system's behaviour; characteristics define its capabilities. Together they define structural design.
  • Design requirements say what the application should do. Characteristics say how to implement them and why choices were made: the operational and design criteria for success.
  • Examples: specific performance levels are often important but rarely written down. No requirements document says "prevent technical debt", yet it is a common design consideration.
  • Explicit versus implicit is covered in Chapter 5.

It influences some structural aspect of the design

  • Architects describe characteristics to tease out design considerations: can this be done in design, or does it need special structure?
  • Security is a concern in virtually every project, with baseline precautions in design and coding
    • It becomes an architectural characteristic when the architect decides the architecture needs special structure to support it.

Characteristic In a monolith In a distributed architecture such as microservices
Security Good coding hygiene: encryption, hashing, salting (and architectural fitness functions, Chapter 6) A more hardened service with stricter access protocols: a structural approach
Scalability "No amount of clever design" lets a monolith scale beyond a certain point The system must change to a distributed architectural style

Architects watch operational characteristics closely because they most often need special structural support.

It must be critical or important to application success

  • Applications can support a huge number of characteristics, but shouldn't: each one adds design complexity. Choose the fewest possible.
  • Implicit
    • characteristics rarely appear in requirements yet are needed: availability, reliability and security underpin virtually all applications
    • Architects use domain knowledge to uncover them during analysis.
    • Example: a high-frequency trading firm may not specify low latency in every system because architects in that domain already know it is critical.
  • Explicit characteristics appear in requirements documents or other specific instructions.

4.3 Architectural characteristics (partially) listed

Characteristics range from low-level code characteristics (modularity) to sophisticated operational concerns (scalability, elasticity). There is no true universal standard; each organisation interprets terms itself, and the fast-changing ecosystem keeps producing new ones. Architects categorise them.

Operational

They overlap heavily with operations and DevOps concerns.

Term Definition
Availability How much of the time the system must be available; if 24/7, steps are needed to get it back up quickly after failure
Continuity The system's disaster recovery capability
Performance How well it performs: stress testing, peak analysis, frequency of functions used, response times
Recoverability Business continuity: how quickly it must be back online after a disaster, including backup strategies and duplicate hardware
Reliability/safety Whether it must be fail-safe or mission critical in a way that affects lives; whether failure costs large sums. Often a spectrum, not binary
Robustness Handling error and boundary conditions while running, for example loss of internet or power
Scalability Ability to perform and operate as users or requests increase

Structural

Architects often have sole or shared responsibility for code quality: modularity, readability, control of coupling between components.

Term Definition
Configurability How easily end users can change configuration through interfaces
Extensibility How well the architecture accommodates changes that extend its functionality
Installability How easy it is to install on all necessary platforms
Leverageability/reuse How far common components can be used across multiple products
Localization Support for multiple languages on entry and query screens and in data fields
Maintainability How easy it is to apply changes and enhance the system
Portability Ability to run on more than one platform (such as Oracle and SAP DB)
Upgradeability How easy and quick it is to upgrade to a newer version on servers and clients

Cloud

When the first edition was published, cloud computing existed but was not pervasive; now most systems touch the cloud. Each architecture style chapter in this edition has a section on how the style handles cloud considerations.

Term Definition
On-demand scalability The provider's ability to scale resources dynamically with demand
On-demand elasticity The provider's flexibility when demand spikes; similar to scalability
Zone-based availability Separating resources by computing zones for more resilient systems
Region-based privacy and security The provider's legal ability to store data from various countries; many countries restrict storing citizens' data outside the region

Cross-cutting

These fall outside the neat categories but are still important design constraints.

Term Definition
Accessibility How easily all users can access the system, including those with disabilities such as colourblindness or hearing loss
Archivability Constraints on archiving or deleting data after a set period
Authentication Ensuring users are who they say they are
Authorization Ensuring users can access only certain functions (by use case, subsystem, page, business rule, field)
Legal Legislative constraints, such as data protection laws like GDPR or financial-records laws like Sarbanes-Oxley (US), and rules on how the application is built or deployed
Privacy Encrypting and hiding transactions from internal employees, even DBAs and network architects
Security Rules on database encryption, network communication between internal systems, remote user authentication and other measures
Supportability The technical support the application needs, including logging and other facilities to debug errors
Usability/achievability The training users need to achieve their goals

Ambiguity and overlap

  • Any list is incomplete, and a project may invent its own characteristics.
  • Many terms are imprecise. Interoperability implies ease of integration, hence published, documented APIs; compatibility is more about industry and domain standards.
  • Learnability has two definitions: how easy it is for users to learn the software, or the level at which the system can learn about its environment to self-configure or self-optimise using machine learning.
  • Definitions overlap: availability and reliability. IP (underlying TCP) is available but not reliable: packets may arrive out of order and the receiver may have to ask again.

The ISO list

The International Organization for Standardization publishes a capability-based list that overlaps with the above but is an incomplete category list. The authors reword it:

  • Performance efficiency: performance relative to resources used under known conditions. Includes:
    • time behaviour (response, processing times, throughput)
    • resource utilisation
    • capacity (how far the maximum established limits are exceeded)
  • Compatibility: exchanging information and performing required functions while sharing hardware or software. Includes:
    • coexistence
    • interoperability
  • Usability: effective, efficient and satisfactory use. Includes:
    • appropriateness recognisability
    • learnability
    • user error protection
    • accessibility
  • Reliability: functioning under specified conditions for a specified period. Includes:
    • maturity
    • availability
    • fault tolerance
    • recoverability
  • Security: protecting information so access matches authorisation. Includes:
    • confidentiality
    • integrity
    • nonrepudiation (can events be proven to have happened)
    • accountability (can user actions be traced)
    • authenticity
  • Maintainability: how effectively developers can modify the software to improve, correct or adapt it. Includes:
    • modularity
    • reusability
    • analysability
    • modifiability
    • testability
  • Portability: transferring a system between environments. Includes:
    • adaptability
    • installability
    • replaceability
  • Functional suitability:
    • whether functions meet stated and implied needs
    • Includes functional completeness, correctness and appropriateness
    • The authors disagree that it belongs: it describes the motivational requirements for building the software, not architectural characteristics (Chapter 7 covers how this thinking evolved).
The many ambiguities in software architecture
  • There is no clear definition for many critical things, including architecture itself, so companies define their own terms, leading to industry-wide confusion (opaque terms, or the same term for very different things).
  • The authors cannot impose a standard nomenclature. Domain-driven design's advice is to establish and use a ubiquitous language across the organisation; they follow and recommend it.

4.4 Trade-offs and least worst architecture

Systems can support only a few characteristics, for four reasons:

  1. Support is rarely free: each one needs design effort from the architect, implementation and maintenance effort from developers, and perhaps structural support.
  2. Characteristics are synergistic with each other and the domain. Example: better security almost certainly hurts performance, because of on-the-fly encryption, indirection for hiding secrets and other work.
  3. No standard definitions, so organisations struggle with ambiguity. Each can build its own list (a ubiquitous language) with objective definitions.
  4. The number and categories keep growing. Decades ago architects ignored operations as a separate "black box"; with microservices, architects and operations must collaborate often, and architecture entangles with other parts of the organisation.

The helicopter analogy: pilots learning to fly helicopters struggle because there are controls for each hand and foot and changing one affects all the others. Choosing characteristics is similarly a balancing exercise.

Never strive for the best architecture; aim for the least worst architecture.
  • Trying to support too many characteristics leads to generic solutions that attempt to solve every business problem; the design becomes unwieldy and such architectures rarely work.
  • Design the architecture to be as iterative as possible
    • The easier it is to change, the less anyone needs to stress over finding the exactly correct thing first time
    • Iteration is one of the key lessons of Agile development and holds at every level.

Summary points

  • Software solution = domain (behaviour) + characteristics (capabilities).
  • Three criteria: nondomain, structural influence, critical to success; explicit or implicit.
  • Four groups of characteristics, an incomplete ISO list, and ambiguity everywhere; build a ubiquitous language.
  • Fewest characteristics; trade-offs; least worst architecture; iterate.
Terms to carry forward
  • Problem domain: the requirements for the system.
  • Architectural characteristic: a nondomain, structurally influential, success-critical capability (also called "-ilities"; avoid "non-functional requirements").
  • Explicit versus implicit characteristics.
  • Function point analysis and non-function points: the 1970s origin of "non-functional".
  • Operational, structural, cloud and cross-cutting groups.
  • ISO capability list: performance efficiency, compatibility, usability, reliability, security, maintainability, portability, functional suitability.
  • Ubiquitous language: shared, objectively defined terms (from DDD).
  • Least worst architecture.
