Fundamentals of Software Architecture 5: Identifying Architectural Characteristics

Source: Mark Richards and Neal Ford, Fundamentals of Software Architecture, 2nd ed. (O'Reilly, 2025), ch. 5. Previous: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 4: Architectural Characteristics Defined. Next: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 6: Measuring and Governing Architecture Characteristics.

Takeaways
  • Characteristics come from three places: the domain concerns, the project requirements and the architect's implicit domain knowledge.
  • Stakeholders and architects speak different languages, so architects translate domain concerns (time to market, user satisfaction) into "-ilities".
  • Composite characteristics such as agility have no single measure; decompose them and do not focus on just one part.
  • Work through a kata (for example Silicon Sandwiches): split candidates into explicit and implicit, then ask whether each is a design or an architecture matter.
  • Limit and prioritise: keep the list short, try to drop the least important, and have stakeholders pick the top three rather than a full ranking.

5.1 Where characteristics come from

An architect analyses two things to create or validate an architecture: characteristics and domain. There are at least three places to uncover characteristics:

  • the domain concerns
  • the project requirements
  • your implicit domain knowledge

Some domains carry their own implicit characteristics beyond generic ones like security and modularity. Example: an architect on medical software that reads diagnostic equipment already understands data integrity and the cost of lost messages; it becomes implicit in every solution they design.

5.2 Extracting characteristics from domain concerns

Most characteristics come from listening to key domain stakeholders and working out what matters from a business view. The catch is a lost-in-translation problem:

  • Architects talk about scalability, interoperability, fault tolerance, learnability and availability.
  • Stakeholders talk about mergers and acquisitions, user satisfaction, time to market and competitive advantage.
  • Neither side knows how to act on the other's words: architects cannot "build user satisfaction", stakeholders do not see why availability matters so much.

Table 5-1 translates the common domain concerns:

Domain concern Architectural characteristics
Mergers and acquisitions Interoperability, scalability, adaptability, extensibility
Time to market Agility, testability, deployability
User satisfaction Performance, availability, fault tolerance, testability, deployability, agility, security
Competitive advantage Agility, testability, deployability, scalability, availability, fault tolerance
Time and budget Simplicity, feasibility

Understanding the key goals turns domain concerns into "-ilities", the basis for correct and justifiable decisions. Example question: is time to market more important than scalability, or should the focus be fault tolerance, security or performance? Perhaps all of them together.

5.3 Composite characteristics

A common pitfall is a false equivalence, such as equating agility only with time to market. Agility is a composite architectural characteristic: it has no single objective definition and is composed of other measurable things.

  • There is no measure for agility, so ask what it is made of: deployability, modularity and testability, all measurable.
  • A common antipattern is focusing narrowly on one part of a composite, "like forgetting to put the flour in the cake batter".

Example. A stakeholder says: "Due to regulatory requirements, it is absolutely imperative that we complete end-of-day fund pricing on time." Focusing only on performance fails, because:

  • Speed does not matter if the system is not available when needed.
  • As more funds are created, it must scale to finish on time.
  • It must be reliable so it does not crash mid-calculation.
  • If it crashes when pricing is 85% complete, it must recover and restart where it left off.
  • A fast system must still calculate the fund prices correctly (auditability).

Many business goals start as composites. Decomposing them and giving the parts objective definitions is the architect's job (see Chapter 6).

5.4 Extracting characteristics from requirements

  • Most come from explicit statements in a requirements document: lists of domain concerns often include explicit numbers of users and scale.
  • Others come from the architect's inherent domain knowledge, one reason every architect should know their domain.

Example: university class registration. Assume 1,000 students and 10 hours for registration. Do you assume students spread evenly over the 10 hours, or design for all 1,000 trying to register in the last 10 minutes? Anyone who knows how students procrastinate knows the answer, yet such details rarely appear in requirements.

Sidebar: the origin of architecture katas

  • Ted Neward,
    • a well-known architect, devised architecture katas over a decade ago so aspiring architects can practise deriving characteristics from domain-targeted descriptions
    • Kata is the Japanese word for an individual martial arts training exercise that stresses proper form and technique.
  • Fred Brooks is quoted: "How do we get great designers? Great designers design, of course."
  • Big projects take time, and architects design perhaps half a dozen systems in a career, so katas give practice
    • Neal Ford and Mark Richards adapted Ted's site on the book's companion site, fundamentalsofsoftwarearchitecture.com.

Working with katas

Each kata gives a problem in domain terms, requirements and extra context. Small teams design (a characteristics analysis plus diagrams) for a set time, share results and vote on the best architecture.

Section Contents
Description The overall domain problem
Users The expected number and types of users
Requirements Domain requirements from users and experts
Additional context Added by the authors: considerations not in the requirements that may still influence design, making it more realistic

Anyone can host a brown-bag lunch; an experienced architect can critique the trade-offs afterwards. Designs will not be elaborate because the exercise is timeboxed.

5.5 Kata: Silicon Sandwiches

Description. A national sandwich shop wants online ordering in addition to its call-in service. Users: thousands, perhaps one day millions.

Requirements

  • Place an order and, if the shop delivers, choose pickup or delivery.
  • Give pickup customers a pickup time and directions to the shop (integrating with several external mapping services that include traffic information).
  • For delivery, dispatch the driver with the order.
  • Provide mobile device accessibility.
  • Offer national daily promotions and specials.
  • Offer local daily promotions and specials.
  • Accept payment online, at the shop or on delivery.

Additional context

  • Shops are franchised, each with a different owner.
  • The parent company plans to expand overseas in the near future.
  • The corporate goal is to hire inexpensive labour to maximise profit.

The architect does not design the whole system; look for things that influence or impact the design, particularly structurally. First separate candidates into explicit and implicit.

Explicit characteristics

The Users section. Thousands, maybe millions of users makes scalability (handling a large number of concurrent users without serious performance degradation) a top characteristic. The problem statement never asked for scalability, only gave expected user numbers: architects decode domain language into engineering equivalents.

Elasticity (handling bursts of requests) is also likely. The two are often lumped together but have different constraints:

  • Some systems are scalable but not elastic. A hotel reservation system's users, absent special sales or events, are probably predictably seasonal.
  • A concert-ticket booking system gets floods of fans when tickets go on sale and needs high elasticity.
  • Often elastic systems also need scalability.
  • Elasticity is not in the Silicon Sandwiches requirements, but a sandwich shop almost certainly gets mealtime bursts, so identify it.

Requirement by requirement

Requirement Characteristic implication
Choose pickup or delivery None special
Pickup time and directions via external mapping services Integration points affect reliability: if the calls fail, the caller's reliability suffers. But beware overspecifying: if the traffic service is down, should the whole site fail, or just lose some efficiency? Guard against building in brittleness
Dispatch the driver None special
Mobile accessibility Mainly UX. Given budget and simplicity, a mobile-optimised web application beats several native apps, so define performance characteristics for page load and other mobile-sensitive things. Collaborate with UX designers and stakeholders: the business may need behaviour only native apps offer
National and local promotions Customisability (and customised traffic information by user address). The microkernel style (Chapter 13) supports it well via plug-ins: default behaviour in the core, location-based parts as plug-ins. A traditional design can use patterns such as Template Method
Payment online, at the shop or on delivery Online payments imply security, but nothing beyond what is implicit; design or architecture can handle it, so a minimal concern here
Franchised shops May impose cost restrictions; check feasibility (cost, time, staff skills) to see whether a simple or sacrificial architecture is warranted
Overseas expansion Internationalisation (i18n): many design techniques exist and it should not need special structure, though it drives UX decisions
Inexpensive labour Usability, but more a design than an architecture concern

A third derived characteristic is performance: nobody buys from a poor-performing shop, especially at peak. It is nuanced (Chapter 6). Define performance numbers together with scalability numbers: set a baseline, then an acceptable level at a given number of users. Characteristics often interact and must be defined relative to each other.

Implicit characteristics

  • Availability: users can access the site. Closely related is stability: the site stays up during interactions, since nobody wants to log in again after a dropped connection.
  • Security
    • appears in every system, but its priority varies
    • it is architectural only when it influences some structure and is critical
      • For Silicon Sandwiches, assume a third party handles payments, so general hygiene (no plain-text card numbers, not storing too much) in good design is enough and no special structure is needed.
  • Characteristics are synergistic, so overspecifying is as damaging as underspecifying: it overcomplicates the design.
  • Customisability
    • is composed of several details: recipes, local sales, locally overridden directions
    • Normally application design, it becomes an architectural characteristic when the domain relies on custom structure
    • Here it is not critical to success.

"There are no wrong answers in architecture, only expensive ones." (Mark Richards)

Design versus architecture and trade-offs

Is customisability architecture or design? Architecture implies a structural component; design lives inside the architecture.

  • Microkernel builds structural support; another style plus the Template Method pattern (parent classes define workflows that child classes override) does it in design.
  • It depends. Ask: are there good reasons not to use a microkernel (performance, coupling)? Are other desirable characteristics harder in one option? How much does supporting everything cost in the architecture versus the design?
  • Always collaborate with developers, the project manager and operations. No decision should be made in isolation from the implementation team; that is the Ivory Tower antipattern. Here the architect, tech lead, developers and domain analysts decide together.
  • Do not stress over the exactly correct set: identifying the important structural elements simply makes a simpler or more elegant design possible. "There is no best design in architecture, only a least worst collection of trade-offs."

Prioritise. Once the first pass is done, ask which characteristic is the least important.

  • Architects usually cull explicit ones first, because many implicit ones support general success.
  • For Silicon Sandwiches the candidate to lose is customisability (handle it in application design) or performance
    • Performance is probably the least critical operational characteristic, which does not mean building something slow
    • it means performance is not prioritised over scalability or availability.

5.6 Limiting and prioritising characteristics

  • Work hard to keep the final list as short as possible
    • A common antipattern is a generic architecture that supports every characteristic; each one complicates the design before the domain problem is even addressed
    • Do not obsess over the count, but remember the motivation: simplicity.
Case study: the Vasa
  • A Swedish warship built between 1626 and 1628 for a king who wanted the most magnificent ship ever.
  • Until then ships were troop transports or gunships; the Vasa would be both. It had two decks (most had one) and cannons twice the usual size. The expert shipbuilders could not say no to King Adolphus.
  • After construction it sailed into Stockholm harbour and fired a cannon salute from one side. Being top-heavy, it capsized and sank.
  • Salvaged in 1961; it now sits in a museum in Stockholm.
  • The lesson drawn: overspecifying characteristics can kill a project.

If the architect hands stakeholders a long list and asks which they want, the answer is always "all of them". The authors' technique is a worksheet:

  • Used in an interactive session run by the architect, who asks stakeholders for the number and details of the characteristics
    • (Downloadable at https://developertoarchitect.com/resources.html.)
  • Left: seven slots for the driving characteristics. Seven is arbitrary: six or eight would work, though "there is some interesting psychology behind the number seven".
  • Second column:
    • implicit characteristics (the figure lists feasibility (cost/time), security, maintainability and observability). They appear in most systems
    • when one is a driving concern needing special design, the architect pulls it into the first column.
  • If the first column is full and a better item arrives, the displaced one goes to Others considered.
  • Last step: collaboratively choose the top three in any order and tick the box beside each. The shortened, prioritised list drives decisions and trade-off analysis.

Trying to agree a full ranked list is "a fool's errand": it wastes time and breeds frustration, since rarely will all stakeholders agree on every priority. Asking them to pick the top three (in any order) gains consensus much more easily and sparks discussion of what matters most.

Summary points

  • Three sources: domain concerns, requirements, implicit knowledge.
  • Translate stakeholder language into "-ilities"; decompose composites such as agility.
  • Katas practise this; split explicit and implicit; decide design versus architecture with trade-off analysis and the implementation team.
  • Keep the list short; drop the least important; stakeholders pick the top three.
Terms to carry forward
  • Composite characteristic: one with no single objective definition, made of measurable parts (agility).
  • Scalability versus elasticity: growth of users over time versus bursts.
  • Architecture kata: a timeboxed practice exercise (Ted Neward): description, users, requirements, additional context.
  • Explicit versus implicit characteristics.
  • Overspecifying: adding unnecessary characteristics and complexity.
  • Ivory Tower antipattern: deciding architecture without the implementation team.
  • Template Method: a design pattern in which a parent class defines a workflow that children override.
  • Driving characteristics worksheet and top three.
