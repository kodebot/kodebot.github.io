Fundamentals of Software Architecture 17: Orchestration-Driven Service-Oriented Architecture

Source: Mark Richards and Neal Ford, Fundamentals of Software Architecture, 2nd edition (O'Reilly, 2025), chapter 17. Previous: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 16: Space-Based Architecture Style. Next: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 18: Microservices Architecture.

Takeaways
  • Orchestration-driven SOA (late 1990s) is a distributed style built on a strict service taxonomy and enterprise-level reuse. The authors call it mostly a historical lesson in how a logical organisational idea can hinder development.
  • Layers: business, enterprise, application and infrastructure services, tied together by an orchestration engine and message bus (an ESB).
  • Reuse is implemented via coupling: a canonical Customer service ripples every change to every division and drags in details most callers do not need.
  • Extreme technical partitioning spreads one domain concept such as CatalogCheckout across dozens of services; the style is a single quantum, with the disadvantages of both monolithic and distributed designs.
  • Today the ESB is still used for integration; beware Accidental SOA. Cost $$$$; only scalability (4) and modularity (4) rate well.

17.1 Context: why this style existed

The authors compare architecture styles to art movements: they make sense in their era and then lose relevance. SOA appeared in the late 1990s as small companies grew into enterprises through mergers and needed more sophisticated IT.

  • Scarce, commercial resources:
    • operating systems were licensed per machine (open source was not yet trusted for serious work), database servers had Byzantine licensing, and application-server vendors fought database vendors over connection pooling
    • Since resources were expensive at scale, architects adopted a philosophy of reusing as much as possible.
  • Organisational concerns:
    • mergers and growth left duplicated information and workflows and inconsistent core business entities, so SOA's goals were attractive
    • Reuse in all forms became the dominant philosophy.
  • It shows how far technical partitioning can be pushed: good motivations, bad consequences at the extreme.
Why so many "service" names?

17.2 Topology and taxonomy

Not every example has these exact layers, but all establish a taxonomy of services with well-defined responsibilities per layer. The exact boundaries vary by organisation and tools (some parts may sit inside an application server).

Layer What it is Example
Business services Top-level entry points for business processes: no code, only input, output and sometimes schema, defined by business users or analysts ExecuteTrade, PlaceOrder
Enterprise services Fine-grained shared implementations: atomic behaviour around business domains and transactional entities; the building blocks of business services, tied together by the orchestration engine CreateCustomer, CalculateQuote; entities Customer, Order, Lineitem
Application services One-off, single-implementation services owned by one application team, not worth making reusable a geolocation service for one app
Infrastructure services Operational concerns (monitoring, logging, authentication, authorisation): concrete implementations owned by a shared infrastructure team working with operations logging, auth
Orchestration engine and message bus The heart: stitches business services together with orchestration, including transactional coordination and message transformation; also an integration hub for custom code, packages and legacy systems an ESB
  • Litmus test for a business service:
    • can the architect answer yes to "Are we in the business of...?" PlaceOrder passes
    • CreateCustomer fails: the company needs to create customers to execute trades but is not in the business of creating customers.
  • The elusive granularity.
    • The goal for enterprise services was perfectly encapsulated blocks of isolated business functionality, freely composed into workflows, so the business would never rewrite that behaviour: a growing collection of reusable assets, in theory
    • The authors say the sweet spot is elusive at best and likely impossible
    • Business components are not construction materials whose solutions last decades; markets, technology and engineering practice keep changing.
  • ESBs today.
    • The authors consider building a whole architecture around an ESB a bad idea, but ESBs are immensely useful in integration-heavy environments: if you need both an integration hub and an orchestration engine, use a tool that bundles them
    • (Learn to discern the true uses of tools, apart from the hype, good and bad.)
  • Conway's Law: the integration architects who own the message bus become a political force, then a bureaucratic bottleneck.
  • Transactions.
    • Offloading transaction behaviour to the engine sounds good, but architects struggle to find the right granularity
    • a few services in a distributed transaction is fine, but complexity grows because entities appear in many workflows and developers must work out the transaction boundaries.

17.4 Reuse...and coupling

Early architects were told to find reuse opportunities as aggressively as possible. Example: an architect sees that each of six divisions of an insurance company has a notion of Customer (Figure 17-3). The "proper" SOA response extracts Customer into one canonical Customer service that the other services reference (Figure 17-4).

The negative trade-offs arrived slowly:

  • Reuse is implemented via coupling. A change to the Customer service ripples out to every other service, making even incremental change risky and forcing coordinated deployments and holistic testing.
  • Everyone inherits everyone's details.
    • Auto insurance needs a driver's licence (a property of the person), so the shared Customer must carry licence details that disability insurance does not care about, yet that team must handle the extra complexity
    • DDD's insistence on avoiding holistic reuse derives from experiences like this.
  • The nightmare of technical partitioning.
    • A task like "add a new address line to CatalogCheckout" touches dozens of services across several tiers plus the single database schema
    • the domain concept is "ground to dust"
      • If an enterprise service has the wrong transactional granularity, developers must change the design or build a near-identical new service: "so much for reuse".

17.5 Data topologies

Not very interesting: usually one or a few relational databases, the common practice of the era. Even transactionality was pushed from the database to the bus, which often offered declarative transactions per entity. Architects of the time treated data as a "foreign country", an integration point rather than part of the problem domain.

Declarative transactions largely failed

  1. Not knowing the runtime transactional behaviour adds complexity, so developers made almost identical copies of entities differing only in transaction scope.
  2. Whatever vendors built into buses, edge cases and failure modes left tangled inconsistency for humans. Some complex features (transactions) cannot be cleanly abstracted; too many leaks.

17.6 Cloud, risks, governance, teams

  • Cloud:
    • the style predates the cloud by decades, so there was no original cloud design
    • Today it is a good integration architecture for cloud and on-prem services that must participate in workflows.
  • Original risks:
    • cost, implementation time (multiyear endeavours with decisions high in the hierarchy) and surprising difficulty to maintain
    • Rather than call them failures, companies mostly turned them into integration architectures with better boundaries aligned with DDD.
  • Accidental SOA:
    • when an ESB is used for integration, the slippery slope is letting it gradually encapsulate the whole architecture, building a full orchestration-driven SOA unintentionally
    • Avoid it with reasonable encapsulation boundaries for orchestration and attention to transactional boundaries.
  • Governance:
    • testing outside formal QA was uncommon, mock frameworks for buses were cumbersome and inconsistent, and "governance" meant heavyweight frameworks, meetings and manual code reviews
    • Today, fitness functions can stop data and bounded contexts leaking across an ESB.
  • Fitness function example.
    • An ESB links an ERP package, an online sales tool and modern Accounting microservices
    • the system may only read from ERP and sales and write to Accounting. First ensure all communication is logged consistently, then:
READ logs for ERP into ERP-logs for past 24 hours
READ logs for Sales into Sales-logs for past 24 hours
FOREACH entry IN ERP-logs
    IF 'operation' is 'update' and 'target' != 'accounting' THEN
        raise fitness function violation
            "Invalid communication between integration points"
    END IF
FOREACH entry IN Sales-logs
    IF 'operation' is 'update' and 'target' != 'accounting' THEN
        raise fitness function violation
            "Invalid communication between integration points"
    END IF
  • Teams:
    • team topologies was unknown then
      • The strict taxonomy is a communication antipattern that helped inspire the team-topologies principles: someone building business services rarely spoke to someone building enterprise services
    • they communicated through contracts and interfaces and enterprise ticketing tools, so building features was slow.

17.7 Style characteristics

Agile had only just started and had not reached the large organisations that used this style, so many modern criteria were not priorities.

  • It is perhaps the most technically partitioned general-purpose architecture ever attempted; the backlash led to microservices.
  • One quantum,
    • although distributed, for two reasons: it uses a single or few databases (coupling points), and, more importantly, the orchestration engine is a giant coupling point: no part can have different characteristics than the mediator that orchestrates everything
    • It finds the disadvantages of both monolithic and distributed architectures.
  • Deployability and testability score disastrously: poorly supported, and not goals of the era.
  • Elasticity and scalability
    • are supported despite difficulty: vendors poured effort into session replication across application servers
    • Performance was never a highlight, because each business request is split across so much of the architecture.
  • Simplicity and cost
    • have the inverse of the relationship architects would prefer
    • Lessons: the practical limits of technical partitioning, and how hard real-world distributed transactions are.

17.8 Examples and use cases

Late 1990s and early 2000s large enterprises, since displaced by domain-based distributed styles such as microservices.

  • Change is a lottery.
    • On a lucky day, an entity change touches only the enterprise services layer
    • on a bad day (an unanticipated change), four or five layers change, highly coupled in each. Architects dread the word change, because the analysis is deep and the scope variable.
  • Modern relevance.
    • An ESB's integration hub (protocol and contract transformation) and orchestration engine (workflows across endpoints) still suit integration architectures
      • The many layers of indirection let enterprise services be implemented as integration points, package software or bespoke code (Figure 17-6): client requests use the bus to decide which enterprise services to call, in what order and what to aggregate
    • the services reach custom code, old systems or packages through APIs.
  • Since most organisations then lacked open source operating systems, alternatives such as microservices were impossibly expensive
    • Learn from past approaches: keep what still makes sense and internalise what failed and why.

Summary points

  • A taxonomy of services plus a central orchestration engine, aimed at enterprise reuse.
  • Reuse is coupling: shared canonical entities spread change and carry everyone's details.
  • Technical partitioning scatters domain concepts; one quantum, poor evolvability and deployability.
  • Keep the ESB for integration only; guard against Accidental SOA with encapsulation and fitness functions.
Terms to carry forward
  • Business, enterprise, application, infrastructure services.
  • Orchestration engine; ESB; integration hub.
  • Canonical service; reuse via coupling.
  • Declarative transactions; semantic diffusion; Accidental SOA.
