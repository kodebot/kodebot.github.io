Fundamentals of Software Architecture 23: Diagramming Architecture

Source: Mark Richards and Neal Ford, Fundamentals of Software Architecture, 2nd edition (O'Reilly, 2025), chapter 23. Previous: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 22: Analyzing Architecture Risk. Next: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 24: Making Teams Effective.

Takeaways
  • Communication is critical for architects: if you cannot convince managers to fund an idea and developers to build it, the brilliance never manifests. Diagramming is a core communication skill.
  • Representational consistency: always show how a part relates to the whole before changing views.
  • Start low-fidelity; avoid Irrational Artifact Attachment. Look for layers, stencils and magnets in a tool, and use layers semantically.
  • Standards: UML (largely faded except class and sequence), C4 (Context, Container, Component, Class), ArchiMate. Build your own consistent style.
  • Guidelines: titles, thick lines (solid = synchronous, dotted = asynchronous), shapes, labels, colour plus iconography, and keys.

23.1 Representational consistency

To describe an architecture, show different views: an overview of the whole topology, then a drill-down into one part. Showing a portion without its place in the whole confuses viewers.

Representational consistency is the practice of always showing the relationships between parts of an architecture before changing views, in diagrams and in presentations.

Example: to show how the plug-ins of the Silicon Sandwiches solution relate to one another, start with the whole topology, then the relationship between it and the plug-in structure, and only then the plug-in structure itself (Figure 23-1).

Use it carefully so viewers understand the scope of what is shown.

23.2 Diagramming tools

The topology of an architecture always interests architects and developers: it shows how the structure fits and gives teams a shared understanding. Learn your tool of choice deeply, but do not neglect low-fidelity artifacts, especially early. Ephemeral artifacts keep architects from becoming overly attached.

[!warning] Irrational Artifact Attachment A person's irrational attachment to an artifact is proportional to how long it took to produce. Four hours on a beautiful Visio diagram means more attachment than two hours. Agile favours "just-in-time" artifacts with little ceremony (index cards, sticky notes): low-tech lets people throw away what is not right and let the artifact's true nature emerge through revision, collaboration and discussion.

  • The classic ephemeral artifact is a cell-phone photo of a whiteboard diagram ("Do Not Erase!"). Many architects now use a tablet on an overhead projector:
    1. An unlimited canvas.
    2. Easy copy and paste of "what if" scenarios without obscuring the original.
    3. Already digitised, with no whiteboard glare.
    4. Electronic images make remote work easier and more collaborative.
  • Eventually you need nice diagrams in a fancy tool, but only after the team has iterated on the design
    • The authors used OmniGraffle for the original diagrams in the book, which O'Reilly illustrators then refined.
  • Baseline features to look for:
Feature Why
Layers Link groups of items logically and show or hide them: a comprehensive diagram for a presentation with overwhelming details hidden until needed; also incremental builds
Stencils and templates A library of common visual components, including composites (the book's standard microservice icon is one stencil item); shared stencils give consistency and speed
Magnets The points where lines snap onto shapes, giving alignment; some tools let you add or create your own
Use layers semantically, not decoratively
  • The base layer is the architecture's topology: containers, databases, dependencies, brokers and other core elements, focused on architecture rather than implementation (say "synchronous communication", not a specific protocol).
  • The next layer holds implementation details: the type of database, the communication protocols.
  • Layered this way, diagrams are extensible: add layers for DDD boundaries, transactional scope or any other meta-information to contrast against the topology.

The tool should also support lines, colours, shapes and other basics and export to many formats.

23.3 Diagramming standards: UML, C4 and ArchiMate

Standard Origin and character
UML Grady Booch, Ivar Jacobson and Jim Rumbaugh created the Unified Modeling Language in the 1980s to unify their competing design philosophies, "the best of all worlds". Like many committee designs it failed to create much impact outside organisations that mandated it. Class and sequence diagrams are still used for structure and workflow; most other UML types have fallen into disuse
C4 Developed by Simon Brown between 2006 and 2011 to address UML's deficiencies and modernise the approach. A good alternative for any company that wants to standardise: an active, large community, kept up with ecosystem changes, template support in many tools, and standards for components, lines, containers, databases and other artifacts
ArchiMate An open source enterprise-architecture modelling language from The Open Group (a portmanteau of architecture and animate) for describing, analysing and visualising architectures within and across business domains. Lighter weight: its goal is "as small as possible", not covering every edge case. Popular among architects

The four Cs of C4:

C View
Context The whole system context, including user roles and external dependencies
Container Physical (often also logical) deployment boundaries and containers: a good meeting point for operations teams and architects
Component The component view, which most neatly aligns with the architect's view
Class The same class diagrams as UML, which are effective, so no replacement

Figure 23-2 is a C4 diagram of the Silicon Sandwiches modular monolith data design: components (Purchase, Promotion, Deliver, MakeOrder, Recipes, Location, ManageInventory and Overrides) marked as [component] with a database below and a key explaining what the notation means.

23.4 Diagram guidelines

Build your own style, whether a custom language or a formal one; borrow from effective representations.

Element Guideline
Titles Title every element unless very well known to the audience. Use rotation and other effects to make titles "stick" to the right thing and use space well
Lines Thick enough to see. For information flow use arrows for direction or two-way traffic; arrowhead types may carry different semantics if consistent. One of the few general standards: solid lines almost always mean synchronous communication; dotted lines mean asynchronous
Shapes No pervasive standard shapes across the industry; most architects make their own set, sometimes adopted organisation-wide. The authors use 3D boxes for deployable artifacts, rectangles for containers, cylinders for databases, with no particular key beyond that
Labels Label every item, especially where ambiguity is possible
Colour Architects often don't use enough. Books were long printed in black and white, so monochrome habits formed. The authors still favour monochrome but use colour where it distinguishes artifacts: for example shades of gray for the quanta in the GGG microservices figure (Figure 23-3)
Keys If shapes are ambiguous, add a key. An easily misinterpreted diagram is worse than no diagram at all

Do not rely on colour alone for critical differences: colour-blind people and those with other visual disabilities cannot see them. Add **unique iconography**, as street-crossing signals do (green and red, plus distinct walking and standing figures).

23.5 Summary

  • Standards give an organisation consistent communication, but architects often break the rules when the standard has no good way to represent the design
    • Establish standards, but allow reasonable exceptions.
  • In the days of heavyweight CASE tools, architects built elaborate models of simple things and included many useless details that were noise.
  • The authors favour lightweight diagramming tools and quick-and-dirty artifacts, especially early in design: "just don't become so enamored with your creations that you lose your objectivity".

Summary points

  • Show context before detail (representational consistency).
  • Sketch first, polish last; irrational attachment grows with time invested.
  • Use layers for topology then implementation; pick UML, C4 or ArchiMate, or your own consistent style.
  • Solid is synchronous, dotted is asynchronous; label everything; colour plus icons; add keys.
Terms to carry forward
  • Representational consistency; Irrational Artifact Attachment.
  • Layers, stencils, magnets.
  • UML; C4 (Context, Container, Component, Class); ArchiMate.
  • Solid versus dotted lines.
