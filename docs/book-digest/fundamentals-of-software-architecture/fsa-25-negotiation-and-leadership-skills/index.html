Fundamentals of Software Architecture 25: Negotiation and Leadership Skills

Source: Mark Richards and Neal Ford, Fundamentals of Software Architecture, 2nd edition (O'Reilly, 2025), chapter 25. Previous: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 24: Making Teams Effective. Next: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 26: Architectural Intersections.

Takeaways
  • Almost every architectural decision will be challenged (by developers, other architects, stakeholders), so negotiation is one of an architect's most important skills; about 50% of the job is people skills.
  • Stakeholders: decode buzzwords, gather information, restate "nines" as downtime, divide and conquer, and raise cost and time last.
  • Other architects: demonstration defeats discussion; stay calm; stop if it gets personal.
  • Developers: justify before demanding, avoid the Ivory Tower, let them arrive at the answer themselves.
  • Leadership: the 4 Cs (communication, collaboration, clear, concise), avoid accidental complexity, balance pragmatic and visionary, lead by example, turn requests into favours.
  • Time: control meetings, protect developer flow, sit with the team.

For more depth the authors recommend Tanya Reilly, The Staff Engineer's Path (O'Reilly, 2022), and Fisher, Ury and Patton, Getting to Yes (Penguin, 2011). These skills take years of learning, practice and mistakes.

25.1 Negotiation and facilitation

The last expectation in Chapter 1 was navigating office politics, because almost every decision will be challenged: by developers who think they know more, other architects with a better idea, and stakeholders who think it is too expensive or slow. Effective architects understand the organisation's politics, negotiate and facilitate, and overcome disagreement to reach solutions all stakeholders agree on.

Negotiating with business stakeholders (Scenario 1)

Parker, a senior vice president and product sponsor, insists the new global trading system must have five nines (99.999%) availability. You know trading pauses for two hours between global markets, so three nines (99.9%) suffices.

Parker dislikes being wrong, hates condescension, isn't technical but thinks they are, and gets involved in non-functional aspects. You must convince them without being egotistical, and without missing something that proves you wrong.

Technique Detail
Read the buzzwords Corporate jargon is generally meaningless but holds clues. "I needed it yesterday" means time to market matters; "lightning fast" means performance; "zero downtime" means availability is critical
Gather information first Parker may not know what five nines means in downtime
Translate "nines" into time Hours, minutes and seconds bring real metrics into the discussion. Validate the concern first ("availability is very important for this system"), then steer to reasonable unplanned downtime
Cost and time last Opening with "that's going to cost a lot" or "we don't have time" starts negotiations on the wrong foot. Try other justifications first; bring up cost and time (including effort) when all else fails
Divide and conquer Sun Tzu in The Art of War: "If his forces are united, separate them." Does the whole system need five nines, or only specific parts? That narrows the costly requirement and the negotiation
Uptime Downtime per year (per day)
90.0% (one nine) 36 days 12 hrs (2.4 hrs)
99.0% (two nines) 87 hrs 46 min (14 min)
99.9% (three nines) 8 hrs 46 min (86 sec)
99.99% (four nines) 52 min 33 sec (7 sec)
99.999% (five nines) 5 min 35 sec (1 sec)
99.9999% (six nines) 31.5 sec (86 ms)

Five nines is 5 minutes 35 seconds a year, about 1 second a day: ambitious, costly and unnecessary here. Three nines is about 86 seconds of unplanned downtime per day, reasonable for this system.

Negotiating with other architects (Scenario 2)

You are the senior of two architects and want asynchronous messaging between services for performance and scalability. Addison insists on REST ("always faster than messaging and can scale just as well"), citing a Google search and a generative-AI prompt.

It is not your first heated debate. You could dismiss them, but that only intensifies animosity, and an unhealthy relationship between the two architects harms the development team.

  • Demonstration defeats discussion. Every environment differs, so Googling rarely gives the right answer; compare both options in a production-like environment and show the results.
  • Stay calm.
    • Avoid being argumentative or personal
    • calm leadership with clear, concise reasoning almost always wins. If it gets heated or personal, stop and re-engage later
    • the other person usually backs down.

Negotiating with developers

Effective architects gain the team's respect by working with them, so requests spark less resentment. Teams that feel disconnected suffer the Ivory Tower architecture antipattern: orders handed down without regard for opinions, losing respect and eventually breaking down team dynamics. Remedy: always justify your decisions.

Example: a layered-architecture query.

Bad Better
Architect: "You must go through the Business layer to make that call." Developer: "I disagree. It's much faster just to call the database directly." Architect: "Since change control is most important to us, we have formed a closed-layered architecture. This means all calls to the database need to come from the Business layer." Developer: "OK, I get it, but how am I going to deal with these performance issues for simple queries?"
"You must" is a demeaning, commanding voice, one of the worst openings Justification first (people stop listening once they hear something they disagree with); "this means" turns a demand into a fact, less personal, and invites collaboration on speeding simple queries while keeping closed layers

Let the developer reach the answer. Choosing between Framework X and Framework Y, where Y fails the security requirements, a developer insists Y is better. Tell them: show how Y meets the security concerns and the team will use Y.

  • Option 1: they try and fail, understand firsthand, and buy in to X: effectively their decision.
  • Option 2: they find a way: you missed something and now have a better solution, and they still feel involved.
  • Either way it is a win. Developers are smart and have useful knowledge; the more they respect you, the easier negotiating gets.

25.2 The software architect as a leader

The authors maintain about 50% of being effective is people skills (facilitation and leadership).

The 4 Cs of architecture

Change is constant, and things get more complex: if an architecture must support six nines (about 86 ms of downtime a day, 31.5 seconds a year), that is essential complexity: "we have a hard problem". Architects and developers fall into adding accidental complexity: "we have made a problem hard".

Neal Ford: "Developers are drawn to complexity like moths to a flame, frequently with the same result."

Figure 25-1 diagrams the major information flows of the backend processing at a very large global bank: does it need to be that complex? No one knows, because the architect has made it complex. Reasons for adding it: to prove worth when things seem too simple, to stay in the loop on every decision, or for job security. It is one of the best ways to lose respect and become ineffective.

The antidote is the 4 Cs of architecture leadership: communication, collaboration, clear, concise (not to be confused with the 4 Cs of the C4 model). Together they make an effective communicator and collaborator and the team's go-to person for questions, advice, mentoring, coaching and leadership.

Be pragmatic, yet visionary

A fairly high level of maturity and practice is needed.

  • Visionary:
    • thinks about or plans the future with imagination or wisdom: strategic thinking, keeping the architecture valid and useful for a long time
    • Risk: too theoretical, so solutions are hard to implement or even understand.
  • Pragmatic: dealing with things sensibly and realistically, on practical rather than theoretical grounds. It means accounting for:
    • budget and other cost factors
    • time constraints
    • the development team's skill set and level
    • the trade-offs and implications of each decision
    • the technical limitations of any proposed design

Example: sudden, unexplained increases in concurrent load. The visionary proposes breaking apart the databases and building a data mesh (distributed, domain-partitioned databases separating analytical from transactional data).

The pragmatist asks: has the company ever used a data mesh? What are the trade-offs? Would it solve the problem? First find and isolate what limits elasticity: is the database a bottleneck for some services, and could data be cached to reduce calls?

Stakeholders appreciate visionary solutions that fit constraints; developers appreciate practical rather than theoretical ones.

Leading teams by example

Bad architects pull rank; effective ones lead by example. The classic story: a captain removed from the troops orders them up a hill; the doubtful soldiers look to the sergeant, who nods slightly, and they advance with confidence. Rank and title mean little. Gerald Weinberg: "No matter what the problem is, it's a people problem."

  • Don't shut down collaboration. In a production-issue meeting, replying "Well, that's a dumb idea" to a suggestion silences that developer and the rest of the team.
  • Questions, not commands:
Command Question
Architect: "What you need to do is use a cache. That would fix the problem." Developer: "Don't tell me what to do." Architect: "Have you considered using a cache? That might fix the problem." Developer: "Hmmm, no, we didn't think about that. What are your thoughts?"
"What you need to do is" kills collaboration "Have you considered" returns control to the developer and starts a collaborative conversation
  • Coach others' language. Notice team members using demanding or condescending language and take them aside to coach collaborative wording; it improves dynamics and mutual respect.
  • Turn a request into a favour. People dislike being told what to do but want to help.
Demand Favour
"I'm going to need you to split the payment service into five services (store credit, credit card, PayPal, gift card, reward points)... It shouldn't take too long." Developer refuses: too busy. Architect: "this is important and needs to be done this iteration" (and never uses the name) "Hi, Sridhar. Listen, I'm in a real bind... I waited too long... Is there any way you can squeeze this into this iteration? It would really help me out." Developer: "I'll see what I can do." Architect: "Thanks, Sridhar, I really appreciate the help. I owe you one."
  • Use names (and correct pronouns).
    • It makes the conversation personal and builds respect
    • people like hearing their own names. Practise names
    • research hard pronunciations
    • repeat the name back and ask if you said it right until you get it.
  • The handshake.
    • Shake hands and make eye contact when meeting someone or greeting an occasional contact: a tradition from medieval times signalling friend, not foe
      • Mind culture (acceptable in the US, UK, Europe and Australia; bowing and its timing in Japan)
      • **Firm but not overpowering
    • look them in the eye** (looking away is disrespect)
    • two or three seconds
    • don't shake everyone's hand every morning (weird), but a monthly meeting with the head of operations ("Hello, Ruth, nice seeing you again") is right.
  • No hugs.
    • Hugging in a professional setting can make people uncomfortable and even become workplace harassment
    • respect personal boundaries and stick to handshakes and commonsense conduct, including when travelling with a coworker.
  • Be the go-to person.
    • Take initiative: offer help on technical struggles
    • Nontechnical too: if someone, say Antonio, looks depressed, "I'm heading to get some coffee
    • Why don't we head over together?" gives an opening for mentoring, while watching verbal and nonverbal signs for when to back off.
  • Brown-bag "lunch and learn" sessions on a technique or technology (design patterns, new language features) practise mentoring and public speaking and mark you as a leader.

25.3 Integrating with the development team

An architect's calendar is full of overlapping meetings (Figure 25-4). Meetings are a necessary evil; control them to make time for the team. Two kinds (Figure 25-5): those imposed on you (invited) and those you impose (you call).

  • Meetings called by others are hardest to control: architects get invited to almost everything.
    • Ask the organiser why you're needed; if it's only to keep you informed, that is what meeting notes are for.
    • Check the agenda: are you needed for all of it, or only one item (then leave after)?
    • Where possible go in place of developers or the tech lead, so the team stays focused; more meetings for you, more productivity and respect.
Ask for the agenda ahead of time to decide whether you are really needed.
  • Meetings you call:
    • keep them to an absolute minimum, set an agenda and stick to it, keep irrelevant issues out, and ask whether the meeting is more important than the work it interrupts (could it be an email?)
    • If you must, schedule first thing in the morning, right after lunch or late in the day, avoiding central work hours.
Developer flow state

  • Sit with the team
    • (on-site). A cubicle apart says "I am special and should not be disturbed"
    • sitting alongside says "I'm an integral part of the team and available". If you can't, walk around and be seen
    • block time in the morning, after lunch or late in the day to talk, help, answer questions and coach
      • An architect always in their office or on another floor cannot guide the team
      • Stopping to say hi to the head of operations on a coffee run keeps stakeholder channels open.
  • Remote teams make collaboration harder; the authors recommend Jacqui Read's Communication Patterns (O'Reilly, 2023), whose Part 4 is devoted to remote teams.

25.4 Summary

These tips help architects form collaborative relationships with developers and stakeholders. Theodore Roosevelt, 26th US president: "The most important single ingredient in the formula of success is knowing how to get along with people."

Summary points

  • Decode what stakeholders really want, use numbers, qualify requirements, and leave cost for last.
  • Win with demonstrations, justifications and questions, not commands or rank.
  • Avoid accidental complexity; be clear, concise, communicative and collaborative; balance pragmatism and vision.
  • Build trust through names, handshakes, favours and being the go-to person.
  • Guard the team's time and flow; be present.
Terms to carry forward
  • Nines of availability; divide and conquer.
  • Demonstration defeats discussion; Ivory Tower.
  • Essential versus accidental complexity; the 4 Cs.
  • Pragmatic versus visionary; leading by example.
  • Flow state; imposed versus imposing meetings.
