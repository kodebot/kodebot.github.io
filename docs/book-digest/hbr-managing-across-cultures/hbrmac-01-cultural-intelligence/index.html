HBR Managing Across Cultures 1: Cultural Intelligence

Source: P. Christopher Earley and Elaine Mosakowski, "Cultural Intelligence", Harvard Business Review, October 2004 (reprint R0410J), reprinted in HBR's 10 Must Reads on Managing Across Cultures (Harvard Business Review Press, 2016). Own-words summary, much shorter than the source.

Takeaways
  • Cultural intelligence (CQ) is the ability to make sense of unfamiliar contexts and blend in. It goes beyond emotional intelligence by telling apart what is true of all people, what is peculiar to one person, and what is cultural.
  • CQ has three parts: head (cognitive), body (physical) and heart (emotional and motivational). Each is hampered without the other two.
  • Anyone "reasonably alert, motivated, and poised" can reach an acceptable CQ; it can be trained. Surveys of 2,000 managers in 60 countries show most are not equally strong in all three.
  • Foreign cultures are everywhere, including companies, professions and regions, not just countries.

1.1 What CQ is

  • A HSBC airport poster shows a grasshopper labelled a pest in the USA, a pet in China and an appetizer in northern Thailand. Even an insect is read through culture, so gestures and speech abroad invite many readings
    • Some outsiders interpret ambiguous gestures as the natives would and even mirror them: that is CQ.
  • Companies have cultures too, and subcultures (sales cannot talk to engineers, PR loses patience with lawyers).
  • Example:
    • an American expatriate on a design team with two German engineers concluded Germans are rude because they condemned colleagues' ideas as "stunted or immature"
    • A little CQ would have shown he was equating the merit of an idea with the merit of the person, which the Germans separated
      • a subtler reader would also ask how much was German and how much was simply engineers
    • A merely emotionally intelligent manager would empathise and soften, but without knowing what was cultural could not know how easy it would be to change the behaviour.
  • Shared with emotional intelligence:
    • Daniel Goleman's phrase, a propensity to suspend judgment and think before acting. Observe until conduct settles into patterns, then predict
    • that avoids stereotyping.
  • The people most socially successful in their home culture may struggle most with strangers
    • those somewhat detached from their own culture, used to observing and trying to fit in, may adapt more easily.

1.2 Head, body and heart

  • Peter,
    • a sales manager from a competitive California devices company acquired by Eli Lilly, moved to Indianapolis, where bonuses were a small share of pay and criticism was restrained
    • His push-and-confront style failed
    • His trouble was threefold: he did not comprehend the changed landscape (head), could not make his behaviour consistent with everyone around (body), and was disheartened (heart).
  • Head.
    • Rote lists of customs and taboos will not prevent gaffes, and natives may not explain their own culture
    • Build learning strategies and notice clues to shared understanding
    • Devin,
      • an Irish ad manager with a German construction client, neither assumed it was like a German clothing retailer nor like a British construction firm
      • the client sent a new representative to every meeting, so he looked for consistencies across people (punctual, deadline-oriented, open to unconventional messages) and inferred the firm's character.
  • Body.
    • Show through actions that you have entered their world: handshake, ordering coffee
    • Adopting mannerisms lets you understand "what it is like to be them", and they trust more
    • Jeffrey Sanchez-Burks (University of Michigan) found candidates who adopted the recruiter's mannerisms were more likely to get offers
    • It fails with deep reservation or lack of poise:
      • the British aerospace manager Melanie recoiled from a French client's cheek kisses
      • at a charity event a Hispanic community leader and an Anglo-American businessman waltzed around the room for 30 minutes as one stepped closer and the other backed away.
  • Heart.
    • Adapting means overcoming setbacks, so you need self-efficacy, built by past mastery
    • A person with high motivation, after obstacles, re-engages with greater vigour
    • Hyong Moon,
      • a GM manager used to design teams, faced a public clash on a cross-functional team (sales objected to safety features such as side-impact air bags raising price)
      • a senior manager had to step in, but he persisted and persuaded sales that air bags could make the car more marketable, drawing on confidence from earlier successes.
  • Together:
    • after Merrill Lynch bought Mercury Asset Management (end of 1997), casual Fridays spread to UK sites. A Mercury manager understood it as an attempt to reduce hierarchy
    • some resisters failed on head (did not see the intent), body (could not change dress) and heart (lacked motivation, dreading being mistaken for Merrill executives).

1.3 Six profiles

  • Provincial: fine among similar people; the Chevrolet truck engineer led a team at Saturn assuming Chevy-style orderliness, failed and went back.
  • Analyst:
    • deciphers rules methodically
    • Deirdre (London broadcast director) met resisting managers individually, asked why, shared ideas and revised the negotiating strategy to include their approaches, so they cooperated.
  • Natural: intuition and first impressions (Unilever's Donald watches a few minutes and "gets a general sense"); may falter in ambiguity.
  • Ambassador: projects certainty of belonging; the most common type; needs humility to know what he does not know.
  • Mimic:
    • high control of behaviour, less insight
    • Ming, a Shanghai power-authority manager, matches foreigners' distance, eye contact and English speed to put them at ease. Mimicry is not pure imitation, which can read as mockery.
  • Chameleon:
    • high on all three, possibly mistaken for a native, and gets results natives cannot
    • only about 5 percent of surveyed managers. Nigel, a British entrepreneur raised mostly in Saudi Arabia, persuaded a Pakistani founder to close a loss-making Lahore start-up by involving community leaders and drawing on Islamic principles of responsibility to the wider community, with an authoritative style suited to the setting.
  • Many managers are hybrids
    • the most common was ambassador plus analyst (e.g. Brenda, an African-American manager in Cairo, berated young Egyptian men who greeted her with a rap phrase, then realised it was friendly ignorance, went back, and ended up having tea and new friends).

1.4 Building CQ

  • CQ can be developed in "psychologically healthy and professionally competent" people. A six-step path, from the Deutsche Bank programme on relations with Indian outsourcing partners: assess
    • choose training for weaknesses (acting classes for body; reading case studies and distilling principles for head)
    • apply with simple tasks first (find a newspaper, greet an interviewee) to build a base before harder ones such as appraisals
    • organise support
    • enter the setting playing to strengths
    • re-evaluate with feedback.
  • Helmut, a northern German at a Berlin firm sent to a southern German plant ("loud, brash, and cliquish"), was resourceful but lacked confidence: he practised in mixed groups, listed ten daily activities, mastered coffee and drinks with colleagues, then gave face-to-face appraisals, noticing that Bavarians are extroverted only with the familiar, and settled on a two-stage style (commanding first, then personal and inclusive).

1.5 Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the article)

  • Treat critique as aimed at ideas, not you. The article's German engineers show that blunt comment on an idea can be neutral; ask yourself "idea or me?" before reacting.
  • Mirror lightly, not as imitation: match pace, pausing and register of respected colleagues (the "mimic"), as Ming does with English speed.
  • Build the heart part with small wins: the article's remedy for low confidence is simple tasks first. Pick one low-risk speaking moment each day.
  • Score yourself on the twelve statements (cognitive, physical, emotional/motivational) and compare the three averages.
Terms to carry forward
  • CQ: head, body, heart.
  • Six profiles: provincial, analyst, natural, ambassador, mimic, chameleon.
  • Suspend judgment until patterns settle.
