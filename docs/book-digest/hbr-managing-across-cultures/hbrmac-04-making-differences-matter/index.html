HBR Managing Across Cultures 4: Making Differences Matter

Source: David A. Thomas and Robin J. Ely, "Making Differences Matter: A New Paradigm for Managing Diversity", Harvard Business Review, September to October 1996 (reprint 96510), reprinted in HBR's 10 Must Reads on Managing Across Cultures (Harvard Business Review Press, 2016). Own-words summary, much shorter than the source. The research is from 1990 to 1996 and US-centred; company names are disguised except where noted.

Takeaways
  • Diversity efforts often fail because leaders define diversity as head-count by identity group. Thomas and Ely define it as the varied perspectives and approaches to work people bring.
  • Two common paradigms, discrimination-and-fairness (assimilation: "we're all the same") and access-and-legitimacy (differentiation: match people to niche markets), each leave the organisation unable to learn from difference.
  • The third, learning-and-effectiveness (integration), keeps equal opportunity, values differences, and lets them change how the work is done.
  • It needs eight preconditions and leaders who take four actions.

4.1 The three paradigms

  • Many managers now say diversity helps effectiveness (morale, new markets, productivity), yet two decades of initiatives rarely produced leaps and some backfired, raising tension
    • The usual assumption is that diversity means recruiting more people from underrepresented groups
    • Organisations then either encourage people to blend in or set them apart in niche roles (African American MBAs marketing to inner-city communities), assuming the group's main value is knowledge of its own people: limiting in two ways, in the authors' words (limited and limiting).
  • Discrimination-and-fairness.
    • Focus: equal opportunity, recruitment, compliance, mentoring programmes
    • Progress is measured by recruitment and retention goals
    • "The staff ..
    • gets diversified, but the work does not." Typical of leaders who value due process, top-down enforcement, bureaucratic control, and strong visible cultures (the US Army is the extreme)
    • It builds on "we are all the same", so disagreement about the work can be misread as unfairness
      • a woman who says an ad strategy is wrong for some ethnic segments may feel she breaks the code by citing her own knowledge of the group.
    • Iversen Dunham
      • (disguised international consulting firm; from the early 1970s it pursued gender and racial diversity; by 1989 about 50 percent of project leaders and professionals were women, 30 percent people of colour, and the 13-member management committee included five women and four people of colour)
      • Tension appeared
      • White project leaders said newer colleagues made issues "too complex", needed cultural sensitivity, and undermined the firm's quantitative strength by suggesting interviews with rank-and-file and middle managers, qualitative data, and acknowledging subjectivity in reports
      • Women and people of colour felt less authority and unspoken doubt
      • Management saw a racial-fairness problem (manager and subordinate treatment), though the authors judged those conflicts too few to merit the attention
        • the peer conflicts were about the nature of the work
      • The paradigm created "a kind of cognitive blind spot"
      • Management resisted a cultural shift and tensions continue.
  • Access-and-legitimacy.
    • Rationale: a multicultural country and consumer base need a demographically diverse workforce for access and legitimacy (municipal finance units diversified early because of city-hall demographics; consumer firms made dedicated segment marketing roles)
    • Strength: a market-based rationale everyone understands
    • Weakness: leaders push staff into pigeonholes without analysing what their capabilities really are
    • Employees feel exploited, with closed paths elsewhere, and special departments are the first cut in downsizing.
    • Access Capital International
      • (a US investment bank expanding into Europe in the early 1980s): US staff lacked credibility abroad, so it hired Europeans with North American MBAs in teams, profitable, "the best investment bank in the world"
      • A US executive: "If the French team all resigned tomorrow, what would we do? ..
      • We've never attempted to learn what these differences and cultural competencies really are." They "knew enough to use people's cultural strengths ..
      • but we never seemed to learn from them."
  • Learning-and-effectiveness.
    • Dewey and Levin,
      • a small public-interest law firm, was all white and represented only white women in employment disputes
      • It hired a Hispanic female attorney, hoping she would bring in clients
    • instead she brought ideas about what cases to take, and more women of colour followed
      • The firm now litigates English-only policies, once outside "traditional affirmative-action work", serving immigrant women
    • a white principal said it "has affected the work ... enhanced the quality". Attorneys of colour feel respected, "not ... window dressing", and the firm attracts and retains staff.
  • The paradigms in one line:
    • assimilation goes too far toward sameness, differentiation overshoots the other way
    • integration promotes equal opportunity, values cultural differences, and lets the organisation internalise them: the aim is that members can say they are all on the same team with their differences, not despite them

4.2 Preconditions and actions

  • Eight preconditions (not all needed to begin, but most):
    1. Leaders understand different identity groups bring different approaches and truly value variety of opinion (a financial-services firm rewarded call counts until it found its first and third most profitable people used relationship-building).
    2. Leaders see both the learning opportunities and the challenges, and persevere.
    3. The culture expects high performance from everyone (low expectations become self-fulfilling).
    4. The culture stimulates personal development.
    5. It encourages openness and tolerates debate and constructive conflict.
    6. It makes workers feel valued.
    7. A well-articulated mission keeps discussions on goals, not on whose perspective is valid.
    8. A relatively egalitarian, non-bureaucratic structure (keep the enabling parts of bureaucracy, drop the change-resisting ones).
  • First Interstate Bank
    • (a midsize midwestern bank serving a minority community; unusually egalitarian and open) shows a shift in progress: it required loan officers to hold college degrees, but support staff doing loan-officer-like work asked for titles and pay
    • The issue was class diversity
    • The leadership is transforming itself and exploring a career path for people without degrees, "far from easy".
  • Four actions by leaders in completed shifts:
    • Make the mental connection
      • between identity differences and how work gets done, proactively rather than only in crisis
      • At Mastiff (a national insurer), a manager wanted to demote a 15-year-veteran African American woman he had hired as a leader three months earlier
      • His vice president learned he had seen her lead brilliantly at church and asked: why can't she bring those skills here? The woman said she feared she "just wouldn't survive here" if she led authentically
      • They chose to support her style instead of demoting her.
    • Legitimate open discussion.
      • At Torinno Foods, the Chinese chemist Linda solved a soup-flavouring problem by "setting aside my chemistry and drawing on my understanding of Chinese cooking" but did not tell her all-white male colleagues for fear it would mark her as unprofessional
      • the organisation lost the chance to debate and build on the idea.
    • Work against dominance and subordination.
      • Zero tolerance for racism, sexism, harassment, and also for function-based superiority
      • At Delta Manufacturing, a CEO realised he had assumed inner-city plant workers could do no better than subpar, and reversed it; performance rose sharply
      • A chemical-company CEO opened the annual strategy conference to a diagonal slice of the hierarchy, with small-group sessions beforehand so newcomers felt able to speak.
    • Keep organisational trust intact: set a tone of honest discourse, acknowledge tensions and resolve them sensitively and swiftly.
  • Conclusion:
    • the "cardinal limitation" is the leadership's vision of why the workforce is diverse
    • the new paradigm demands commitment to learning and "giving improvement-generating change greater priority than the security of what is familiar".

4.3 Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the article)

  • Share the source of your ideas.
    • Linda hid that her insight came from her culture. If an approach from your own background solves a team problem, say so
    • the article's finding is that hiding it costs the team.
  • Ask what a colleague's method adds, not whether it is "right": the Iversen conflict was about different working methods, not unfairness.
  • Notice assimilation pressure:
    • if you feel you must mute your style to fit, the article treats that as the organisation's loss as well as yours
    • Raise it with the team as a working-style question.
Terms to carry forward
  • Assimilation, differentiation, integration paradigms.
  • Learning-and-effectiveness paradigm; eight preconditions; cognitive blind spot.
