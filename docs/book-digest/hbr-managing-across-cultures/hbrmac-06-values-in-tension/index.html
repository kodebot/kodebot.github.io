HBR Managing Across Cultures 6: Values in Tension

Source: Thomas Donaldson, "Values in Tension: Ethics Away from Home", Harvard Business Review, September to October 1996 (reprint 96502), reprinted in HBR's 10 Must Reads on Managing Across Cultures (Harvard Business Review Press, 2016). Own-words summary, much shorter than the source. The examples are from the 1980s and 1990s.

Takeaways
  • When a host country's ethics seem lower than home's, neither "do as the Romans do" (cultural relativism) nor "do as at home" (ethical imperialism) works.
  • Three guiding principles: respect core human values (the absolute threshold), respect local traditions, and recognise that context matters.
  • Core values: respect for human dignity, respect for basic rights, good citizenship. Between them and the host's rules lies moral free space, where managers use judgment.
  • Use two tests: for relative development ("would it be acceptable at home at a similar stage of development?") and for cultural tradition ("can business succeed without it, and does it break a core value?").

6.1 The two extremes

  • Cultural relativism
    • ("when in Rome ...") says no culture's ethics beat another's, so tolerate bribery or ignore insider-trading rules
      • It breaks down for serious harm
      • In the late 1980s European tanneries and drug firms sought cheap waste dumping across West Africa
    • Nigeria
      • accepted toxic PCBs, unloaded by unprotected workers in thongs and shorts near homes, unaware of the contents
      • Many countries cannot police multinationals even if they want to
      • When investors wanted the SS United States stripped of asbestos, a US bid was over $100 million against under $2 million from a Ukrainian firm in Sevastopol
    • the ship was towed there in October 1993. "Cultural relativism is morally blind."
  • Ethical imperialism
    • (do everywhere what you do at home) is built on absolutism with three problems: it assumes one list of truths (Japanese ethics centres on loyalty, American on liberty and rights), one set of concepts ("rights" has no word in Confucian or Buddhist tradition; Michael Walzer: "There is no Esperanto of global ethics"), and one global behaviour standard (low wages might be ethical where they attract investment and raise living standards)
    • A US computer firm's 1993 sexual-harassment course in Saudi Arabia used a bar scene case, which "baffled and offended" participants
      • a manager in China followed company policy, turned a thieving employee over to authorities, and he was executed
    • The "front page of the newspaper" test is unreliable internationally.
  • Between them, distinguish what is merely different from what is wrong.

6.2 Core values and context

  • Respect local traditions.
    • In Japan, gift giving looked like bribery to Westerners; most now accept it with different limits
    • Hong Kong managers tolerate some bribery more than Westerners but far less tolerate failing to acknowledge a subordinate's work
    • Local respect is not relativism: Union Carbide's Bhopal disaster showed that firms using sophisticated technology must assess the host country's ability to oversee safe use.
  • Context matters: the fungicide EDB, banned in the US, becomes harmless quickly in hot climates, so with monitoring it may be used ethically there.
  • Core human values
    • (the "hypernorms" of Donaldson and Dunfee): the right to good health, to economic advancement, and the Golden Rule found in every major tradition (Confucius, Analects, Book 15: do not do to others what you would not want done to yourself)
    • Using John Rawls's overlapping consensus, Western values (individual liberty, egalitarianism, political participation, human rights) and non-Western ones (kyosei, Japanese: living and working together for the common good; dharma, Hindu: fulfilling inherited duty; santutthi, Buddhist: limited desires; zakat, Muslim: alms for the poor) converge on three:
    • Respect for human dignity (people are not mere tools; safe products and workplaces).
    • Respect for basic rights (health, education, safety, an adequate standard of living).
    • Good citizenship (supporting essential institutions, protecting the environment).
  • Intolerable examples:
    • dumping pollutants near homes
    • inadequate hazardous-materials standards
    • child employment that blocks basic education
    • lying about product specifications. Levi Strauss ended its relationship in 1992 with the Tan family, allegedly forcing 1,200 Chinese and Filipino women to work 74 hours a week in guarded compounds on the Mariana Islands.

6.3 Culture, tension and the two tests

  • Creating an ethical culture.
    • About 90 percent of Fortune 500 companies have conduct codes and 70 percent statements of vision and values, but many only "paste them on the wall", costing managers credibility
    • Good practice: Johnson & Johnson's Credo Challenge sessions (managers discuss ethics in current problems and may criticise the credo), Lockheed Martin's ethics site with complaint channel
    • A code must be explicit when temptation is greatest (rules on gifts, customs payments, intermediaries) yet leave room for judgment: Motorola's code forbids illegal payments, yet allows small gifts "in rare circumstances" where refusal would harm the firm's legitimate interests and the gift benefits Motorola, not the employee.
  • Moral free space.
    • The author and Thomas Dunfee call the gray zone where practices differ but do not break core values "moral free space". Example: Indian firms promise a child of an employee a job, even over better-qualified applicants
    • in a clan-centred country with catastrophic unemployment he cannot condemn it for Indians (in the US it would breach equal employment rules).
  • Two kinds of conflict:
    • Relative development
      • (standards differ because of economic level): ask "Would the practice be acceptable at home if my country were at a similar stage of economic development?" Angolan workers on an offshore rig may be paid less than Gulf of Mexico workers
      • Ciba-Geigy's Entero Vioform, banned in the US for side effects, was rightly wanted by India against epidemic dysentery. But some standards are inadequate at any stage (an oil-rig environment that sharply raises cancer risk).
    • Cultural tradition
      • (e.g. Saudi Arabia barring women from corporate management, rooted in religion and unaffected by wealth): permissible only if the answer is no to both "Is it possible to conduct business successfully in the host country without undertaking the practice?" and "Is the practice a violation of a core human value?" Japanese gift giving passes, within limits of scope and intention
      • Lockheed president Carl Kotchian's suitcases of cash to Japanese politicians in the 1970s went beyond it and led to the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act
      • Roh Tae Woo took $635.4 million in bribes as South Korea's president (1988 to 1993).
  • Bribery
    • is intolerable even when routine: it disrupts distribution, removes incentives to compete on quality and cost, and denies people a minimal standard of living by destroying predictability
    • A US company shipping to Rio de Janeiro lost about 10 percent of crate contents to dock handlers and began sending two crates, and handlers learned to take the second
    • Indian CEOs admit constant bribery with disgust.

6.4 Guidelines for ethical leadership

  1. Treat corporate values and formal conduct standards as absolutes. Around 1950 a Motorola executive walked away from a $10 million sale (nearly 25 percent of annual net profit) when South American officials asked for $1 million in "fees"; CEO Robert Galvin backed him and said Motorola would not accept the sale or deal with those officials again; the story built a culture.
  2. Design conditions of engagement for suppliers and customers (Sears bars prison labour and breaches of worker health and safety; BankAmerica conditioned loans on environmental standards and human rights).
  3. Let foreign units help formulate standards (Rhône-Poulenc Rorer lets subsidiaries add principles; Texas Instruments' Global Business Practices Council: "global ethics strategy, locally deployed").
  4. Support efforts to reduce institutional corruption (General Electric spoke out in India; Transparency International helps coalitions in Russia and Bangladesh).
  5. Exercise moral imagination. Coca-Cola refused Egyptian bribe requests and sponsored a fruit-tree project; Levi Strauss, finding Bangladeshi suppliers employing children under 14, did not simply force dismissals but had them keep paying the children's wages while at school (Levi's covered tuition, books and uniforms) and offered jobs at 14. "Mokita" (a South Seas word): "the truth that everybody knows but nobody speaks".

6.5 Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the article)

  • Use the two-question test as a quick way to explain a decision to a mixed team: "Is this just different, or does it break a basic value?"
  • When a practice from your background surprises British colleagues (or the reverse), ask whether it is a tradition or a core value before judging.
Terms to carry forward
  • Cultural relativism; ethical imperialism (absolutism).
  • Core human values; overlapping consensus; moral free space.
  • Conflict of relative development versus of cultural tradition.
