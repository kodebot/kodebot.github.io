HBR Managing Across Cultures 7: Global Business Speaks English

Source: Tsedal Neeley, "Global Business Speaks English", Harvard Business Review, May 2012 (reprint R1205H), reprinted in HBR's 10 Must Reads on Managing Across Cultures (Harvard Business Review Press, 2016). Own-words summary, much shorter than the source.

Takeaways
  • English is now the common language of business, and many multinationals mandate it. A policy of one language beats unrestricted multilingualism, but rollout is messy and met with resistance.
  • You do not need native fluency: roughly 3,000 to 5,000 words do it for most people, and about 3,500 words take a learner from beginner to advanced.
  • The main failures are emotional: shock, eroding self-confidence, job insecurity, going silent, which hurts performance because people stop contributing ideas and reporting errors.
  • The remedy puts duties on both sides: native speakers must level the playing field (slow down, simplify); non-native speakers must keep using English.

7.1 Why English, and the Rakuten case

  • Neeley's opening:
    • Airbus, Daimler-Chrysler, Fast Retailing, Nokia, Renault, Samsung, SAP, Technicolor and Microsoft in Beijing are among companies mandating English
    • A French company lost a deal in Paris when a prospective customer brought colleagues who did not speak French, and the room could not communicate: a "shocking wake-up call".
  • Rakuten
    • (Japan's largest online marketplace): CEO Hiroshi Mikitani announced in March 2010, in English, that English would be the official language for some 7,100 Japanese employees, to reach his goal of being the number one internet services company in the world
    • cafeteria menus and elevator directories switched overnight
    • staff had to show competence on an international English scoring system within two years or risk demotion or dismissal
      • Honda's CEO, Takanobu Ito, called it "stupid for a Japanese company"
      • Result per Neeley: three of six senior engineering executives are not Japanese and do not speak Japanese
    • half of Rakuten's Japanese employees can engage in internal English communication adequately, and 25 percent use English regularly with partners and foreign colleagues.
  • Three drivers:
    • competitive pressure (you cannot count on customers or suppliers sharing your language)
    • globalisation of tasks and resources (Nestlé saw efficiency gains in purchasing and hiring)
    • cross-border M&A (Hoechst and Rhône-Poulenc chose English for Aventis in 1998 to avoid favouring French or German; Italian appliance maker Merloni adopted English in the 1990s to boost its international image).
  • Will Mandarin be next?
    • Unlikely: English has a giant head start (British Empire from the 16th century, philanthropy by American and British organisations), and "it's easier to pick up 'broken English' than 'broken Mandarin'"
    • About 1.75 billion people (one in four) speak English at a useful level: about 385 million native speakers, about a billion fluent speakers in formerly colonised nations such as India and Nigeria, and millions of learners.

7.2 Obstacles

  • Change is always a shock.
    • At FrenchCo, Marie liked the policy until the first meeting held in English, with translator headsets: "They're humiliating ..
    • I felt like an observer rather than a participant at my own company."
  • Compliance is spotty. At GlobalTech (Germany), two years after the English-only rule, a service rep, Hans, called the Indian software team about a multimillion-dollar customer outage, but they could not start because communications were in German, while documents awaited translation.
  • Self-confidence erodes.
    • A FrenchCo employee: "one's value as an English speaker overshadows one's real value"
    • Interviews with 164 GlobalTech employees found nearly 70 percent still frustrated two years in
      • at FrenchCo 56 percent of medium-fluency and 42 percent of low-fluency speakers worried about advancement
    • People often underestimate their ability or overestimate the challenge: about 3,500 words take you from beginner to advanced, against 10,000 more for advanced to native, with a smaller payoff.
  • Job security falters. Requirements change "sometimes overnight", hard on top performers.
  • Employees resist: reverting to the mother tongue, scheduling global meetings at awkward hours (shifting anxiety to native speakers), avoiding writing in English (no documentation), or staying silent: an HR manager: "They're afraid to make mistakes, so they will just not speak at all."
  • Performance suffers.
    • Silent employees do not offer ideas or flag errors
    • an Indian engineer lost the side exchanges with context and root-cause hypotheses when meetings slid into German.

7.3 Implementation tips and the adoption framework

  1. Involve all employees: make the case, promise support, add cultural-awareness training; rally people around using English to accomplish goals, not to pass proficiency tests.
  2. Managers are referees and enforcers: set group norms, monitor, and correct switching into the mother tongue.
  3. Native speakers must level the playing field: speak more slowly, simplify vocabulary, avoid dominating conversations, encourage non-native speakers to contribute, with coaching if needed.
  4. Non-native speakers must comply, including in informal talk, and avoid exclusionary tactics.
  • The two factors:
    • buy-in (belief a single language will benefit me or the firm) and belief in capacity (confidence I can gain enough fluency) give four types: inspired
    • frustrated
    • indifferent and oppressed. Without buy-in people will not brush up; without belief, they lose hope.
  • Raising belief:
    • give experience (Rakuten sent senior executives to the UK and US for immersion, offered weeks-long programmes in the Philippines, and plans to send over 1,000 engineers to conferences abroad)
    • foster positive attitudes (managers model risk-taking and mistakes; Mikitani offered to teach middle managers English himself)
    • verbal persuasion ("You can do it", "I believe in you")
    • study habits (language vendors who know the company context, work time given for study).
  • Raising buy-in:
    • messaging (Mikitani replied to about 120 managers' weekly reports pushing language growth; surveys showed a dramatic rise in the view that the policy was "necessary")
    • internal marketing (an English intranet with success stories, monthly meetings)
    • branding staff as global, not local, via an enterprise social network.
  • Cultural identity: the more people you can communicate with, the better placed you are to spread your culture and message.
  • The CEO as role model: Mikitani holds one-on-one reviews with top Japanese executives in English: "If you forgive a little, you'll give up everything."

7.4 Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the article)

  • Use the numbers, not the shame.
    • The article says useful working English is about 3,000 to 5,000 words, and that fear of mistakes, not lack of vocabulary, makes people go silent
    • Contribute early in every meeting, even one sentence.
  • Ask native speakers to level the field. The article puts the duty to slow down and simplify on them; you can say, "Could we go one point at a time?" without apology.
  • Write things down in English. The article shows undocumented work is a cost; a short written summary after meetings turns your clarity into authority.
  • Drill the shortest strong structure (point, reason, example) in speaking-practice/.
Terms to carry forward
  • Englishnization (Mikitani's term).
  • Buy-in and belief in capacity; inspired, frustrated, indifferent, oppressed.
  • Native speakers level the playing field.
