HBR Managing Across Cultures 8: 10 Rules for Managing Global Innovation

Source: Keeley Wilson and Yves L. Doz, "10 Rules for Managing Global Innovation", Harvard Business Review, October 2012 (reprint R1210F), reprinted in HBR's 10 Must Reads on Managing Across Cultures (Harvard Business Review Press, 2016). Own-words summary, much shorter than the source.

Takeaways
  • Single-site innovation leans on shared tacit knowledge, trust and an executive on the spot. Dispersed projects lose these, so they need a different playbook.
  • The ten rules group into enabling conditions (start small, stable context), structure (senior oversight, rigorous project management, a lead site), resourcing (define up front, capability over availability, knowledge overlap, few partners), and communication (do not rely only on technology).
  • Based on more than a decade of field research at 47 companies and a 2004 survey with Booz & Company of 186 companies in 19 countries (innovation spend over US$78 billion).
  • All ten need to be in place; one or two give only fleeting success.

8.1 The ten rules

  1. Start small. Staff used only to colocated work struggle in global projects: in the absence of everyday encounters they cannot signal trustworthiness, and sites often compete for resources. Run small, dispersed projects of two or three sites first. Schneider Electric and Toshiba's joint venture STI (electrical drives and inverters) ran small non-critical joint projects under senior scrutiny; engineers who had been unenthusiastic quickly agreed working practices and trust.
  2. Provide a stable organisational context. During restructuring or acquisition integration, senior managers look elsewhere, decisions hang and turf battles flare. Elecompt (disguised) launched a global project amid acquisitions and an R&D reorganisation; fear of job losses led many skilled engineers at one site to leave, causing delays. Shelter teams and bolster loyalty, especially when expanding R&D in China, where talent competition leaves little time for loyalty.
  3. Assign oversight and support to a senior manager as arbiter, risk manager, support provider and ultimate decision maker. At Essilor an executive-team member heads every international project. On the photochromic lens project with PPG and Transitions Optical (more than 20 sites), hitting the launch date required cutting production validation, which none of the 18 production managers wanted to risk; the executive took it to the committee, which agreed and ruled that the project, not the production sites, owned the risk; the product launched on schedule.
  4. Use rigorous project management and seasoned leaders. Siemens uses Design for Six Sigma to set common tools, coaching and feedback timetables; Essilor has a corporate project unit staffed across functions and geographies, whose managers serve years then return to specialties, building cross-cultural skills. Standard tools may not suffice: STI's project manager made all initial communication on a topic voice-to-voice after email caused misunderstandings; Synopsys mixed workspaces of parallel product teams to avoid "us versus them".
  5. Appoint a lead site responsible for delivery on time and budget; sites see the project through their own contribution. At STI the French site, which defined the requirements, coordinated, integrated and made final decisions; at Elecompt every site had equal weight, decisions were negotiated and stalled ("an escalation of problems without corresponding solutions"), until structures were added two years in.
  6. Invest time defining the innovation. Colocated teams learn and adapt as they go; dispersed teams must define architecture, module functionality, interfaces, process flows, timelines and knowledge needs up front. Studies show a positive correlation between investment in definition and success. Essilor, with under two years to deliver, spent nine months defining modules and interfaces, with short colocation of representatives and constant project-manager travel.
  7. Allocate resources on capability, not availability. At Elecompt a US team with the most availability but without the required experience was given critical software and struggled; it moved later, after morale dipped, time was wasted and costs rose. Availability staffing "completely undermines the basic rationale for global innovation".
  8. Build enough knowledge overlap to see interdependencies before the costly integration phase. At Siemens, each module is built by a specialist team and overseen by a virtual team with representatives of the other modules.
  9. Limit subcontractors and partners. Prefer trusted, familiar and nearby partners. Boeing's 787 Dreamliner used over 50 main partners across the US, Europe and East Asia, with little visibility; new materials initially prevented attaching the wings to the fuselage; Boeing colocated partners for six months and delivered almost three years late, losing orders to the Airbus A350.
  10. Do not rely solely on technology. Email, web meetings, social media and video mask differences and cause tension. Add generous travel budgets, site visits, team meetings, temporary transfers, and cross-site reporting lines to build allegiance to the global project. Tata Communications, highly dispersed even at the top, pairs its ICT investment with hefty travel budgets.

8.2 Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the article)

  • Follow STI's voice-first rule on your team: for a disputed or ambiguous topic, speak (call or video) before writing; written English is where nuance gets lost both ways.
  • Define interfaces and decisions up front in your own words, in writing, then confirm verbally; the article ties clarity at the start to success when iteration is hard.
  • If you lead a distributed team, name a lead site or owner for each decision so debates do not stall.
Terms to carry forward
  • Lead site; knowledge overlap; colocation.
  • Capability, not availability.
