HBR Managing Across Cultures 9: Lost in Translation

Source: Fons Trompenaars and Peter Woolliams, "Lost in Translation", Harvard Business Review, April 2011 (reprint W1104A), reprinted in HBR's 10 Must Reads on Managing Across Cultures (Harvard Business Review Press, 2016). Own-words summary, much shorter than the source. Survey percentages are as reported in the article.

Takeaways
  • Cultures differ in how they perceive and respond to failure. From THT Consulting's 30-year global survey, five dimensions matter most.
  • The five: control (prevent or respond), rules versus relationships, individual versus team responsibility, identification with failure (blame the idea or the person), and status by performance or position.
  • Each pole has strengths and costs; firms that reconcile both (Emirates, AMD, IBM) gain a platform for innovation.
  • The cultural unit is not only the country; test your own organisation by function, level and geography.

9.1 Five dimensions

  1. Do we control our environment, or does it control us? Internal-control cultures believe good planning avoids most failures; external-control cultures see failure as inevitable and build skills to respond quickly. About 90 percent of Israelis and about 80 percent of Americans, British, Australians and Canadians felt strong control; only 40 percent of Chinese and 50 percent of Russians; South Korea and Indonesia about 70 percent. Internal control: failure is a personal threat, so people may turn authoritarian or write exhaustive contracts, but they waste less because they do not repeat mistakes. Fatalistic cultures adapt well but leave causes untreated and pick up the same pieces again and again. Emirates blends both: Western pilots in simulators try to avoid failure and propose cockpit and route changes, Arab pilots take risks to feel a stall; learning from both makes it "one of the safest airlines in the world".
  2. Rules or relationships? Rule-centred societies (US, Britain) want global rules and a level field (better distributive justice, but obsession with rules; "why the United States has so many more lawyers than Japan"). Relationship-centred ones (China, Russia, India) resolve failure privately through relationships. 70 to 80 percent of Swiss, North Americans and Australians believe no exceptions should be made for friends; in the BRIC countries only 25 to 40 percent would put the rule above the person. Headquarters and local offices collide: locals appear to conform while following local custom, so the organisation cannot learn. A rules culture gives clarity and cost efficiency but red tape; a relationship culture tolerates failure and learning but wastes and curtails competing with friends. Management by exception (attention to surprises outside rules) lets rules and exceptions sustain each other. A German financial-services firm's value "integrity" meant sticking to principles for Americans but helping friends for Koreans; a Brazilian colleague suggested bending a rule once for a struggling friend, promising a tougher next review and mentoring, which shows loyalty and commitment to rules.
  3. Individual or team responsibility? Individualist cultures produce independence and productive competition (Best Salesperson of the Quarter). In communitarian cultures groups share blame: at a US multinational's Indonesian plant, the director said "The whole work group has accepted responsibility" for a batch-ruining error; the woman responsible was ashamed and tried to resign, coaxed back by coworkers; the group admitted they had not helped a new colleague enough. The US expatriate worried "if everyone is responsible, then in effect no one is". Communitarianism nurtures learning but can choke individual creativity; for learning to spread, redefine failure as a learning opportunity. IBM rewards salespeople for presenting lessons learned from client work in addition to volume bonuses (group performance up 30 percent). Survey on blaming the individual: Russians nearly 70 percent, Australians 58, Americans 54, Danes 53, British 48, Dutch 43; China 36, India 35, Japan and Brazil under 35.
  4. How much do we identify with failure? Non-identifying cultures treat failure as "an idea that didn't work" and celebrate it as learning, but may jump to conclusions. Share who blame the idea rather than the person: 72 percent of the Dutch, 66 percent of Americans, 31 percent of Indians, 29 percent of Germans, 24 percent of Chinese, 12 percent of Japanese. At AMD in Dresden, Americans found Germans slow and risk-averse; Germans found Americans hasty in tossing underdeveloped ideas into the "war room" brainstorms. AMD built time-outs into meetings so Germans could swap and criticise ideas privately in German, then collate them on Post-it notes for the Americans, who were astonished at the creativity.
  5. Status by performance or position? Achieving cultures take initiative but guard achievements, litigate, and may exaggerate or take credit (an MBA student left the library with eight books so others could not read them). Ascribing cultures avoid acting when the boss is present, since the boss takes responsibility for outcomes. At a British dairy, operator Malcolm saved money in 99 of 100 shutdowns but waited for the boss on day shifts; making him supervisor fixed it. At a US bank in Argentina, imported titles (Departmental Secretary I, II, III) demotivated staff; retitling them as Personal Assistants to named superiors lifted morale at no budget cost.

9.2 Applying it

  • A failure-culture survey
    • (six questions): are failures a fact of life or avoidable by planning
    • what would you do if a friend made a mistake you must report publicly
    • is individual creativity or team consensus more important for avoiding mistakes
    • do you address criticism to the task or the person
    • does who made a mistake affect its seriousness
    • are failures attributed to the person or team, or to the department head.
  • Check variation inside companies (a Japanese car maker may resemble GM more than Sony) by function, business line, rank and geography.

9.3 Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the article)

  • Ask the survey questions in your own team. Learn whether your British engineers separate the idea from the person (the Dutch and Americans mostly do; the article says Germans, Indians and Japanese mostly do not).
  • Offer a private critique route after meetings, as AMD did; some people think best away from the group.
  • Say how you will treat mistakes ("Tell me early; we fix the cause, not blame the person"), since the article shows people hide failures where identity is tied to them.
Terms to carry forward
  • Internal versus external control; rule-centred versus relationship-centred.
  • Communitarian versus individualist; identifying versus non-identifying.
  • Achieving versus ascribing status; management by exception; co-opetition.
