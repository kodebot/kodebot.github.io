HBR Managing Across Cultures 10: The Right Way to Manage Expats

Source: J. Stewart Black and Hal B. Gregersen, "The Right Way to Manage Expats", Harvard Business Review, March to April 1999 (reprint 99201), reprinted in HBR's 10 Must Reads on Managing Across Cultures (Harvard Business Review Press, 2016). Own-words summary, much shorter than the source. The research is from the 1990s.

Takeaways
  • Expat assignments cost two to three times a home-country role ($300,000 to $1 million a year) and mostly give "anemic returns". The authors studied about 750 US, European and Japanese companies over a decade.
  • Successful firms follow three practices: send people for the right reasons (knowledge creation and leadership development), send the right people (cultural skill matched to technical skill), and manage repatriation deliberately.
  • The practices reinforce each other; all three are needed.

10.1 The problem

  • About 80 percent of midsize and large companies send professionals abroad
    • 45 percent plan more. Findings: 10 to 20 percent of US managers sent abroad returned early (job dissatisfaction or difficulty adjusting)
    • of those who stayed, nearly a third did not perform up to expectations
    • a quarter of those who finished left within a year of repatriation, often to a competitor, double the turnover of managers who did not go abroad.
  • Why:
    • executives assume good business rules are the same everywhere
    • do not fund selection or training
    • do not "coddle" well-paid expats
    • and do not think returnees need help. Only 11 percent of HR managers have worked abroad themselves, so HR gets "bogged down in the administrative minutiae".

10.2 Right reasons

  • Wrong reasons:
    • reward favourites with Paris or London posts, dump the mediocre, or only fill an immediate need
    • Effective firms expect fire-fighters "to plant forests when the embers are cool": generate and transfer knowledge, or develop global leadership, or both.
  • Knowledge:
    • a Canadian firm opening a phone plant in Vietnam would expect its manager to transfer knowledge to local professionals and learn from them
    • Nokia has no central R&D but 36 centres in 11 countries, bringing engineers and designers together for assignments of up to two years to invent products (the 6100 series, launched in Beijing).
  • Leadership:
    • GE's CEO: "The next head of GE will be somebody who has spent time in Bombay, in Hong Kong, in Buenos Aires." Several-month assignments change a person's mental map: a tall American hit his head on a Japanese restaurant doorjamb on two days, ducked on the third
    • expats "learn to duck"
      • Stephen Burke of Disney (EuroDisney, 1993) saw that banning alcohol offended local customers and that tour operators matter more in Europe than in North America
    • he won wine sales and vacation packages, and attendance and hotel occupancy soon rose to the park's first operational profit.
  • Tell expats the rationale too: a lean-manufacturing expert moved from Asia to the US was told his job was to pass on knowledge, not to groom him for promotion, so he kept realistic expectations.

10.3 Right people

  • Firms often send people who are "capable but culturally illiterate". A US carmaker's steel-contract negotiator in Korea offended consensus-minded suppliers with a confrontational style, refused to change, and was recalled
    • his replacement needed a year to repair the damage.
  • Five traits sought:
    • a drive to communicate (not giving up after early failures)
    • broad-based sociability (not only fellow expats)
    • cultural flexibility (eating local food, following local sports)
    • a cosmopolitan orientation (respecting different norms), and a collaborative negotiation style.
  • Three assessment methods and their costs:
    • Huntsman
      • (chemicals; sales $4.75 billion): Jon Huntsman, Jr. takes managers with global potential on international trips and watches whether they approach strange sights and shopkeepers with curiosity or look for "the nearest Pizza Hut"
      • also watches them with foreign guests at home.
    • LG Group
      • (a $70 billion Korean conglomerate): an early-career survey of about 100 questions (bought for $300 to $500 per person) about past behaviour, followed by a personal development plan
      • about 97 percent later meet expectations.
    • Colgate-Palmolive
      • (about 70 percent of sales outside the US): recruits graduates with cross-cultural experience and sends them on 6 to 18 month stints without costly expat benefits (one manager became country manager in Ukraine before 30). Most accurate, most expensive
      • suits firms needing many global managers.

10.4 Right ending

  • Findings:
    • about a third were still in temporary roles three months after coming home
    • over three-quarters felt their job was a demotion
    • 61 percent lacked opportunities to use foreign experience
    • returnee turnover reached 25 percent
      • One firm lost all 25 managers it had sent abroad within a year of their return ("a check for $50 million tossed to the winds")
      • A European engineer, after a four-year Saudi assignment costing about $4 million, was scolded that Saudi ways had "nothing to do" with headquarters, waited nine months for a permanent job with less authority, and joined a competitor.
  • Reasons:
    • returnees want recognition and a chance to use skills ("If you have been the orchestra conductor overseas, it is very difficult to accept a position as second fiddle back home")
    • the company and personal lives have changed (mentors gone, children unsettled).
  • Practices:
    • Monsanto starts planning three to six months before return, with an HR officer and senior line manager assessing gained skills, a self-assessment report, and a three-way meeting to pick the best job
    • Unocal runs a daylong debriefing for expats and families (videos, shared tips, keeping a journal)
    • Programmes are often outsourced or shared in a consortium
    • Honda of America Manufacturing
      • does all three: clear strategic objectives, a strengths-and-weaknesses survey, active job matchmaking six months before return and a debrief interview
      • nearly all expats meet expectations and returnee turnover is under 5 percent.
  • GE Medical Systems all but eliminated unwanted post-return turnover while international sales rose from 10 percent to over 50 percent of total sales in ten years.

10.5 Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the article)

  • Treat your own move as an assignment. If you are an expatriate yourself, the three practices apply to you: know your purpose in the role (knowledge to share, leadership to build), keep building local ties beyond other expats, and plan what you want to do with this experience next.
  • Seek the "drive to communicate" signals: the article says success goes to people who keep trying after early embarrassing attempts, which matches your daily speaking drills.
Terms to carry forward
  • Expat, repatriation; mental map.
  • Five traits: drive to communicate, sociability, flexibility, cosmopolitan orientation, collaborative negotiation.
