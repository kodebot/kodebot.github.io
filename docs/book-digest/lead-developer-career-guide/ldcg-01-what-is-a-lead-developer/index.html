Lead Developer Career Guide 1: What is a lead developer?

Source: Shelley Benhoff (Manning, foreword by Steve Buchanan), Lead Developer Career Guide, ch. 1 (author per the back cover; the book mentions HoffsTech as the author's company). Next: Lead Developer Career Guide 2: Lead developer career trajectory.

Takeaways
  • A lead developer (technical lead) is a senior developer plus leadership: guides and mentors, makes technical decisions, represents the team to project managers, clients and stakeholders.
  • Many seniors are promoted on technical skill alone; the gap is soft skills: communication, decisions, conflict, empathy.
  • Your success = your team's success: delivery against plan, low technical debt, ticket and bug open/close rates, clean releases.
  • Five responsibilities: lead the team, work with project teams, talk to clients and stakeholders, set development standards, build technical architecture.
  • Three expectations: team support, working relationships, being a leader.

Who can be a lead developer

Anyone, from any background. Diversity statistics quoted:

  • US (BLS): 21.5% of software developers are women; White 55.0%, Asian 36.4%, Black, Hispanic or Latino 5.7%; about 8% LGBTQIA+ (www.bls.gov/cps/cpsaat11.htm).
  • Global (Stack Overflow 2022 survey, 73,000+ developers, 180 countries): about 93% men, 4.8% women, 1.39% non-binary or similar
    • 39.38% white, 37.25% European, 9.7% Indian, 9.48% Asian, 5.71% Hispanic or Latino (https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2022/#demographics-gender-prof).
  • Why it matters:
    • diverse teams build for a wider audience. The author's first hire was three white women and one white man: unconscious bias
    • she should have demanded a more diverse pool from the recruiter. Communities: Black Girls Code, Girls Who Code, Women Who Code, Latinas Who Code.
  • Degrees are not required: Patrick Collison (Stripe) and Sahil Lavingia (Gumroad) have none.

Who the book is for

  • Junior developers: learn to study and hone technical skills; communication and active listening ("what and why").
  • Senior developers: prepare for leadership; sell your technical approach, get buy-in, mentor.
  • Engineering managers: help leads grow; understand their struggles.
  • Not for: non-developers or developers without a team to lead.

Top industries

Retail, technology, healthcare, banking/fintech, manufacturing, business services, government.

Industry Top companies Pros Cons
Retail Walmart, Amazon, Kroger, Costco e-commerce focus, employee discounts layoffs tied to sales, holiday long hours
Technology Meta, Apple, Amazon, Netflix, Google best talent, perks, high salaries, vesting very high competition, stringent reviews, long hours

  • Government (US): stable with good benefits, but legacy code (COBOL programmers still in high demand).
  • Whatever the industry the lead developer role is the same.

Being successful

  • Technical background from junior, senior or developer advocate roles: build to business or functional requirements, write technical requirements, understand deployment
    • A lead architect may supply the technical approach, but the roles are often combined.
  • Technical skills: programming languages, technical specifications, features/components/systems, deploying code.
  • Leadership skills: facilitate communication, make final decisions, gather feedback, emotional intelligence, self-awareness.
  • Quick decisions in an emergency by gathering team feedback; empathy builds trust, trust raises productivity.
  • Measures of success: tasks completed vs planned each iteration, ticket/bug open and close rates, production deployments with no critical errors.
  • You can get promoted into the role; get mentors from different backgrounds (gender, race, education, experience) to find your own style.

Lead developer tasks

Senior vs lead

  • Senior: focuses on own work; development tasks; mentors juniors; troubleshoots errors and bugs; little client contact.
  • Lead:
    • represents the whole team to project managers, clients and stakeholders
    • builds technical architecture
    • documentation
    • communicates specs to the team
    • calculates estimates
    • develops coding standards
    • presents the technical approach.

Starting a project (cycle): gather feedback (business and technical requirements) → form technical approach → create estimates with the team (they do the work) → present approach → refine estimates; estimates change often.

Leading the development team

  • Lead by example:
    • set the tone. Emergency plan (site down): stay calm, plan the attack, assign tasks, tell everyone the plan step by step, update as steps complete
    • involve QA and give them the timing.
  • Build trust:
    • if the team doesn't trust you they won't bring problems and may quit. Turnover facts: LinkedIn 13.2% in tech (https://mng.bz/NB6x)
    • Work Institute: cost about 33.3% of annual salary (https://mng.bz/Dpgg)
    • up to two months to hire and six months to ramp up
    • a developer may take a year to acclimatise
    • so provide onboarding documentation.
  • Back up your people:
    • if an idea is decent, defend it
    • if not exact, build on it ("That's a good start, but what do you think about xyz?"). Example: the team wanted Atlassian Jira instead of a director's suggested tracker
    • she explained it in a one-on-one and kept the team involved in choosing processes.
  • Open communication: keep promises, be receptive to feedback, act honestly and fairly.
  • Prevent burnout: promote work-life balance, encourage breaks, share workload, recognise achievements; get team feedback on estimates.

Working with project teams

  • Lead is the liaison between project manager and developers; provide estimates and approach; organise tasks, priorities, bugs and technical debt.
  • When priorities change, let a developer reach a stopping point, document where they left off, and check in to the repository.
  • With QA: match quality to requirements, help the test strategy, document technical requirements, answer questions, learn common problems.
  • Clients and stakeholders:
    • one of the hardest skills (especially for introverts)
    • attend kickoffs, sprint planning and retrospectives
    • technical info comes from you, not the PM, reducing miscommunication.

Communicating with clients and stakeholders

  • Assess comfort
    • by body language and by how they react when things go wrong (a client who only asks "when" and "how much" doesn't trust the team)
    • A unified front eases concerns
    • References: https://mng.bz/lrxR, https://mng.bz/BgMl, https://mng.bz/dZ21.
  • Understand their point of view: don't design before business requirements exist (scope creep); know why you build each feature; suggest improvements to add value.
  • Treat them as people: open with "How is everyone doing?"; weekend chat; shared interests; mild humour. Offshore and domestic teams alike.
  • Consulting/B2B raises the stakes (retaining the client; multi-million budgets); internal projects are easier; third-party vendors are business partners: same professionalism.

Setting development standards

  • Goal: you can't tell who wrote the code; any developer can find what they need quickly.
  • Granular: brace placement, comments before every function, naming conventions, file/directory/module/function patterns.
  • Document, review at onboarding, enforce in code review; discuss common errors with the whole team.
  • Follow organisational standards first; update as tech stacks change; propose improvements.
  • Stay current: newsletters, Slack/Discord communities (Docker's Slack with Captains and Community Leaders; .NET; Sitecore); let the team suggest improvements.

Building technical architecture

  • Build and document architecture from scratch within budget; ask other leads for help.
  • Learn by shadowing the lead, getting their documentation, prototypes, personal projects, boot camps, pattern books, case studies, a portfolio.
  • Seek an impartial outside opinion.
  • Budget:
    • servers, software, hosting, databases, security
    • as granular as possible up front
    • reassess on upgrades
    • use AWS or Azure cost calculators
    • budget every environment (development, testing, staging, production).
  • Produce a technical architecture diagram (flowchart)
    • follow organisational standards for shapes, lines, colours and legends
    • it plans the whole lifecycle and shows how systems interact (example: Azure AD, Front Door, WAF, App Service, SQL, Cosmos, Redis, queues).

Lead developer expectations

Responsibilities are accountabilities; expectations are others' standards for you (communicator, mentor, leader). Speak non-developers' language: technical but not in the weeds.

Team support

  • Answer questions within about one hour (the author's rule) to avoid losing deep focus; set boundaries but compromise.
  • Anticipate needs: answer fully in meetings; if someone repeats a question, they may not understand or there's no documentation; ask them to repeat back their understanding.
  • FAQs: central repository; separate FAQs for developers (in the code repository) and non-developers; everyone can add; lead checks accuracy and duplicates.
  • Glossary:
    • everything in the architecture diagram, acronyms in full (organisation-specific acronyms google badly)
    • alphabetical or grouped
    • a background knowledge section linking to industry documentation.

Working relationships

  • Respect expertise; know your unconscious bias (automatic stereotypes). Common biases:
    • Ageism: older people are less capable.
    • Beauty bias: judging appearance.
    • Conformity bias: peer pressure to agree.
    • Affinity bias: drawn to people like us.
    • Confirmation bias: seeking to confirm past ideas.
    • Attribution bias: assuming motives from actions.
    • Gender bias, ableism, cultural bias, racism.
  • Appreciate people: praise in meetings, say thanks, team lunches, virtual happy hours, games, D&D.
  • Be honest and direct: don't sugarcoat or cover problems; consistent honesty earns respect.

Being a leader

  • Decisions: weigh risk, form a plan, execute, check in; share information, don't just announce the plan.
  • Accountability:
    • keep promises
    • if not, say when
    • apologise when wrong. The author over-promised at her first lead job
    • learned to push back on non-priorities so people came to her when overloaded.
  • Lead change: change causes anxiety; follow the project's purpose and vision; empower the team to propose changes.
  • Inspire without people-pleasing: telling different people different things breaks trust; explain decisions up front; listen to unhappy people one on one.

Case study (the author)

  • Developer since 1998
    • was a web developer and trainer, then a manager (with no management or project-management training), then lead developer at a web agency (external clients, stress, introversion, avoiding conflict, wanting to please everyone).
  • Grew via mentors and reading (Harvard Business Review), leaning on the team, admitting "I don't know", learning from failures.
  • Greatest joy: watching mentees succeed (a talented developer coached on confidence and presenting; she told him she still gets nervous and keeps a hand towel on her desk; he is now an engineering manager).
  • Takeaway: "people work with me, not for me": the job is to draw out their talent. She adds mentoring to job descriptions to attract talent.

Summary points

Lead developers lead and mentor teams and communicate with project teams; come from any background; day-to-day tasks are the five above; expectations are the three above; need technical expertise (specs, features, troubleshooting, deploying) plus leadership (communication, decisions, feedback, emotional intelligence, self-awareness).
