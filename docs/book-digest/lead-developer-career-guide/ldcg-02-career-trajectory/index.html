Lead Developer Career Guide 2: Lead developer career trajectory

Source: Shelley Benhoff, Lead Developer Career Guide (Manning), ch. 2. Previous: Lead Developer Career Guide 1: What is a lead developer?. Next: Lead Developer Career Guide 3: Learning lead developer skills.

Takeaways
  • Common path: junior → senior → lead → management, but no path is set in stone (help desk, QA, trainer, writer, sales engineer, developer advocate can all lead here).
  • Companies look for technical + business + soft skills; seniors should practise mentoring, feedback, conflict resolution, empathy.
  • Résumé: objective, summary, skills at the top; cover letter tailored; network early.
  • Technical interviews: ask questions, explain why, say how you'd find answers. Leadership interviews: business goals, budgets, staffing; credit the team.
  • Stand out with open source, a blog, a GitHub portfolio; be full-stack plus CI/CD, cloud, Docker/Kubernetes.

2.1 Developer roles

The author could not find a zero-experience junior role, so she started on help desk; later roles included web developer, courseware designer, manager, lead developer, business owner. Visa-sponsored workers have the extra hurdle of finding sponsoring employers.

Junior developer

  • Basic skills from higher education, boot camp or self-study; typically under a year to three years of experience (the author calls the "experience required" rule gatekeeping).
  • Requirements: passion for technology, basic programming, detail-oriented, willingness to learn, teamwork, flexibility.
  • Responsibilities: keep improving skills, assist the team, attend meetings, troubleshoot errors and fix bugs.
  • Learn to break concepts down and find documentation; hardest part is feeling like a burden. Troubleshooting teaches critical thinking.

Senior developer

  • Requirements: more than three years, complex projects, high-quality features, all phases of the SDLC, self-managed tasks and projects.
  • Some firms add a mid-level role (same responsibilities, more experience). The author was junior for five years, then mid-level, then straight to management
    • promotions are rarely automatic, so ask.
  • Expected to mentor, voice technical opinions, work independently, speak up in meetings, apply higher-level concepts.
  • Soft skills to build before leading: communication, providing feedback, mentorship, conflict resolution, empathy, emotional intelligence.
  • Hiring seniors, the author wants willingness to help the team and skill in the existing stack, not 100% of the job description.

  • Prepare for lead: watch how leads draw architecture diagrams, offer to help with documentation, take initiative (present technical information, spot improvements), reflect on lessons learned.

Mentor the development team yes
  • Usually a combined role: lead also writes code, does code reviews, maintains best practices, may build initial infrastructure and write local-environment instructions.
  • Business skills:
    • assist project planning, work with budgets (hopefully avoiding cost growth), understand business requirements, goals and desired outcomes
      • Translate business requirements into technical ones
    • ask about business drivers (brand awareness, end-user support, sales). For agencies, the goal is customer retention.
  • Soft skills:
    • mentoring, presenting to project teams, facilitating communication between teams, talking directly to clients, empathy and emotional intelligence, feedback, conflict resolution
    • If you don't know an answer, help the team find it (internal, clients, vendors), let seniors shine, and document answers to avoid repeats
    • Learn to make engaging slides.

Management and beyond

  • Roles: engineering manager, technical director, CTO; you won't code daily (keep skills with personal projects). You can stay a senior or lead for your whole career.
  • Hiring
    • (hardest skill): probe the candidate's specific contribution, not the project's significance
    • hire complementary skills. Forbes survey: recruiting costs about 33% of first-year salary (https://mng.bz/ZVRN).
  • Own budgets (project costs and salaries), have difficult conversations, keep business processes current.
  • Four-stage trajectory: junior (learns) → senior (expert, mentors) → lead (face of the team) → management (budgets, staffing, processes).

Other routes into development

  • Degree not required; boot camps and certifications; diverse backgrounds spur innovation.
  • Intern (the author interned at Black & Decker); help desk/tech support (networking, troubleshooting, teaching users).
  • Data science (statistics, ML, software; data hygiene; some prompt engineering) and AI/ML (algorithms, system design, agile-like iteration).
  • Technical training: teach by asking learners how they'd solve it; instructional design (learner, goals, objectives, formats: written, video, audio).
  • Technical writing: documenting a product by feature (vs training's start-to-finish how-to).
  • QA: test from the user's perspective (requirements can fail business needs), regression testing.
  • Technical marketer/content creator and sales engineer: translate technical concepts for non-technical audiences; sales engineers are good at zeroing in on problems.
  • Developer advocate / developer relations: public speaking, content (videos, webinars, live streams); a lateral move before or after lead.

2.2 Moving through a career

  • Network early so you don't apply anonymously: online communities, events, open source, meetups, LinkedIn. The author builds her brand on LinkedIn by posting about her technologies.

Résumé

Keep different résumés per job type (e.g. lead developer vs developer advocate). Contents:

  • Name and contact (phone, email; optionally address, LinkedIn, portfolio URL).
  • Career objective (the position title).
  • Career summary (bulleted highlights).
  • Technical skills summary (languages, methodologies, tools).
  • Professional experience: table of the past 5 to 7 years (company, location, dates, tasks and accomplishments).
  • Additional experience: shortened older roles.
  • Education and certifications: everything, even unrelated.
  • Keywords: applicant tracking systems count keyword frequency, so page count isn't a penalty.
  • Common errors: spelling, grammar, punctuation; missing contact info; no Technical Skills section; poor organisation; no tailoring.
  • AI tools: ChatGPT to compare against a job description and suggest keywords; Grammarly for style.

Cover letter

Recruiters rarely read every word; a letter summarises your fit. Include:

  • position title and company;
  • where you found the posting;
  • résumé highlights matching the job;
  • availability for interview and start date;
  • contact information.

Keep a template but tailor it (generic = spam). Track applications in an Excel spreadsheet and use a Word mail merge; this also prevents duplicate applications.

Technical interviews

  • Coding test:
    • ask for clarification (the author doesn't want "yes" people)
    • follow instructions to the letter
    • show consistent, well-thought-out code. Practice: LeetCode, HackerRank, Project Euler, Interview Cake, Cracking the Coding Interview.
  • Discussion: walk through your logic, explain why not just how, name best practices and where learned, be confident, discuss alternative approaches, show you can adapt to different company standards.
  • Don't know? Describe how you'd find the answer (research is the daily job).
  • Stay calm; outline answers beforehand.

Leadership interviews

  • Focus on business goals, budgeting, staffing; abstract technical questions; your leadership approach.
  • Cultivate style: observe project and team managers (meetings, projects, budgets); automate processes to decrease costs.
  • Executive rounds: prepare a slide deck even if not asked (résumé, industry trends, best practices, team successes, not your own); hiring managers want leaders who give credit.
  • Shift gears from technical expert to managing teams; keep a record of successes; use data to predict staffing and mitigate costs.
  • Show leadership in your current job:
    • take responsibility for mistakes
    • be proactive (scheduled maintenance, automated nightly tasks, status reports; track the reduction in emergencies and share it).

2.3 The job market

Assess the competition: research postings (qualifications, pay), analyse descriptions (technologies), track trends in forums, monitor competitors' online presence, attend events, and ask mentors (especially hiring managers) what puts a résumé in the "yes pile".

Stand out

  • Customise the résumé; use action verbs (achieved, built, optimised, automated) and quantify results.
  • Open source: report bugs, write code, submit pull requests; shows teamwork and repository/SDLC understanding; rarely listed on résumés.
  • Blog: explain problems you hit and how you fixed them; doubles as your notebook (the author: https://hoffstech.com/blog/).
  • GitHub: hiring managers search it; the profile has a job-profile and "available for hire" checkbox; host a portfolio site there; a repository for each walkthrough.

In-demand skills

  • Specialise in complementary skills (JavaScript with Angular/React)
    • don't chase trends
    • Stack Overflow's top desired skills for 2024 (https://mng.bz/RNmR)
    • CodinGame top-five in-demand jobs for 2023 (https://mng.bz/2gKo).
  • Full-stack developers are most sought after.
  • Leads need CI/CD and DevOps: configure local, QA, staging and production; cloud (AWS, Azure); Docker and Kubernetes; the whole SDLC.
  • Application developers (Linux, Mac, Windows, iOS, Android; web and mobile).

2.4 Case study: Dan Wahlin (Microsoft principal/director; 20 years as CEO of Wahlin Consulting)

  • Career plan:
    • "Begin with the end in mind" (Stephen Covey, The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People): visualise the role, write the steps, build the plan into your daily routine (practise manager skills now; don't wait for the perfect time), be consistent and persistent
    • roadblocks are muscle-building
      • He visualised managing a team, writing a book, releasing a training course, corporate training worldwide, conference speaking, magazine articles, running a company, and did them through effort, not exceptional talent.
  • Staying competitive:
    • lifelong learning
    • admit you don't know everything (imposter syndrome is normal)
    • treat failures as lessons and share them
    • contribute to the community (open source, pull requests, blog, social, ask your employer to publish your posts)
    • put yourself out there (videos, conference talks, lunch and learns)
    • his first conference "yes" took a few years of rejections
    • ask organisers for feedback or volunteer first.
  • Interviews:
    • smile and be friendly
    • prepare and practise (mock interviews, "I don't know", questions to ask, the "five years" question)
    • some interviews test stress resilience
    • bring a real project (he got a first tech job without the required years by showing one)
    • follow "I don't know" with how you'd find out
    • have related projects, posts, videos ready
    • give effort to take-home projects only for jobs you really want.

Summary points

Path junior → senior → lead; related roles help; show leadership and business skills for management; résumé with objective, summary and skills up top; stand out with certifications, open source, blog, GitHub portfolio; succeed in technical interviews by being detail-oriented, asking questions, explaining why, and staying calm.
