Lead Developer Career Guide 6: Optimizing the development process

Source: Shelley Benhoff, Lead Developer Career Guide (Manning), ch. 6. Previous: Lead Developer Career Guide 5: Writing technical documentation. Next: Lead Developer Career Guide 7: Working with project teams.

Takeaways
  • The development process runs from market research to launch; optimising it brings efficiency, collaboration, quality, project management.
  • Pain points (unclear direction, missing tools or people, falling satisfaction): recognise, prioritise by impact, fix.
  • Receive feedback from developers and non-developers; check your ego.
  • Automate repetitive SDLC work, remove bottlenecks (including yourself, by delegating), prefer preventative over reactionary work.
  • Document the process and the QA process (plan, design, execute, report) and review them on a schedule.

The development process

Six stages (figure 6.1):

  1. Market research: identify a need, gather customer preferences and behaviour.
  2. Design: blueprint of features, functionality, UI.
  3. Prototyping: rough model to test feasibility and get user feedback.
  4. Product development: developers work with the project team to build, test, debug.
  5. Quality assurance: thorough testing against standards.
  6. Launch: initial release, then updates from continuous customer feedback.

Benefits of optimising: improved efficiency, increased collaboration, enhanced quality (fewer bugs), improved project management (tracking and course correction). It also avoids scope creep, delays and miscommunication.

6.1 Identifying opportunities for improvement

  • Optimised organisation:
    • deadlines met on a clear roadmap, on budget, satisfied customers, higher morale, flexibility to respond to market changes
    • Unoptimised: missed deadlines, rushed and under-tested work, lost trust, frustrated team, slow reaction to trends.
  • Author's worst job:
    • management expected project managers and developers to do QA. QA professionals have specific skills
    • without them proper testing isn't followed or documented and "chaos ensues". Where QA existed, problems were fixed before production, business was retained, and top talent was hireable.

Pain points

A pain point is an area of difficulty, frustration or dissatisfaction in a process.

Pain point Symptom Result Solution
Unclear direction or communication lower productivity confusion, miscommunication clear channels, achievable goals, regular meetings
Lack of tools poor-quality products products miss user needs research and invest in the right tools continually
Lack of people demotivated, disengaged team frustration, burnout ongoing training and support; work with project management so developers aren't overworked
Decreased customer satisfaction fewer future opportunities reputation harmed produce high-quality products by addressing all pain points
  • The author most often met unclear communication:
    • miscommunication arises when you think you have no time for important talks or assume everyone is on task
    • You and the project manager must set the lines.
  • Prioritise pain points by their effect; use data and analytics to find them and measure fixes.

Providing ideas

  • Stay up to date (conferences, articles, industry leaders on social media).
  • Embrace change: experiment with tools; lead by example.
  • Collaborate with other teams to learn how they overcame the same challenge.
  • Continuous improvement: retrospectives and improvement workshops.
  • Presenting ideas: state the benefits to the team and organisation, use flowcharts, diagrams and graphs, invite honest feedback, show enthusiasm, follow up with regular updates.

    • experienced developers must review AI-generated code (correctness, security, project requirements)

Managing the process: eight steps

Process management is the systematic design, execution, control and monitoring of development processes.

  1. Define it (requirements gathering, design, coding, testing, deployment) with input from the team, managers, QA and project teams; everyone's expertise is equally valid.
  2. Document it.
  3. Communicate it (meeting, presentation or documentation walkthrough).
  4. Assign roles and responsibilities (the lead isn't solely responsible).
  5. Establish deadlines (calendar, reminders).
  6. Encourage collaboration (introduce people to other teams; contact list of stakeholders).
  7. Use tools: Jira, Trello, Asana, Microsoft Project; version control such as GitHub.
  8. Review and update regularly.

6.2 Receiving feedback

Feedback aligns the product with stakeholders' needs and lets stakeholders see the constraints of the process.

Interviewing stakeholders

Ask developers and non-developers (they understand business requirements, customer needs and fit in the organisation).

Developers Non-developers
What are the biggest challenges developing software? What are the most important considerations when developing software for your team?
What tools or processes would make your job easier? How do you measure the success of software projects?
How do you collaborate with other teams? How do you prioritise competing demands for resources and time?
How do you stay informed about technologies and trends? What are the biggest pain points in the current process?

Constructive criticism

  • Constructive criticism focuses on improving performance; don't take it personally. It reveals weaknesses you haven't noticed and models a culture of continuous improvement.
  • Example the author received: "Your presentation was well-organized and clear, but I think you could improve by incorporating more visuals."
  • She once took criticism as an attack, feared asking questions and was slow
    • a co-worker advised an open mind, and that change led to becoming a lead developer and then a manager. "Constructive criticism is a gift."

Open mind

  • Listen to junior developers (fresh perspectives) and non-developers (project managers, clients, stakeholders).
  • Check your ego: confidence plus humility; it opens collaboration, innovation and your growth as a leader.

6.3 Reviewing the SDLC

  • The development process is the whole journey from idea to launch and beyond
    • the SDLC is the more structured version with phases that ensure quality standards, business objectives and user requirements.
  • Six stages (figure 6.2): planning → analysis → design → implementation → testing → maintenance.
  • Review regularly: keeps it efficient (fewer bottlenecks), uses current methods, stays relevant. Never reviewed: outdated, slow, costly, lower quality, no innovation.

Automation

  • Start by listing repetitive, manual, time-consuming tasks (generating documentation, backups, code reviews) and ask the team.
  • Candidates: code testing and deployment, CI/CD, reporting, backup and recovery.

Removing bottlenecks

A bottleneck is anything that slows or obstructs progress.

  • Lack of resources: work with the project manager (hire, outsource) or revise the plan so no one burns out.
  • Conflicting stakeholder priorities: align priorities in conversations (especially with the project manager) to manage the backlog.
  • Process inefficiencies (outdated methods, manual steps, poor communication): modernise, automate, improve channels.
  • The lead as bottleneck: delegate. Learn your team's skills, trust them, free yourself for critical work, develop their skills, and build a culture of trust and recognition.

Preventative vs reactionary

Approach Focus Effect
Preventative avoid problems: scalable, maintainable, best-practice design reduces technical debt, healthy codebase
Reactionary fix problems as they arise (patch bugs, security holes, refactor) quick fixes but more complex, harder to maintain code
  • Preventative tools: code review, clear documented code, linters and automated tests.
  • Author's example:
    • an organisation preferred reactionary maintenance ("saves time"), so its flagship system kept going down and clients doubted its stability
    • She automated maintenance to run overnight with email alerts, emergencies fell, clients were impressed, and the organisation adopted the approach.

6.4 Maintaining the process

A well-maintained process tracks progress, aids communication, helps find and fix problems early (including through QA), builds stakeholder trust and speeds up onboarding.

Documenting the process

Make a documentation plan (research, technical requirements, implementation; stakeholders like IT support, DevOps, QA), use mixed formats (text, diagrams, code comments).

Phase Document Stakeholders
Concept concept document (goals, objectives, expected outcomes) marketing, project management, lead developer
Research research report (findings, recommendations) marketing, lead developer, legal, project management
Analysis analysis report (approach, steps) marketing, project management, lead developer, UX/UI
Development detailed development docs (steps, code snippets, diagrams) development, DevOps, QA
Launch launch report (process, challenges) development, DevOps, QA, marketing, project management, UX/UI

Documenting QA

  • QA is an asset for suggesting improvements; include them.
  • Combine test types:
    • Unit: automated code-level checks of algorithms and integrations.
    • Integration: interactions between parts of a system.
    • Manual: human perspective; does the UX match the requirements?
  • Four QA stages (figure 6.3, e-commerce example):
    1. Planning: scope, objectives, acceptance criteria, risks, resources (e.g. test functionality, usability, security, performance).
    2. Test design: test cases and criteria, environment (checkout process, browser and device compatibility, concurrent users).
    3. Execution: run and record (Selenium, mobile devices, load-testing tools).
    4. Reporting: document and communicate; prioritise by severity and impact (e.g. checkout failing on some mobile devices: high priority before launch).

Maintenance schedule

  • Schedule regular reviews (monthly or quarterly); involve all team members; document changes and reasons with history (OneDrive or Dropbox); improve continuously.
  • New teams
    • should review often, both during and after deployments
    • Staging can't replicate production 100%, but revising the process documentation before launch avoids launch-day errors.

6.5 Case study: Ryan H. Lewis (staff engineer at Reddit; host of the Building Reddit podcast; author of The Cloud Developer Workbook; led teams at T-Mobile, Expedia, startups)

  • Pain points fall into slowing velocity and lowering morale.
    • Broken deployment pipeline: few automated tests; he added a testing framework and tests, making it resilient. Taking unwanted tasks sets a positive tone.
    • Vague product requirements: push back in planning and set a standard for necessary requirements; do technical design and documentation proactively.
    • Siloed knowledge: pair people from different areas; velocity dips briefly, then enthusiasm and velocity rise.
  • Reducing technical debt
    • It builds up in long-running projects or with poor communication; use retrospectives to surface it.
    • Keep a prioritised mitigation list ordered by impact with effort estimates; slot small chunks into each sprint, negotiating lighter sprints (beginning or end of milestones).
    • Explain WIFM (what's in it for me) to product managers. His example: a homegrown client-side data management solution made development take at least 2x as long
      • it was replaced with a modern open source one.
    • Set standards for story point estimates.
    • On long-term debt, split the team into feature work and debt work and swap every two sprints.
  • Advice
    • Write tests early: integration or end-to-end tests; unit tests have a lower ROI on new projects because internals change.
    • Design docs and doc review at Reddit find "gotchas" before implementation; note areas that often break regression testing.
    • Don't assume your experience is everyone's (especially remote teams); use retrospectives for pain points and velocity.
    • Pair programming (one types, one watches and suggests) prevents debt, surfaces process improvements and builds camaraderie.

Summary points

Lead developers keep the process efficient; pain points (communication, tools, resources) hurt quality; process management is systematic; gather stakeholder perspectives; stay open to criticism and humble; automate with Jenkins or Selenium; prefer preventative practice; document QA (planning, test design, execution, reporting).
