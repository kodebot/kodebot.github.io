Lead Developer Career Guide 8: Speaking with clients

Source: Shelley Benhoff, Lead Developer Career Guide (Manning), ch. 8. Previous: Lead Developer Career Guide 7: Working with project teams. Next: Lead Developer Career Guide 9: Being a mentor.

Takeaways
  • Understand needs with active listening, insightful questions, and end-user interviews; clients often don't know their users' pain points.
  • Separate wants from needs; keep the budget in view; present your approach in the client's language, with visuals and metrics.
  • Trust comes from listening, quality on time and in budget, accountability, and a human connection.
  • Be diplomatic: realistic expectations, positive language, calm body language.
  • Difficult clients: stay calm, let them talk, stay engaged (weekly meetings, dashboards), keep your manager in the loop.

8.1 Understanding clients' needs

  • Understanding needs gives tailored solutions, trust, repeat business and referrals
    • Misunderstanding gives software that misses the mark, wasted time and money on both sides, lost trust and reputation.
  • On failing projects the author checks the gap between written requirements and what the client is asking, talks to the client directly, and listens
    • Clients don't always know what they need, but it's not the lead's job to decide for them: it's collaboration.

Active listening

Fully engage and seek the message, including tone, body language and context.

  • Ask open-ended questions: "Can you tell me more about how you plan to use the software?", "What are your biggest challenges that this software will solve?"
  • Repeat back what you heard.
  • Watch body language: a client who says they're excited but sits closed off and tense may have reservations; address them with empathy.
  • Practice:
    • at the end of every meeting repeat the discussion and the action items, and write meeting notes with the project manager
    • Use all channels (written too), especially with people who are blind or deaf.

Insightful questions

Question Purpose
What are your goals for this project? prioritise the most useful features; think beyond single tasks to the whole project's purpose
What challenges will this project solve? find pain points; often reveals automation opportunities
How do you envision using the software day to day? design a usable interface (e.g. CMS-based sites: users manage pages without developers)
What are your long-term goals and how can this software help? build a solution that grows with the business
What are your expectations for the development process? manage expectations; set reasonable ones and deliver

Follow up: if a project is going badly, meet frequently (the author once had a daily 15 to 30 minute call; others weekly for an hour), keep a checklist of concerns and ask for feedback after each fix.

Interviewing end users

End users may not be on the project team. The author did B2B (met end users personally) and B2C SaaS (millions of users, no contact).

Format Use
Structured predetermined questions; compare answers across interviews
Semi-structured open-ended answers within a general structure; unexpected insights
Unstructured free-flowing; explore in depth
Focus groups small group discussion of experiences
User testing observe use of a prototype for usability and functionality
Surveys fastest; anonymous, so more truthful; starting point
Heatmaps which screens are never used, which paths are taken; leads to targeted questions
  • Author's combination:
    • surveys plus focus groups. A client with exceptional technical skills over-engineered software that non-technical users couldn't follow
    • a focus group with every end user (small B2B group) found discrepancies in use cases
      • Large groups need surveys
      • Without the interviews, mismatched requirements would have failed the project.
  • Prepare, tailor questions, take notes
    • interview the client and users
    • resolve discrepancies in a meeting with the client and project team
    • PMs may present the findings while you explain how the technical approach must change.

8.2 Suggesting technical approaches

A successful presentation builds trust and buy-in; an unsuccessful one confuses the client, makes them doubt feasibility and competence, and can lose projects.

Wants vs needs

  • Listen first
    • to what they want, then translate to what they need (core purpose, functionality, long-term goals; requires understanding business, audience, industry trends)
    • Handle client-proposed solutions carefully.
  • Cautionary tale:
    • at one company managers always implemented clients' proposed solutions
    • systems were over-engineered and not user-friendly, none of the projects succeeded and the company was shut down.
  • Ask probing questions per feature about purpose and intended UX
    • If a client insists on an unnecessary solution, explain drawbacks gently, involve the PM, offer alternatives with pros and cons so they decide informed.
Wants Needs
"Simple" e-commerce website advanced inventory management, order tracking, customer support systems
every cutting-edge technology in a mobile app upgrade to new technologies only as needed
robust loyalty program with login and dashboard a simple login and dashboard, rolling out features gradually
flashy web design a user-friendly interface
custom solutions for one project scalable, sustainable technologies with long-term support and compatibility

Considering the budget

  • Know limits at the start
    • prioritise core features with the PM (e-commerce: secure payment gateways first; explain less obvious priorities like inventory management supporting checkout before search or recommendation widgets)
      • House metaphor: foundation first, then the rest.
  • Avoid over-engineering: it raises development time and ongoing maintenance cost; reuse libraries, frameworks, open source.
  • Anecdote:
    • a client wanted everything custom-built and hated Angular. A previous developer's Angular build had left an unconscious bias
    • after several discussions and researched alternatives weighed together, Angular was chosen over a custom library.
  • Over budget: analyse the causes (scope changes, unexpected problems, inaccurate estimates, resource constraints), brainstorm with the team, re-evaluate priorities, reallocate resources, optimise allocation, or seek more funding.
  • Agile balances budget and quality: small milestones, feedback loops, continuous delivery with automated testing and integration; surfaces budget challenges and scope creep early.

Presenting your case

  • Align first: the author once presented her own ideas to a new client without realising their technical expert had outlined theirs; the client pushed back on almost everything.
  • Use visuals (diagrams, flowcharts), little jargon, real-world examples, metrics from past projects
    • anticipate questions on feasibility, scalability, risks
    • make the client feel heard.
Step Example
Understand your audience a cost-focused client: show resource optimisation and lower expenses
Clearly define the problem compare an e-commerce site to a store where customers abandon carts because of checkout queues
Outline your approach a flowchart showing the before and after effects of each page-load optimisation
Highlight benefits quantify: a 25% decrease in bounce rates and 15% increase in conversions (the book's illustration)
Address potential concerns explain testing and QA; cite similar successful implementations
Use real-world examples past results: reduced load times, engagement, search rankings

She keeps a playbook (questions, frequent concerns, case studies). Example: a non-technical but curious CMS client got flowcharts of the architecture; a tight budget led to an MVP approach; they felt more confident and thanked her constantly.

8.3 Forming relationships

Clients are people. Without a relationship: silos, missed insights, dissatisfaction, no referrals. With one: a unified team, valuable exchange of ideas, a product aligned with the long-term vision.

Establishing trust

  • Listen and consider their ideas (they are the business experts).
  • Taking over a project: ask what they'd like improved, without blaming the previous lead; with a new client ask about pain points on previous projects.
  • Deliver quality on time and in budget:
    • adhere to deadlines, minimise technical debt, communicate frequently (agile), build stable systems with best practices, and schedule regular maintenance
      • Clients often assumed downtime was normal
    • showing regular maintenance prevented it, and they added maintenance time to the schedule and trusted her more. Skipping maintenance is a gamble.
  • Accountability:
    • acknowledge mistakes promptly, take responsibility, find solutions, don't blame or lie
    • being caught in a lie means clients doubt everything. Her biggest pet peeve is developers who never apologise.

Getting to know clients

  • Begin meetings with casual conversation (clients who run: swap favourite races); lunch at a conference; find shared interests.
  • Empathy: acknowledge worries about deadlines and budgets; ask "How are you really?" (she began this in the pandemic; it prompted talks about mental health).
  • Celebrate milestones: virtual happy hour or game night if remote; she prefers travelling for an in-person lunch or dinner because people multitask online.

Remaining diplomatic

  • Manage expectations: transparent about timelines, challenges, constraints; for unrealistic demands, explain limits and offer alternatives.
  • "Extra features in the same timeline": ask why they're a priority and what can be postponed; warn it can affect quality and stability; offer alternatives; quality first.
  • Positive language: optimistic words build trust; negativity breeds negativity ("misery loves company").
  • Reframe (inspired by CBT, cognitive behavioural therapy, a goal-oriented psychotherapy replacing negative thought patterns with balanced ones):
Instead of Say
"We can't do that." "That is an interesting idea; let's talk about how we can accommodate your request."
"You're wrong about how the technology works." "I understand where you're coming from, and you're not alone. Many people are confused by this; let me clarify some details for you."
  • Authentic: don't get angry or impatient; tone must match words; body language: no frowning or crossed arms; sit up straight, shoulders back, neutral expression.

8.4 Dealing with difficult clients

  • Handled well:
    • constructive conversation, healthier relationship
    • Handled badly: strained relations, delays, escalating frustration, contract termination, reputational damage, team demotivation and turnover.
  • Author's example:
    • a rude client systems administrator gave no help
    • she escalated to the PM (initially brushed off), pushed, and got a different administrator
      • Keep your manager in the loop. "As a first-time lead developer, difficult clients can make you feel inadequate"
    • you're not alone.

Staying calm

  • Calm lets you think clearly, find root causes, and not escalate; empathise (their pressures); don't take it personally.
  • Self-awareness:
    • know your triggers, pause, breathe
    • if you can't answer, say you'll research and get back (the client demanding an immediate answer: "I don't want to provide inaccurate information").
  • Support system: team, mentors, colleagues; the author's teams met directly after client meetings to debrief ("we weren't alone").
  • Failure is never one person's fault; confidence from past successes and feedback.

Letting them talk

  • Listen even if you disagree; paraphrase and reflect; find root causes and misconceptions; clarify patiently and align expectations.
  • Her toughest client
    • (unrealistic expectations, strict timeline): "I understand how important this project is to you
    • Let's work together to identify the areas where we can improve and find a solution that meets your expectations." Asking what expectations they faced on their end built trust.
  • When clients can't articulate dissatisfaction, read cues and ask. Starter questions:
    • Can you describe the specific challenges or problems you currently face with the project?
    • What were your initial expectations, and how have they been met or not?
    • Which features or aspects don't meet your requirements? Examples?
    • Is any aspect confusing or unclear?
    • Did you notice any communication gaps?
    • Where has the project deviated from your original vision?
    • What obstacles on your end affected the project?
    • What outcomes were you hoping for, and have they been addressed?
    • Any concerns about timeline, budget or resources?
    • What would be the ideal resolution?

Remaining engaged

  • Clear lines of communication:
    • the author schedules a weekly meeting with difficult clients
    • proactive status updates
    • transparency ("I appreciate you bringing this to my attention. Let's discuss it openly to find the best course of action together.").
  • Client dashboard with progress reports (completed milestones, upcoming tasks, roadblocks, risks).
  • Unrealistic demands:
    • "While I understand the importance of these features, implementing them within the given time frame may compromise the project's stability
    • How about we prioritize the most critical ones and plan for the others in subsequent phases?"
  • Don't avoid them: avoidance causes more conflict; have the difficult conversations with an open mind.

8.5 Case study: Jamie Maguire (independent software architect; Microsoft MVP in AI; author; founder of Social Opinion and Daily Tracker; www.jamiemaguire.net)

  • Establishing trust:
    • be active online (blog, LinkedIn, Twitter) for social proof
    • accreditations, public GitHub repos, his SaaS and YouTube demos
    • in consultations understand the ask, reference similar solutions at a high level, turn the camera on for remote calls
    • say "I don't know, but we can find out"
    • give code or video demos of similar prototypes
    • deliver what you promise, when you promise.
  • Out-of-scope requests: politely remind them it wasn't in the statement of work; add to the backlog and price it later; small unforeseen requests can be accommodated.
  • Presenting an approach:
    • concise, simple language
    • introduce yourself, play back the client's problem, list pain points and requirements, then solution, benefits and value
    • identify risks, third-party costs, cross-team or data dependencies and "unknown unknowns"
    • diagrams or short demos.
  • Difficult clients: find another way to explain; for contractual issues refer to the contract and original statement of work.

Summary points

Understanding needs via active listening; budget balancing with core features and lean approaches; trust through listening, transparency and timely quality; diplomacy via realistic expectations and alternatives to unrealistic demands.
