Lead Developer Career Guide 9: Being a mentor

Source: Shelley Benhoff, Lead Developer Career Guide (Manning), ch. 9. Previous: Lead Developer Career Guide 8: Speaking with clients. Next: Lead Developer Career Guide 10: Taking the lead.

Takeaways
  • A mentor voluntarily shares experience and guidance; a trainer teaches specific skills in a structured way. Mentoring is broader, ongoing, and two-way.
  • Good mentors: empathy, constructive feedback, lead by example, make introductions. Bad advice is worse than no advice.
  • Build trust: set expectations, be authentic, never white-lie, respect confidentiality, be patient; get to know mentees as people.
  • Career plan: short-, mid-, long-term goals, revisited regularly; assess skills via code review, assessments, pair programming, stakeholder feedback.
  • Pay it forward: turn mentees into mentors, mentor outside your organisation, join mentoring communities.

The author has mentored 100+ people (colleagues and the global community) and says she learns from every mentee.

9.1 What is a mentor?

A mentor is an experienced person who voluntarily gives guidance, support and advice to a less experienced one: a trusted advisor, role model and source of inspiration. Mentors shape skills, knowledge and mindset.

Approach Good mentor Bad mentor
Practising empathy builds trust and a supportive environment ignores concerns, offers generic advice
Constructive feedback guides improvement demeans mentees
Leading by example positive environment where mentees thrive allows toxic behaviour
Facilitating networking introduces contacts, events, communities limits opportunities
  • Empathy: without it a mentor dismisses concerns, pushes their own agenda; "no advice is better than bad advice" because it can derail a mentee's path.
  • Feedback: specific and actionable, acknowledge accomplishments, challenge and support, encourage stepping out of comfort zones.
  • Inspire by actions: share knowledge generously. Bad mentors hoard knowledge, often when a mentee surpasses them; a mentee outgrowing the mentor means the mentor did their job.
  • Style takes time; missteps are fine.

Mentors vs trainers

Mentor Trainer
Passes on experience and wisdom; inspiration knowledge, expertise, practical skills
Scope comprehensive personal and professional development narrow skill sets or domains
Method consistent meetings, real-time problem solving, no strict timetable structured instruction, programmes, hands-on exercises
Contact ongoing usually none after the course
  • Story:
    • she joined a highly structured, training-centred mentoring programme (about 30 mentors, low mentee enrolment)
      • She suggested prioritising career advice over training and flexibility
    • mentees thrived and enrolment grew by word of mouth.
  • The roles intersect: mentors add training elements; trainers form mentoring relationships.

Mentoring expectations

  • Share wisdom from successes and failures, but caveat that things change and the outcome may differ; mentees forge their own path (don't copy 100%).
  • Safe space to discuss aspirations, concerns, goals; listen, ask thought-provoking questions, give feedback; guidance spans technical advice and career strategy.
  • Story, overcoming failure:
    • she was hired to migrate a desktop app to the web
    • every other developer left and wasn't replaced
    • her requests for help were denied
    • the project went over budget and failed, and she was let go and devastated. A mentor told her it wasn't her fault: high turnover is the biggest sign of mismanagement
    • start job-hunting when you see it. It made her resilient
    • mentees (layoffs are common) relate.
  • Resources and advocacy:
    • suggest learning resources, open opportunities, introduce connections, advise on promotions and leadership roles
    • stress soft skills (mentee feedback on this inspired the book).
  • Trust and confidentiality; mentees are often shy at first.
  • Growth mindset: failure is a stepping stone ("if you learn from it, it's not truly a failure"); share your failures to build authenticity.

Achieving success as a mentor

  • Lead by example: strong work ethic, lifelong learning; be honest about bad days, but don't take them out on anyone ("bad behaviour trickles downward").
  • Tailor to each mentee:
    • some want hands-on coaching, others pointers for independent learning
    • Assess learning style by asking (what worked before, strengths and weaknesses), then observe (visual, auditory, kinesthetic).
  • Empower independence:
    • delegate gradually, trust them to find solutions, guide their thinking rather than feed answers
    • Her training background made this hard: her first mentee felt overwhelmed because she gave too much information at once.
  • Growth opportunities:
    • conferences, workshops, open source (give on-the-job time if budget allows; personal time slows progress)
    • recommend books, courses, tutorials for their stack
    • advocate for projects and promotions
    • if there is no upward mobility, help them plan the next move, even outside the organisation (some organisations measure leaders by retention, a poor practice).
  • Personal well-being:
    • interest in life outside work
    • guidance on work-life balance, stress, self-care
    • celebrate achievements
      • Story: a mentee afraid of public speaking (bad school experiences) was supported and celebrated after a first conference talk, even though it wasn't perfect, then applied for more.

9.2 Forming relationships with mentees

  • Foundation:
    • trust, rapport, a non-judgemental safe space
    • be accessible and reliable. The author promises confidentiality
    • if she must reschedule she gives notice and uses email or chat instead.
  • Without relationships:
    • mentees hesitate to ask, causing misunderstandings and delays
    • feel disconnected and disengage
    • poor knowledge transfer
    • for teams, lack of cohesion, silos, duplicated effort, lower morale, higher turnover.

Establishing trust

  • Communicate expectations early; mentees help set goals, outcomes and commitment.
  • Be authentic and transparent: share successes and failures; "no one starts out being perfect".
  • No white lies,
    • including bad news. Story: an early mentor reviewed and approved her code then changed it later, telling her it was excellent
    • she felt uneasy and later realised it prevented growth. Better: use comments on pull or merge requests to teach best practices.
  • Show competence while letting mentees explore; she lets team members shadow her coding.
  • Be patient with mistakes; help analyse problems.
  • Confidentiality:
    • make it explicit, especially for mentees in your team (divorce, illness)
    • don't push them to disclose. Story: a mentor repeated her personal information to mutual friends
    • she ended the relationship.

Getting to know mentees

  • Regular one-on-ones; ask about short- and long-term aspirations. Starter questions:
    • How do you manage work-life balance, and what strategies keep you well?
    • What are your hobbies outside software development?
    • What role does your support network play in your development?
    • How do you recharge and care for your mental and physical wellbeing?
    • Which life skills or personal development areas do you want to explore?
    • How do you prioritise time for activities or relationships outside work?
  • Team-building:
    • retreats, picnics, conferences
    • With a fully remote team meeting in person for a week she took everyone to lunch daily and banned work talk (books, movies, hobbies, current events).

Finding common ground

  • Shared interests relax communication
    • Experience: early leads who never got to know her or held regular meetings misdiagnosed her problems (they didn't understand her educational background)
    • leads who did understood her thinking.
  • Social media: follow mentees on GitHub, offer help, congratulate progress at work, comment supportively on LinkedIn: public support shows care.

9.3 Inspiring personal and career growth

Positive examples motivate; set clear goals yourself to help mentees set theirs; prioritising growth builds a culture where growth is shared, lifting morale and productivity.

Making a career plan

  • Gives clarity and direction, motivation, ownership
    • Interests change (burnout, specialist vs generalist): encourage learning every skill they're interested in and prioritise by goals.
  • Align with organisational needs (promotions, projects, job rotations) and create a talent pipeline, reducing disruption from attrition.
  • Example plan for aspiring lead developer (figure 9.1):
    • Short-term (1 to 2 years): certification or training; lead a small project or feature; work with cross-functional teams to see the whole SDLC.
    • Mid-term (3 to 5 years): expertise in a technology or domain; own larger multi-team projects; mentor juniors.
    • Long-term (5+ years): technical lead role guiding and mentoring a team; contribute to architectural decisions and strategic planning; keep learning emerging technologies.
  • Author's story:
    • a mentor saw her interest in leadership and set goals (running meetings, writing project plans, meeting other teams)
    • when a team lead role opened she realised she was already doing the work and got the job.
  • Review regularly:
    • celebrate successes, give feedback, adjust. When she lost a promotion to senior web developer, a mentor suggested technical writing or training
    • she added instructional design and writing and completed several courses within a year.

Identifying skills to improve

  • Technical assessment:
    • code reviews (standards, efficiency, maintainability)
    • technical assessments or quizzes (concepts, algorithms, data structures, languages)
    • pair programming (problem solving, organisation, debugging).
  • Feedback from colleagues, teammates, project managers (objectives, deadlines, complexity), clients and stakeholders (deliverable quality).
  • Soft skills: conveying ideas, listening, collaborating, decision making, ability to guide others, organisation, meeting deadlines.
  • Match to goals: the skills for a desired role or specialisation, with relevant projects or training.

Being a role model

  • Attention to detail: pre-empt errors (e.g. suggest a missing test case and align with QA).
  • Continuous learning: share resources and your own personal development (she focuses on self-awareness).
  • Communication: model active listening; explain concisely; treat non-technical colleagues respectfully, answering in plain language.
  • Collaboration: code reviews, knowledge-sharing sessions, acknowledge team success (without it some felt unappreciated).
  • Work ethic and accountability: meet deadlines, be punctual, tell people of calendar conflicts, own mistakes.
  • Work-life balance:
    • breaks, boundaries, delegation, personal interests. The author was a workaholic because her leaders were (checking work on vacation)
    • burnout made her make "silly little mistakes" until she took time off. "Your actions speak louder than words."

9.4 Paying it forward

Empowering mentees to become mentors

  • Readiness: strong mastery, can explain concepts, passion for teaching.
  • Benefits:
    • reinforces knowledge, uncovers gaps, new perspectives
    • a continuous learning cycle
    • builds leadership, emotional intelligence and communication
    • job satisfaction and loyalty
    • a mentor pipeline for succession.
  • Seniors
    • should mentor even if not aiming to lead
    • juniors can too ("there is always someone less skilled than you in a particular area"; many already mentor via personal projects and blogs).
  • It ensures the community has a continuous supply of mentors.

Mentoring outside your organisation

  • Spreads expertise
    • supports diversity, equity and inclusion by reaching underrepresented groups and people with limited access to networks
    • broadens your perspectives
    • lasting networks and friendships.
  • She mentors only people who ask, otherwise she'd have no time; many of her 100+ mentees are now mentors.

Mentoring communities and events

Benefits: refine teaching and communication, build network, fulfilment, self-reflection.

Community or event Description
Meetup.com developer meetups worldwide
Dev.to online community with mentorship programmes
GitHub open source; mentorship programmes (Campus Experts, GitHub Mentorship)
Codementor connects developers with mentors for specific challenges or career guidance
Women Who Code global nonprofit for women in tech: events, workshops, mentorship
CodeNewbie inclusive community for learners and career changers: Twitter spaces, podcasts, virtual meetups
Developer conferences Google I/O, Apple WWDC, Microsoft Build: workshops, networking, mentorship

Her experience: a Women Who Code panel on personal branding for developers brought many career-guidance messages; she tries to answer every message and tells people she's available, otherwise they may assume she has no time.

9.5 Case study: Steve Buchanan (principal program manager; 8 technical books; former 10-time Microsoft MVP; www.buchatech.com)

  • Achievements: mostly short-term mentoring, now more long-term with a career planning focus; mentees became Microsoft MVPs, authors and speakers.
  • Career plan:
    • the industry changes quickly
    • also mentors on getting through layoffs. Step back, find the goal, work backwards
    • study people who already do what you want
    • plot the path in Microsoft Visio (e.g. "expert in identity or security: what will it take?").
  • Events:
    • spoke at Blacks in Tech (Minnesota) on personal branding and at OSCON (O'Reilly) on leading technical teams
    • plans courses on managing your career, building a brand, leading technical teams.
  • Advice for first-time mentors:
    • mentees are human and prone to error
    • research "what does it take to be a good mentor"
    • 90%+ of ownership is on the mentee: ask them to set a duration and schedule and what they want from it
    • check buy-in
    • **learn from mentees too
    • have several mentors yourself** because no mentor suits every need.

Summary points

Mentors share wisdom, tailor guidance, help set goals, build trust through transparency; successful mentors lead by example and empower independent decisions; trust needs authenticity, technical competence, patience and confidentiality; a career plan with milestones creates clarity and a talent pipeline; role models show attention to detail, accountability and balance.
