Lead Developer Career Guide 10: Taking the lead

Source: Shelley Benhoff, Lead Developer Career Guide (Manning), ch. 10. Previous: Lead Developer Career Guide 9: Being a mentor. Next: Lead Developer Career Guide 11: Leading with emotional intelligence.

Takeaways
  • Take the lead: set vision, decide, mentor. Without it communication breaks down, silos form, decisions stall.
  • Instructions: confident, clear, concise, specific; keep an FAQ.
  • Feedback: specific and genuine praise; negative feedback private, constructive, with "I" statements, with follow-up; the sandwich technique; plan sessions.
  • Emergencies: assess, define scope, refer to the plan, communicate, decide; stay positive, delegate, write an emergency plan in advance.
  • Confidence: know strengths and weaknesses, stop comparing, challenge negative thoughts.

Why lead

Benefits (figure 10.1):

  • Facilitating communication and collaboration: open channels, knowledge sharing, innovation.
  • Decision making and problem solving: a holistic view lets leads weigh options and remove roadblocks.
  • Mentorship and skill development: guide, create growth opportunities.

Without leadership (figure 10.2): limited direction (confusion, poor decisions, no accountability); communication and collaboration problems (silos, misalignment, unspoken concerns); slow decisions (excess deliberation, no consensus); limited mentoring for juniors.

Author's example: a lead started production deployments and left the team to handle problems with no training or playbook and was unresponsive: long deploys and outages that upset clients.

Not everyone aspires to lead; individual contributor roles are valid.

10.1 Providing instructions

Outcome Clear instructions Unclear
Alignment clear objectives and tasks ambiguity, confusion
Efficiency prioritisation, completion delays, wasted effort
Quality standards followed inconsistencies, rework
Confidence empowerment uncertainty, hesitancy
Communication smooth collaboration misunderstandings, conflicts
Productivity progress stalls
Accountability defined roles no ownership

Scenario (illustrative): tight deadlines, scope creep, several undocumented API integrations. A lead who says "figure it out" gets integrations against best practices and the same questions repeated. A lead who communicates requirements and timelines, encourages collaboration and documents everything gets better quality and confidence.

Speaking with confidence

  • Confidence carries authority and trust
    • doubt makes people hesitate to follow
      • Tip: replace "I might be wrong, but shouldn't we use the latest version of the framework?" with "I think we should use the latest version of the framework."
  • Author's early mistake:
    • late project, the PM asked for a path
    • she presented options but couldn't decide and spent weeks studying each, then used the first (the industry standard; others were experimental)
      • She should have trusted her instincts.
  • Trust your instincts through experience, learning and self-awareness; instinct complements data (e.g. an alternative approach hunch that beats the deadline).
  • Hesitant communication breeds a culture of doubt; confident communication invites open dialogue.

Clear and concise

  • Use plain language (non-native speakers), no jargon, idioms or colloquialisms; be specific; get to the point; also think through the task from start to after it's finished.
  • Example:
    • "Develop a RESTful API endpoint that retrieves user data from the user authentication SQL database via a secure connection
    • The endpoint should accept a GET request at '/user/{id}' and return the user's name and previous orders in JSON format
    • Make sure to handle error cases, including invalid IDs or database connection failures." Include table, view and method names where known.
  • Tips (figure 10.3): active voice; short sentences and paragraphs; numbered lists or bullets; screenshots or diagrams; proofread.
  • Author's change:
    • "Add a new user" became "To add a new user, open the user management page, and click the Add User button
    • Enter the user's name, email address, and password
    • Click the Save button." (Most instructions she saw were too short.) Think from the developer's knowledge level.

FAQs

  • Find questions via meetings, support tickets, surveys. Example: a client kept asking about data security on Jira tasks
    • she raised it in a meeting and added it to the Confluence FAQ, cutting back-and-forth.
  • Tools: internal: Confluence (integrates with Jira), Zendesk, Basecamp; external: a CMS, Document360, ProProfs.
  • Organise
    • by topic, question type or product feature ("How to create a new account", "How to log in", "How to reset my password")
    • organised public FAQs reduced support tickets and raised morale.
  • Keep them updated.

10.2 Giving feedback

Effective feedback (figure 10.5):

  • Right time and place: negative feedback in private, one on one; public positive feedback is fine but don't always praise the same person (jealousy).
  • Well-timed: soon after the event, before the project ends; regular sessions (code reviews, one-on-ones, team meetings).
  • Be open to feedback yourself.
  • Cultural differences: the author is blunt, which some cultures see as unprofessional; rushing feedback caused dissension. Use humour to ease tension; don't rush.

Positive feedback

  • Be specific and genuine: instead of "Great job", "I'm impressed at how you solved that complex problem. Your attention to detail and problem-solving skills are exceptional."
  • Foster a growth mindset: praise effort and growth; offer resources or responsibilities.
  • Encourage peer recognition.
  • Tip: keep a feedback journal per team member to recall specifics at performance reviews.
  • Story: she let a developer who liked leading meetings co-write the agenda, shadow her, then run them; teammates gave feedback and the team grew close.

Negative feedback

  • Be constructive (how to improve, offer help and resources), respectful (they may disagree), listen to their perspective, end on a positive note.
  • Follow up: schedule meetings to check progress, offer mentorship, training or assignments.
  • Use "I" statements:
    • "I'm concerned about the quality of your code
      • I've noticed a few errors
      • I recommend taking more time to test your code locally before submitting it so that you can catch any errors
      • I'm happy to provide guidance when you need it
    • just ask."
  • Bad example: a manager criticised her in the hallway within earshot of the team (embarrassment, hurt confidence).
  • Tip: don't invite someone else to the meeting to avoid doing it; it feels like ganging up. Learn by recalling negative feedback you received and how it felt.

Planning feedback sessions

  • Define objectives (e.g. for missed deadlines: find root causes and agree a time-management plan).
  • Time and setting: private, comfortable, minimal distractions; adapt to their style (coffee break); remote: video, in a relaxed environment (she calls from her Florida porch).
  • Sandwich technique (praise, critique, praise):
    1. Open with praise: "Your attention to detail and problem-solving skills were outstanding. The code you wrote was clean, efficient, and well-documented."
    2. Critique with an example and action: "...during the last sprint, there was some confusion about the project timeline and requirements. To enhance collaboration, I suggest actively engaging in team discussions and providing regular updates."
    3. Close positively: "Despite the communication challenges, I have no doubt that you have the skills and potential to excel in teamwork..."
  • Outline the flow and steer back if it drifts; reassure those who apologise too much that feedback is your job.

10.3 Handling emergencies

  • A leader gives clear communication channels, central contact, less panic, quick decisions. Bad example: when a website went down, the lead stayed silent
    • the team scrambled, duplicated work, made more errors, and took very long to fix it.
  • Five phases (figure 10.6):
    • assess severity → define scope → refer to the emergency plan → communicate with the team → make critical decisions. Gather impact on systems, users, stakeholders
    • establish channels (team chat, open conference line)
    • update continually
    • weigh options, risks, long-term effect.

Remaining positive

  • Lead by example
    • (composure, optimism: "we have the expertise")
    • foster a supportive environment
    • communicate (regular updates)
    • focus on solutions (break into tasks, celebrate small wins, growth mindset)
    • practise self-care (breaks, balance).
  • She reassures teams they're capable and says she needs their input because people go quiet when overwhelmed.
  • Story:
    • ten hours into a failing production deployment she grew irritable and short with colleagues
    • a teammate suggested a break while QA tested, and a 15-minute break to eat and stretch helped
      • She advised self-care but hadn't followed it: "take care of yourself before you can support the success of others."

Bringing the team together

  • Team spirit: she once had to deploy alone and others' check-ins lifted morale.
  • Story:
    • a database administrator accidentally deleted data that user accounts depended on
    • redundant backups also partly failed
      • She assigned one person to gather backups, another to add a maintenance alert to the user dashboard, and coordinated development, DBAs and the PM
      • Some data couldn't be recovered
    • the team found the cause and put processes in place.
  • Delegate:
    • distribute workload by expertise and dependencies
    • she delegates stakeholder communication to the project manager so she can stay heads down
    • delegation shows trust and raises confidence.

Outlining a plan

A plan reduces downtime, clarifies roles, and tailors scenarios (failures, security breaches, data loss, natural disasters). Communication section:

  • Roles and responsibilities (developers, system administrators, project managers).
  • Primary and backup channels (email groups, chat, emergency numbers).
  • Up-to-date contact information in a central document and email signatures.
  • A communication protocol:
    • how and when to report, update, escalate
    • timebox updates (every half hour or hour by severity)
    • escalate critical errors to a team, not an individual.

Decision-making and response

  • A framework to prioritise actions and resources; document each decision and its rationale; reassess as information arrives.
  • Incident response:
    • notify the lead immediately (and PMs and key stakeholders as needed)
    • non-technical emergencies too (natural disaster, mass layoff, company shutdown; her PM acted as counsellor and helped her find another job).
  • Steps:
    • assess severity and scope
    • step-by-step procedures per emergency type
    • identify critical systems
    • delegate by expertise
    • be the point of contact
    • give stakeholders expected resolution time and needed actions
    • document the incident.
  • After:
    • root-cause analysis, feedback, lessons learned, update the plan, tell the team, and have new developers review the plan during onboarding

10.4 Improving your confidence

  • Set achievable goals in milestones; embrace challenges; analyse failure causes (often you weren't set up for success).
  • Empower and mentor your team; the author feels proud seeing mentees post new jobs or certifications. Some self-doubt keeps you humble.

Strengths and weaknesses

  • Confidence ≠ perfection. Evaluate technical strengths (projects led, challenges overcome) and leadership and communication (clear instructions, constructive feedback, listening).
  • Her weakness: avoiding conflict, which breeds more conflict; she made a professional development plan with training.
  • Gather team feedback
    • in one-on-ones (two-way). Story: promoted above her team, she took negative feedback as jealousy
    • later she realised she was internalising her own self-doubt and improved by listening.

Avoid comparison

  • Celebrate your own strengths; limit social media (curated, idealised); she shares struggles publicly and tells people not to compare themselves to her.
  • Join supportive communities and find mentors
    • self-compassion (treat yourself as you'd treat a friend)
    • her mantra, attributed in the book to Walt Disney, "Keep moving forward" (attribution per the book, unverified).

Challenging negative thoughts

  • Recognise patterns, test validity against evidence, reframe: "I'm not skilled enough for this task" becomes "I may face challenges, but I have the knowledge and experience to overcome them."
  • Her pattern:
    • blaming herself for system failures or turnover, rooted in being a scapegoat for poor leaders
    • no one should blame one person for a team failure. Talk it through with mentors.
  • Intrusive thoughts
    • (figure 10.9): recognise they're normal
    • challenge and reframe
    • mindfulness and grounding exercises (meditation, deep breathing)
    • seek professional support such as cognitive behavioural therapy (the figure labels it "CST", which looks like a typo for CBT).
  • Surround yourself with supportive colleagues, avoid workplace toxicity, build a support system.

10.5 Case study: Deborah Kurata (developer, team lead, manager; founded a consultancy that peaked at 30 consultants; Microsoft MVP; Google Developer Expert in Angular)

  • Readiness:
    • she "fell into it" with no mentor and learnt by failing
    • She used "manage by walking around" (daily check-ins, design and debugging chats) before standups
    • The challenge was getting her own work done (coming early, staying late, closed-door lunch hours failed; delegating helped a bit).
  • Negative feedback:
    • First ask whether to give it at all ("good" is often "good enough").
    • Schedule it as a "check in", not a vague "we need to talk" that makes them sweat.
    • Open positive and specific, then state the issue (e.g. the client was surprised to see them playing a game on a laptop in the meeting, which seemed disrespectful), don't go on, let them respond and find how to improve.
    • Don't clean up their messes (don't redo code or documentation): guide them.
  • Emergencies:
    • 9/11 (2001): everyone around one computer, little bandwidth
    • sent people home at lunch
    • she felt in shock. A mass layoff after a buyout moved operations from Silicon Valley to Los Angeles
    • nobody would relocate
    • she let the team vent through the stages of grief (denial, anger, frustration) while completing the transition
    • she was five months pregnant and was rehired as a consultant to finish it.
  • Advice:
    • focus outward ("What can I do for the team?"), help without doing someone's job
    • Her consulting experience: a project lead silently rewrote her documentation ("Why am I even here?")
    • Leaders must give feedback rather than redo work
    • **"Leading a team isn't like getting an award
    • It's more like getting a puppy."** Give it a try, learn from mistakes, don't give up.

Summary points

Clear, concise instructions prevent confusion; "I" statements soften negative feedback; the sandwich technique balances praise and critique; stay positive in emergencies; an emergency plan minimises downtime and enables communication and decisions; confidence comes from self-assessment and avoiding comparison.
