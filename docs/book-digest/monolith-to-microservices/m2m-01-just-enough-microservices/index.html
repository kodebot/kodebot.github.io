Monolith to Microservices 1: Just Enough Microservices

Source: Sam Newman, Monolith to Microservices: Evolutionary Patterns to Transform Your Monolith (O'Reilly, 2019), chapter 1. Next: Monolith to Microservices 2: Planning a Migration.

Takeaways
  • Microservices are independently deployable services modelled around a business domain. They talk over networks and hide their own data storage behind well-defined interfaces.
  • The one idea to keep: independent deployability. Release a change to one microservice into production without deploying anything else, as your normal habit.
  • Group code by cohesion of business functionality, not of technology; three-tier architectures follow how teams used to be grouped (Conway's law).
  • Reduce four kinds of coupling: implementation, temporal, deployment, domain. Never share databases unless you really must.
  • A monolith is a unit of deployment and a valid choice, not a synonym for legacy. Use bounded contexts and aggregates from domain-driven design to find service boundaries.

1.1 What are microservices?

  • Independently deployable services modelled around a business domain, communicating over a network, so they are a form of distributed system. They encapsulate data storage and expose data through well-defined interfaces
    • databases are hidden inside the service boundary.
  • A type of service-oriented architecture (SOA) that is opinionated about how boundaries are drawn and that independent deployability is key. Microservices are technology agnostic.

Independent deployability

  • Change a microservice and deploy it to production without using any other service; and this is how you actually manage deployments, a discipline practised for the bulk of releases.
The book's one takeaway

Embrace independent deployability. Get into the habit of releasing a change to a single microservice into production without deploying anything else; many good things follow.

  • It requires loose coupling:
    • explicit, well-defined, stable contracts between services
    • Sharing databases is especially problematic
    • The wish for loosely coupled services with stable interfaces guides how to find service boundaries.

Modelled around a business domain

  • A change across a process boundary is expensive: two services changed and deployed in order take more work than one change. So make cross-service changes as rare as possible.
  • The book's fake company is Music Corp, which sells CDs. Its current system is a three-tier architecture: web UI, monolithic backend, database, each owned by a different team.

  • Example change:
    • let customers choose a favourite genre
    • It needs a UI change, a backend change and a database change, managed by three teams and deployed in the right order (Figures 1-1 and 1-2).
  • The architecture is not bad
    • It is common because it is universal and because of Conway's law: "Any organization that designs a system..
    • will inevitably produce a design whose structure is a copy of the organization's communication structure" (Melvin Conway, How Do Committees Invent?)
    • Teams were grouped by competency (database administrators, Java developers, frontend developers), so the assets were too.
  • The forces have changed:
    • poly-skilled teams, fewer hand-offs, faster shipping
    • In the three-tier layout business functionality is spread across all tiers: high cohesion of technology, low cohesion of business functionality
    • Choose cohesion of business functionality instead.
  • Alternative (Figure 1-3):
    • a Customer service with a thin slice of UI, logic and storage
    • the favourite genre change is local. The genre list comes from a Catalog service, and a new Recommendation service could read the favourite genre in a later release.

Own their own data

  • Microservices should not share databases
    • A service that wants another service's data asks that service, which decides what to share and maps internal detail onto a stable public contract.
  • Slices of business functionality should encapsulate UI, logic and data where appropriate: high cohesion, lower coupling. Chapter 4 covers splitting monolithic databases.
Database advice

Do not share databases unless you really have to, and even then do everything to avoid it. It is among the worst things for independent deployability.

1.2 Advantages and problems

  • Advantages:
    • independent deployments open new models for scale and robustness
    • mix technologies
    • more developers can work in parallel without colliding
    • developers can understand their part more easily
    • process isolation allows different languages, styles, platforms or databases
      • Above all, flexibility: more options for solving future problems
      • None of it is free, and what you want from the architecture drives how you decompose.
  • Problems:
    • networks. Latency is far higher than in-process calls and varies
    • networks fail (lost packets, cables)
      • Transactions, easy in a single process, become so hard that at scale you will likely drop them for other techniques with different trade-offs
      • Any call can fail and the service you call may be offline or misbehaving
    • getting a consistent view of data across machines is hard
    • and new microservice-friendly technology can help you make mistakes faster and more expensively. Newman's line: they seem a terrible idea, except for all the good stuff.
  • Almost all "monoliths" are also distributed systems (process, database, browser: at least three machines)
    • the difference is the extent. James Lewis: "Microservices buy you options", at a cost you must decide is worth it (Chapter 2).
  • User interfaces: do not make only the server side microservices and leave the UI a monolithic blob; break it apart too (Chapter 3).
  • Technology:
    • resist adopting lots of new technology with the architecture
    • You do not need Kubernetes, Docker, containers, the public cloud, Go or Rust; use a stack you know (the book says PHP is fine) and change things to solve problems as you meet them
    • Beware contempt culture towards technology stacks.
  • Size:
    • "how big should a microservice be" is the most common question and one of the least interesting
    • Lines of code is meaningless (25 lines of Java may be 10 of Clojure)
    • The nearest useful idea is Chris Richardson's goal of "as small an interface as possible"
    • Size is contextual
    • Instead ask how many microservices you can handle (hence incremental migration) and how to define boundaries to avoid a coupled mess.
History of the term "microservices"

In 2011, at ThoughtWorks, Newman's colleague James Lewis became interested in what he called "micro-apps": companies using SOA optimised for replaceable services that could be rewritten in other stacks if needed. Lewis said services should be "no bigger than my head". In 2012 at an architectural summit they decided these were not self-contained applications and settled on "microservices" (unhyphenated, which Newman insisted on).

And ownership

  • Traditional IT keeps development apart from the part of the business that sets requirements and meets customers (Figure 1-4)
    • Technology companies merge the silos: product owners sit in delivery teams aligned to customer-facing product lines, and central IT exists to support them (Figure 1-5).
  • Domain-aligned services make this shift easier and reduce shared services, which is key to minimising delivery contention.

1.3 The monolith

  • "Monolith" here means primarily a unit of deployment: all functionality must be deployed together. Three types:
    1. Single-process monolith (Figure 1-6): all code in one process, perhaps several instances. Usually reads and stores data in a database, so it is already a simple distributed system. This is the type the book focuses on.
      • Modular monolith
        • (Figure 1-7): the process consists of separate modules worked on independently but deployed together. A good choice for many organisations if module boundaries are well defined
        • it allows parallel work while avoiding distributed complexity
          • Shopify is an example (Kirsten Westeinde's talk is cited)
          • Challenge: the database tends not to be decomposed like the code
          • Some teams decompose the database along module lines (Figure 1-8), which is hard to do to an existing monolith
        • the Chapter 4 patterns help.
    2. Distributed monolith: several services that must be deployed together. It has the disadvantages of distributed systems and of monoliths without enough upside of either, and usually arises from too little focus on information hiding and cohesion. Leslie Lamport's definition of a distributed system is quoted: one in which the failure of a computer you did not know existed can render your own unusable.
    3. Third-party black-box systems (payroll, CRM, HR, SaaS): you cannot change the code, but many decomposition techniques still apply.
  • Challenges:
    • vulnerability to coupling (implementation and deployment coupling)
    • many people in one place get in each other's way (same code, different release timing, unclear ownership and decision rights): delivery contention. A microservice architecture gives concrete boundaries to draw ownership around, but neither guarantees nor prevents it.
  • Advantages:
    • simpler deployment topology avoiding distributed pitfalls
    • simpler developer workflows
    • easier monitoring, troubleshooting and end-to-end testing
    • simpler code reuse (copy, library or service in a distributed system, but just use it in a monolith).
  • A monolith is a choice, and a valid one, not synonymous with legacy. Denigrating it risks not serving ourselves or our users well. Chapter 3 gives tools to assess what fits.

1.4 Coupling and cohesion

  • Constantine's law:
    • "A structure is stable if cohesion is high, and coupling is low" (Larry Constantine)
    • Coupling: changing one thing requires changing another
    • Cohesion: how related code is grouped
    • The monolith is often the opposite of both: unrelated code stuck together, and any change means redeploying everything.
  • In distributed systems the cost of structural change is high (several independently deployable services, breaking contract changes), so aim for stable structure and stable contracts, which independent deployability needs.
A brief history

Constantine outlined coupling and cohesion in 1968 at the National Symposium on Modular Programming, the same conference where Conway's law got its name; Structured Design (Constantine and Yourdon, 1979) followed. 1968 also gave the NATO conferences from which software engineering rose, a term previously coined by Margaret H. Hamilton.

  • Cohesion: "the code that changes together, stays together". If invoice approval changes, you want to change as few services as possible.
  • Coupling comes in types needing different solutions. Newman presents his own categorisation, not an exhaustive one (prior art by Meyer, Yourdon and Constantine).
Information hiding

Outlined by David Parnas in 1971 while defining module boundaries (the better-known 1972 paper is often cited as the source; "The Secret History of Information Hiding" is recommended). Separate the parts that change often from the stable ones; keep the boundary stable and hide what is likely to change, so internal changes are safe while compatibility holds. Newman's practice: expose as little as possible; once something is in the interface it is hard to take back, but if it is hidden now you can share it later. OO encapsulation (bundling fields and methods, hiding with visibility) is related but not identical.

Implementation coupling

  • A is coupled to how B is implemented
    • when B's implementation changes, A changes. Often the most pernicious form but the easiest to reduce, since implementation detail is an arbitrary developer choice that may change.
  • Classic example:
    • sharing a database
    • In Figure 1-9 the Recommendation service reads the Order service's tables directly, so renaming a column or splitting the Customer Order table breaks it, as it is tied to schema structure, SQL dialect and even row contents.
  • Better: hide the detail behind an API (Figure 1-10), or have the Order service publish a dataset in a separate database meant for bulk consumers (Figure 1-11), which keeps the public contract and lets you tune the model to consumers' needs.
  • Outside-in thinking: start from what service consumers need, and actually ask the people who will call it. The common opposite is to expose an internal data model.
Treat interfaces like a user interface

Shape the service contract with the people who call it, as you would a UI. The service is easier to use and the external contract stays separate from the internal implementation.

Temporal coupling

  • When a message is sent and how it is handled are tied in time (Figure 1-12): Warehouse calls Order over synchronous HTTP, which calls Customer synchronously; all three must be up.
  • Reduce it by caching (Order caches the customer data it needs) or by an asynchronous transport such as a message broker, so the message is handled when the downstream service is available
    • Service communication is covered in Chapter 4 of Building Microservices.

Deployment coupling

  • Changing one line in a statically linked module means deploying the whole monolith. Coupling can also be chosen: a release train deploys everything on a fixed schedule
    • Newman treats it as a transitional step towards release on demand, and has seen organisations deploy every service at once without checking which changed.
  • Deploying carries risk
    • change only what needs changing. Smaller releases mean less to go wrong and easier diagnosis, which is the heart of continuous delivery (Humble and Farley)
    • Newman's interest in microservices came from continuous delivery. Reducing deployment coupling does not need microservices (Erlang can hot-deploy modules).

Domain coupling

  • Services must interact, and the interactions model the real domain: placing an order needs the basket's items; shipping needs an address. This is domain coupling.
  • Music Corp:
    • Order Processing sends the full order to Warehouse (Figure 1-13), and Warehouse fetches the customer from the Customer service to notify them
    • Sharing the whole order is unnecessary (the warehouse need not know prices; an invoice can go as a pre-rendered PDF) and exposes things such as card details.
  • Introduce a Pick Instruction with only what Warehouse needs (Figure 1-14), another example of information hiding. Putting the customer details in too (Figure 1-15) removes Warehouse's need to call Customer
    • Order Processing probably needs customer data anyway.
  • Alternatively Order Processing emits an event that Warehouse consumes (Figure 1-16), flipping the dependency
    • Which is better depends on understanding the wider interactions, which domain modelling helps with
    • Some information must flow, so domain coupling cannot be avoided, only minimised.

1.5 Just enough domain-driven design

  • Modelling services around the business domain needs a way to build that model: domain-driven design (DDD), from Eric Evans' Domain-Driven Design (2004)
    • Further reading: Vaughn Vernon's Domain-Driven Design Distilled (2014).

Aggregate

  • Best seen as a representation of a real domain concept (Order, Invoice, Stock Item) with a life cycle, so it can be a state machine
    • Treat it as a self-contained unit: state transitions and state live together.
  • A microservice handles the life cycle and storage of one or more aggregate types
    • Another service can request a state change or the aggregate can react to events (Figure 1-17: Payment triggering Paid on Invoice)
    • The aggregate can say no; ideally illegal transitions are impossible.
  • Aggregates relate to other aggregates (Figure 1-18: one Customer, several Orders, modelled separately and possibly in different services).
  • Breaking a system into aggregates is subjective and can change for performance or ease of implementation; start from the users' mental model. Event Storming (Chapter 2) helps.

Bounded context

  • A larger organisational boundary with explicit responsibilities
    • In Music Corp the warehouse (orders, returns, stock, forklift races) and finance (payroll, shipping payments) are different contexts
    • Internal concerns (types of forklift) are hidden from outsiders
    • A bounded context contains one or more aggregates, some exposed, some hidden; relationships between contexts become inter-service dependencies.

Mapping to microservices

  • Both are units of cohesion with explicit interfaces, so both work as service boundaries
    • At first target services around whole bounded contexts; later split along aggregate boundaries
    • Even then you can hide the split behind a coarser-grained API, since decomposition is an implementation decision.

Summary points

  • Microservices: independently deployable, domain-modelled, networked, hiding their data.
  • Use information hiding and DDD for stable boundaries; reduce implementation, temporal, deployment and domain coupling.
  • The monolith, modular or not, remains a valid option; the chapters ahead cover how to choose and migrate.
Terms to carry forward
  • Independent deployability; delivery contention; distributed monolith; modular monolith.
  • Conway's law; Constantine's law; information hiding.
  • Implementation, temporal, deployment, domain coupling.
  • Aggregate; bounded context; outside-in design.
