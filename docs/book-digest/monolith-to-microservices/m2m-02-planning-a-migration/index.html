Monolith to Microservices 2: Planning a Migration

Source: Sam Newman, Monolith to Microservices (O'Reilly, 2019), chapter 2. Previous: Monolith to Microservices 1: Just Enough Microservices. Next: Monolith to Microservices 3: Splitting the Monolith.

Takeaways
  • Microservices are not the goal. Decide what you want to achieve that your current architecture cannot give you, check the alternatives, and decide how you will know it is working.
  • Common reasons (autonomy, time to market, scaling, robustness, more developers, new technology) each have simpler alternatives worth trying first.
  • Bad ideas: an unclear domain, a real startup, customer-installed software, or having no good reason.
  • Migrate incrementally, extract one service at a time, and treat extraction as done only in production. Use domain modelling and a value versus difficulty quadrant to choose where to start.
  • Manage the people side (Kotter's eight steps, team structure, skills) and review progress with checkpoints and measures; beware the sunk cost fallacy.

2.1 Understanding the goal

  • You do not "win" by having microservices. Adopt them as a conscious, rational decision to achieve something your current architecture cannot.
  • Knowing the goal drives where you focus, avoids analysis paralysis, and avoids cargo cult thinking ("if it is good for Netflix it is good for us").
  • A common failing:
    • at a workshop, an attendee did not know why they had come ("our boss told us")
    • the boss said "our CTO said we're doing microservices". Without a clear why, transitions cost money and people and can run a year before anyone remembers the reason (not just sunk cost: they literally do not know why).
  • Asked for ROI figures:
    • studies are few, and rarely transferable between contexts
    • In their absence, apply critical thinking and an experimental frame of mind
    • ROI needs an assessment of the return you want
    • Focus on outcomes, not one approach, even if it means ditching work or going back to the boring approach.

Three key questions

  1. What are you hoping to achieve? Outcomes aligned with the business and describable as benefits to end users.
  2. Have you considered alternatives to microservices? Often a more boring technique gives the same benefit.
  3. How will you know if the transition is working? (See 2.9.)

Asking these is sometimes enough for a company to think again.

2.2 Why you might choose microservices, and the alternatives

Reason How microservices help Alternatives to try
Improve team autonomy teams that own services have more control; small units (Gore keeps units under 150; Timpsons cut internal rules to two: look the part and put the money in the till) give teams ownership of parts of the codebase (a modular monolith), name decision makers by expertise, self-service provisioning so central operations need not field tickets
Reduce time to market change and deploy individual services without coordinated releases; more people can work in parallel path-to-production modelling: at an investment bank, Kief Morris mapped the stages and found about six weeks from developer start to production but over 40 weeks from the idea to developers starting; fixing that mattered more
Scale cost-effectively for load scale only the constrained services; scale down or off others (why many SaaS firms use them) a bigger box (vertical scaling); run several copies of the monolith behind a load balancer or queue (horizontal scaling; limited if the database is the bottleneck); replace technology (non-trivial)
Improve robustness impact on one area need not bring down everything; focus effort on critical parts multiple monolith copies across failure planes (racks, data centres); more reliable hardware and software; investigate past outage causes (reliance on manual processes; the 2017 British Airways outage was triggered by one person's action)
Scale the number of developers independent pieces reduce delivery contention (Brooks, The Mythical Man-Month: more people help only if work partitions with limited interaction) a modular monolith with team-owned modules, though deployment still needs coordination
Embrace new technology vary language, platform or database per service; isolate a risky technology new languages on the same runtime (the JVM); a "burning platform" can be replaced incrementally by the strangler fig pattern (Chapter 3)
Resilience versus robustness

John Allspaw (a colleague of David Woods, who pioneered the resilience model): robustness is reacting to expected variations (a machine may die, so load balance an instance); resilience is an organisation able to adapt to what it did not anticipate (a culture of experimentation, such as chaos engineering). Microservices do not give robustness for free: spreading functionality over processes can just enlarge the failure surface.

Reuse is a poor goal

Reuse is not what people actually want; it is a means to faster delivery or lower cost, so track those instead. Example: Music Corp's Customer Services team needs invoice PDFs, and the warehouse already generates PDFs for packing slips. Reusing it means coordinating with another team; writing their own may be faster. Optimising for reuse can slow time to market.

2.3 When microservices might be a bad idea

  • Unclear domain:
    • wrong boundaries cost a lot (many cross-service changes, coupling)
      • Example: ThoughtWorks' SnapCI team knew continuous integration well, yet their first service boundaries were subtly wrong
    • after months of high change cost they merged the services into one application, and a year later split it again with stable boundaries. Prematurely decomposing is costly
    • decomposing an existing codebase is easier than starting with microservices.
  • Startups:
    • real startups experiment and their domain shifts
    • Netflix, Airbnb and others moved to microservices later; they suit scale-ups with product-market fit
    • Brownfield systems are easier to partition: code to examine, people to ask, a known baseline for performance
    • Newman does not say never, but split only on clear boundaries, and if you struggle with two services, ten will be hard.
  • Customer-installed and managed software:
    • microservices push complexity into operations
    • you cannot expect customers to have the skills or platform (Kubernetes installs vary widely), when they are used to one process on a known platform.
  • No good reason: doing it because everyone else does is terrible.

2.4 Trade-offs between goals

  • People try to change many things at once (scale, team autonomy, Kotlin), locking microservices in as the approach even if horizontal scaling would do. Separate the core driver from secondary benefits
    • secondary goals must not detract from the main one.
  • Exercise:
    • treat each desired outcome as a slider starting in the middle (Figure 2-1)
    • Raising one lowers another, which gives a ranking (for example polyglot programming matters less than resilience)
    • Priorities change as you learn, and the ranking guides local decisions in autonomous teams.

2.5 Taking people on the journey

  • Disagreements often come from different views of the goal; first share the goal. To sell the idea, use Kotter's model.

Kotter's eight steps

  • Used for large organisational shifts, possibly overkill for a team of 10, but the early steps still help (Figure 2-2).
Step Applied to microservices
Establish a sense of urgency your idea is one of many; look for "teachable moments" after a crisis; share urgency about the goal, not "do microservices now"
Create the guiding coalition enough people to make it happen: colleagues, a tech lead or architect, maybe a CIO or CTO; trust is earned through smaller wins; include people outside IT, since trade-offs (for example caching reduces latency but users may see stale data) need the people who represent users
Develop a vision and strategy vision is the goal (realistic yet aspirational), strategy the how; stay committed to the vision, not rigidly to a strategy (sunk cost)
Communicate the change vision not too big to believe (a CEO's "reduce costs and deliver faster by moving to microservices in 12 months" was not believed); start small (Google's "Test Mercenaries" and one-page "Testing on the Toilet" articles); do as much face to face as possible before broadcasting
Empower employees for broad-based action remove roadblocks: lack of bandwidth (bring in people), provisioning that takes months (on-demand virtual machines, containers or public cloud); do not spend a year designing "the perfect microservice platform" before you have problems; Google's testing effort started with a few teams and grew tools (test frameworks, test visibility in code review, a company-wide CI tool)
Generate short-term wins extract easy functionality first, balanced against benefit; a hard "easy" task is valuable insight
Consolidate gains and produce more change pause and reflect; deeper cuts get harder (database decomposition cannot be put off forever); a technique that worked in one area may not work elsewhere
Anchor new approaches in the culture share stories of success and failure until it is the normal way; then leave space for better approaches to displace it

2.6 The importance of incremental migration

"If you do a big-bang rewrite, the only thing you're guaranteed of is a big bang." (Martin Fowler)

  • Chip away at the monolith like a block of marble rather than blowing it up. The cost of a large experiment is high and feedback unclear
    • small steps are analysed and learned from
    • a retrograde step is only a small one
    • you unlock each service's value incrementally. Stock advice: start somewhere small, deploy one or two services to production and reflect.

It is production that counts

  • Extraction is not complete until the service is in production and actively used
    • Most lessons (troubleshooting, tracing, latency, referential integrity, cascading failures) appear only there
    • The next chapters show techniques to deploy while limiting the impact of problems.

Cost of change

  • Small steps let you see the effect of each change and change course.
  • Reversible and irreversible decisions
    • (Jeff Bezos, 2015 shareholder letter): one-way doors (Type 1) need method, care and consultation
    • two-way doors (Type 2) can be made quickly by high-judgement people or small groups
      • Treating Type 2 as Type 1 makes everything life or death
      • Newman prefers the names Irreversible and Reversible (a nod to Martin Fowler) and sees a spectrum (Figure 2-3)
    • most microservice decisions are towards the reversible end, since software can often be rolled back. Assess the impact of changing your mind later.

  • Easier places to experiment:
    • moving code in a codebase is cheap
    • splitting a database, untangling a coupled integration, or rewriting an API with many consumers is costly
      • Make mistakes where the impact is lowest: the whiteboard
      • Sketch the design and run use cases (search for a record, register, buy an album) across the proposed boundaries
    • look for circular references and chatty services that should be one.

2.7 Where to start

Domain-driven design

  • A domain model gives a logical decomposition and helps prioritise. Figure 2-4 shows Music Corp's bounded contexts and relationships
    • each is a potential decomposition unit. Newman calls a domain model a near-essential step.
  • How far do you go?
    • Just enough to choose where to start: perhaps high-level groupings and a deeper dive into the parts that most need attention. You may miss systemic issues, but do not obsess
    • refine as you learn.

Event Storming

  • Created by Alberto Brandolini:
    • technical and non-technical stakeholders build a shared domain model bottom up: define domain events (things that happen), group them into aggregates, then into bounded contexts
    • It does not mean building an event-driven system
    • The output is the shared understanding; the hard part is getting the right people in the room
    • Further reading: Introducing EventStorming (in progress at publication).

Using a model for prioritisation

  • Count upstream and downstream dependencies
    • Notification has many inbound dependencies (Figure 2-5), so extracting it means changing many local calls to service calls: likely harder
    • Invoicing has none (Figure 2-6): easier; the strangler fig pattern can proxy inbound calls before they reach the monolith.
  • Caveats:
    • the model is logical, so the code may be more entangled
    • it does not show database ownership (Invoicing might manage lots of data, implying database decomposition, Chapter 4, which you may avoid for the first services). Weigh ease against benefit: if the goal is time to market and Invoicing rarely changes, it may not be worth starting there.

A combined model

  • Place each candidate on two axes (Figure 2-7):
    • value of decomposing (x) and difficulty (y)
    • Do it as a team
    • The wanted corner (Figure 2-8) is easy and valuable; pick one or two services from it (Invoicing in the example)
    • Re-run it as you learn: things thought easy turn out hard and vice versa (Notifications might prove easier than expected).

2.8 Reorganising teams

  • Shifting structures:
    • IT used to group by competency (Java developers, testers, DBAs) with hand-offs (analyst, developer, test, operations)
    • Like layered architectures, more teams involved means slower change
    • Silos are breaking down:
      • testers join delivery teams
      • DevOps moves operational responsibility to delivery teams, and central teams shift from doing to enabling (embedded specialists, self-service tooling, training)
    • The shift is a trend, not universal or quick.
  • Not one size fits all:
    • do not copy others
    • The Spotify model came from the 2012 paper "Scaling Agile @ Spotify" (Henrik Kniberg and Anders Ivarsson); Spotify never called it a model and no longer uses it
    • Jessica Kerr: "Copy the questions, not the answers."
  • Telling delivery teams "now you deploy and run 24/7 support" can alienate people who have never done it. Present it as an aspiration with reasons and craft the journey
    • if teams will own the whole life cycle, their skills must grow (help, training, embed operations people), and it can be done incrementally.
  • DevOps does not mean NoOps:
    • it is a cultural movement breaking barriers between development and operations
    • specialists may still exist. Recommended: Team Topologies (Manuel Pais and Matthew Skelton, 2019) and The DevOps Handbook (Gene Kim, Jez Humble, Patrick Debois, 2016).

Making a change

  1. List all activities and responsibilities in delivering software, with stakeholders from every role; map them to current teams, optionally over a path-to-production model (Figure 2-9). This shared "as-is" picture helps silos understand each other, and shows that some teams already do a lot (deploying their own test environments makes the step to production smaller, though tier-1 support and diagnosis are skills built over years).
  2. Redraw for a sensible horizon (six months to a year). In the example (Figure 2-10) frontend and backend responsibilities merge, teams provision their own test environments using a self-service platform from operations, and the delivery team handles all incidents during working hours while operations coach them.
  3. Check feasibility with people on the ground; split into specific responsibilities to go incrementally (first removing operations' provisioning of test environments).

Changing skills

  • Have people self-assess privately
    • At The Guardian (rebuilt online by ThoughtWorks), developers rated themselves from 1 ("this means nothing to me") to 5 ("I could write a book") against a list of core skills
    • only the mentor saw scores
    • the aim was personal targets, not 5 everywhere
      • Coaches ensured chances to grow (stories using that technology, videos, courses, conferences)
      • Figure 2-11 shows a personal chart (author's wish to grow Kubernetes and Lambda; content with Go)
      • Keep it private, or people will fear a low mark affecting reviews.
  • Anonymised ratings form a team skill map (Figure 2-12:
    • the team wants to improve Kafka, Kubernetes and PACT testing) to justify group learning or a course
    • The answer may also be to hire (for example a Kafka expert) who then helps colleagues
    • Start with your own people, culture and users' needs.

2.9 How will you know the transition is working?

  • Define measures from your outcomes, quantitative and qualitative, and review them regularly. Ask not just "is this working?" but "should we try something else?"

Regular checkpoints

  • Informal for small teams (retrospectives), explicit and regular (for example monthly leadership sessions) for large programmes
    • Cover: restate what the transition should achieve (stop if business direction changed)
    • review quantitative measures
    • ask for qualitative feedback
    • decide what to change.

Quantitative and qualitative measures

  • Quantitative examples:
    • for time to market, cycle time, number of deployments and failure rates; for scale, performance test results
    • "You get what you measure": measures can be gamed (a vendor paid by tickets closed closed tickets unresolved, prompting reopening)
    • Some measures change slowly or worsen first: expect cycle time to get worse in the first months because change reduces productivity before it improves, which is another reason for small steps.
  • Qualitative:
    • "software is made of feelings" (Astrid Atkinson)
    • Are people enjoying it, empowered, supported, or overwhelmed? Include this in scorecards to management and do not rely on numbers alone.

Avoiding the sunk cost fallacy

  • Continuing because you have invested, despite evidence
    • The bigger the bet and fanfare, the harder to pull out
    • Also called the Concorde fallacy (the supersonic airliner that never became financially viable)
    • Small steps and checkpoints make changing direction easier; you need not quit at the first sign of trouble, but ignore evidence at your peril.

Being open to new approaches

  • Expect to revert changes, try things, and sometimes pause to see the effect. Build constant improvement into everything; treating change as one-off projects leaves you years behind.

Summary points

  • Ask: what to achieve, what alternatives, how will I know. Rank goals with sliders.
  • Move incrementally; extraction is done only in production; pick candidates by value and difficulty from a domain model.
  • Look after people (urgency, coalition, vision, wins), skills and structure; measure, review, and avoid sunk cost.
Terms to carry forward
  • Cargo cult thinking; path-to-production modelling; burning platform.
  • Robustness vs resilience; competing consumers (queue-fed monolith copies).
  • Type 1 and Type 2 decisions (irreversible, reversible).
  • Event Storming; strangler fig (preview of Chapter 3).
  • Sunk cost (Concorde) fallacy; Kotter's eight steps.
