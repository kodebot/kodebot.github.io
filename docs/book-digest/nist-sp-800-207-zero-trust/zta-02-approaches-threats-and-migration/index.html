NIST SP 800-207 Zero Trust Architecture 2: Approaches, Deployment, Threats and Migration

Source: NIST Special Publication 800-207, Zero Trust Architecture (US government publication), sections 3.1 to 7. Local copy books/NIST_SP_800-207_Zero_Trust_Architecture.pdf. Own-words summary, much shorter than the source. Sections 6 (interactions with existing federal guidance) and the appendices were not digested. Previous: NIST SP 800-207 Zero Trust Architecture 1: Basics, Tenets and Components.

Takeaways
  • Three approaches to build a ZTA: enhanced identity governance, micro-segmentation, and network infrastructure and software-defined perimeters; a full solution uses elements of all three.
  • Four deployment models differ in where the PEP sits: device agent and gateway, enclave, resource portal, application sandboxing.
  • The trust algorithm can be criteria-based (all conditions must pass) or score-based (a weighted confidence compared with a threshold).
  • Main risks: subverting the decision components, denial of service against them, stolen credentials, poor visibility, stored data as a target, vendor lock-in, and non-person entities.
  • Migrate one workflow at a time, starting with a low-risk process and observing before enforcing.

2.1 Approaches

Approach Policy centred on Notes
Enhanced identity governance identity and assigned attributes; device and environment adjust the confidence suits open networks with visitors and non-enterprise devices, and SaaS apps you cannot instrument; downside: basic network access exists, so reconnaissance and denial-of-service remain possible and must be monitored
Micro-segmentation resources or small groups behind gateway PEPs (switches, next-generation firewalls, host-based agents) needs an identity governance programme; PEPs must be managed and able to reconfigure; stateless firewalls are possible but a poor choice for admin cost and slow adaptation
Network infrastructure and software-defined perimeters an overlay or software-defined network where the policy administrator acts as the network controller often application-layer; uses software-defined networking and intent-based networking ideas

2.2 Deployment models

Model Where the PEP sits
Device agent / gateway two parts: an agent on each enterprise asset and a gateway directly in front of each resource, which acts as its proxy
Enclave-based gateways at the boundary of a resource enclave (for example a data centre) serving one business function, useful for legacy systems that cannot sit behind their own gateway
Resource portal one gateway portal for a resource or enclave, such as into a private cloud or legacy data centre
Device application sandboxing vetted applications run in compartments (virtual machines, containers) to protect them from a compromised host or other applications

2.3 The trust algorithm

  • The policy engine is the "brain" and the trust algorithm its main thought process
    • Inputs: the access request (resource and requester details such as OS version and software), subject identity and attributes, asset state, resource requirements, policy and threat intelligence, and historical behaviour.
  • Two variations:
    • Criteria-based: a set of qualifying conditions configured per resource; access only if all are met.
    • Score-based: a confidence level computed from weighted data sources; access if it exceeds the resource's threshold.
  • A second distinction: whether a request is judged alone or in relation to the same subject's previous requests (contextual).

2.4 Network requirements and use cases

  • A ZT environment separates (logically or physically) the control plane (configuring assets, judging and granting access, setting up communication paths) from the data plane (the actual application traffic).
  • Network requirements the publication lists (section 3.4.1):
    1. Enterprise assets have basic connectivity (LAN routing and DNS).
    2. The enterprise can tell which assets it owns and their security posture, from enterprise-issued credentials, not from spoofable data such as MAC addresses.
    3. The enterprise can observe all network traffic and collect connection metadata, even where it cannot inspect layer 7.
    4. Resources are not reachable without going through a PEP; they may not even be discoverable, which stops scanning and denial-of-service against them (some infrastructure such as DNS must stay reachable).
    5. The data and control planes are logically separate.
    6. Enterprise assets can reach the PEP.
    7. The PEP is the only component that talks to the policy administrator in a business flow.
    8. Remote assets can reach resources (for example cloud email) without first going through the enterprise network over a VPN.
    9. The decision infrastructure is scalable, because delay or inability to reach a PEP harms the workflow.
  • Scenarios the document walks through:
    • an enterprise with satellite facilities
    • multi-cloud enterprises (applications in one cloud reaching data in another directly rather than through the enterprise)
    • contracted services and non-employee access
    • collaboration across enterprise boundaries
    • and public or customer-facing services, where the enterprise cannot impose internal policy on requesting assets.

2.5 Threats to a ZTA

Threat Point
Subversion of the decision process the policy engine and administrator are critical; anyone with configuration access could make unapproved changes or mistakes
Denial of service or network disruption resources cannot connect without the policy administrator; mitigate by hosting policy enforcement in a properly secured cloud or replicating it in several places
Stolen credentials or insider threat zero trust reduces but does not remove the risk: attackers must still compromise an account or device, and a good ZTA limits what it can reach
Visibility on the network some traffic is opaque to layer 3 analysis tools (non-enterprise assets, encrypted application traffic)
Storage of system and network information stored scans, traffic metadata and logs become a target; protect them like network diagrams
Proprietary data formats or solutions no common open standard among the data sources can lock you in to a provider
Use of non-person entities in administration software agents and AI managing the policy components need strong authentication of their own

2.6 Migrating to zero trust

  • Pure ZTA (greenfield): know the workflows, narrow the components, map their interactions.
  • Hybrid:
    • an enterprise is unlikely to migrate in one technology refresh cycle
    • ZTA and non-ZTA workflows coexist, one business process at a time, so common elements (identity management, device management, event logging) must work in both.

  1. Identify actors (human and non-person entities such as service accounts); privileged users such as developers and system administrators need extra scrutiny when assigning roles.
  2. Identify assets (hardware such as laptops, phones and IoT devices, and digital artefacts), including non-enterprise devices on the network.
  3. Identify key processes and evaluate risks; rank workflows and start with a low-risk one so disruption is limited.
  4. Formulate policies for the candidate, using the NIST Risk Management Framework to value the asset or workflow, and identify upstream resources.
  5. Identify candidate solutions by deployment model and vendor fit (for example does it need components installed on the client asset?).
  6. Initial deployment and monitoring: begin in observation mode because first policy sets are rarely complete (important accounts might be denied needed access); refine.
  7. Expand the ZTA once the policy set is refined: steady operation with lower-tempo changes, feedback from stakeholders, then the next workflow.

Summary points

  • Pick the approach that matches your starting point; most real deployments mix all three.
  • Protect the policy components as the crown jewels.
  • Migrate workflow by workflow, observe first, then enforce.
Terms to carry forward
  • Micro-segmentation: placing small groups of resources behind enforcement points.
  • Software-defined perimeter (SDP): an overlay network built by a controller from policy decisions.
  • Criteria-based vs score-based trust algorithm.
  • Enclave: a group of resources behind one gateway.
  • Contextual trust algorithm: judges a request in light of the subject's history.
