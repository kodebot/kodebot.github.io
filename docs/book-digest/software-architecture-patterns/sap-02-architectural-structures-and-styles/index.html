Software Architecture Patterns 2: Architectural Structures and Styles

Source: Mark Richards, Software Architecture Patterns, 2nd edition (O'Reilly, 2022), chapter 2. Previous: Software Architecture Patterns 1: Introduction. Next: Software Architecture Patterns 3: Layered Architecture.

Takeaways
  • Styles give a head start on supporting certain architecture characteristics (the "-ilities") and make it easier for developers, architects, testers, operations and sometimes business stakeholders to communicate.
  • First classification: monolithic (one deployment unit) or distributed (several deployment units, usually services). Choosing between them is the first step in selecting a style.
  • Monolithic styles are simple and cheap but weak on scalability, fault tolerance and elasticity; distributed styles are strong there but pay for it in complexity and cost.
  • Second classification: technical partitioning (components grouped by technical use, for example layers) or domain partitioning (components grouped by business domain).
  • Choose the partitioning to match your team structure and the kind of change you expect.

2.1 Architecture classification

Styles belong to one of two structures:

  • monolithic: a single deployment unit
  • distributed: multiple deployment units, usually consisting of services

Distributed architectures support very different architecture characteristics from monolithic ones, so this is the first decision when matching a style to a business problem.

Monolithic architectures

  • Generally much simpler than distributed ones, so easier to design and implement.
  • Fairly inexpensive overall; most can be built and deployed much more quickly.
  • Weak points are operational: scalability, fault tolerance, elasticity.
    • A fatal error, such as an out-of-memory condition, makes all functionality fail.
    • MTTR (mean time to recovery) and MTTS (mean time to start) are usually measured in minutes, so recovery is slow. Long start-up times also hurt scalability and elasticity.
    • Scaling is possible by load balancing several instances, but the whole application must scale even if only a small part needs to, which is inefficient and costly.
  • Examples the chapter gives: layered architecture (Chapter 3), the modular monolith, the pipeline architecture, and microkernel architecture (Chapter 4).

Distributed architectures

  • Multiple deployment units work together to perform a cohesive business function. Today most consist of services, though each style has its own formal name for a service.
  • Their "superpowers" are operational:
    • scalability and elasticity, typically at the individual service level
    • fault tolerance: when one service fails, others can often carry on, and a failed service can recover so fast the end user may not notice
    • agility (responding quickly to change): functionality is separately deployed, so a change is easier to locate, the testing scope shrinks to the affected service and deployment risk drops
    • MTTS and MTTR are usually seconds, sometimes milliseconds
  • The costs:
    • They suffer the fallacies of distributed computing, eight beliefs about networks that are false, such as "the network is reliable", "bandwidth is infinite" and "latency is zero"
      • The chapter says these are as real today as when they were coined in the late 1990s.
    • Other difficulties: distributed transactions, eventual consistency, workflow management, error handling, data synchronisation and contract management.
    • Higher overall cost, both initial implementation and ongoing maintenance.
  • Examples the chapter gives: event-driven (Chapter 5), microservices (Chapter 6), service-based, service-oriented, and space-based (Chapter 7).

Which one should I choose?

  • Ask first whether the system has different sets of architecture characteristics to support: does the whole system need to scale and stay highly available, or only parts of it?
    • Systems with several different sets generally call for a distributed architecture.
    • The chapter's example: customer-facing functionality that needs scalability, responsiveness, availability and agility, next to an administrative or back-end processing function that needs none of those.
  • Simple systems or websites usually warrant the simpler, cheaper monolithic style.
  • More complex systems that perform several business functions usually warrant distributed architectures.
  • The "need for speed", high volumes of scalability and high fault tolerance all lean towards distributed.

2.2 Architecture partitioning

Whether monolithic or distributed, an architecture is also either technically partitioned or domain partitioned.

Technical partitioning

  • Components are organised by technical usage.
  • The classic example is the layered (n-tiered) architecture (Chapter 3): presentation components for the user interface, business-layer components for business rules and core processing, persistence-layer components for database interaction, and a database layer holding the data.
  • A domain is spread across every layer. The customer domain has screens in presentation, logic in business, queries in persistence and tables in the database. As namespaces:
    • app.presentation.customer
    • app.business.customer
    • app.persistence.customer
  • The second node of the namespace is the technical layer.

  • Good when most changes sit in one technical area: constantly changing the look and feel without touching business rules, or changing business rules with no effect on the data or presentation layers.
  • Bad for domain-based changes. The chapter's example is adding an expiration date to wish-list items, which touches every layer:
    • add a column to the wish-list table (database layer)
    • change the SQL (persistence layer)
    • add the business rules (business layer)
    • change the contracts between business and presentation layers, and the screens (presentation layer)
    • depending on system size and team structure, this simple change might need three to four teams to coordinate
  • Examples the chapter gives: layered, microkernel, pipeline, event-driven and space-based.
  • Microkernel is the only style that can be either, depending on how plug-ins are used: plug-ins as adapters or special configuration settings make it technically partitioned.

Domain partitioning

  • Components are organised by domain area, not technical usage: presentation, business logic and persistence logic for a domain and its subdomains are grouped together.
  • Namespaces look like app.customer, app.shipping, app.payment; the second node is the domain.
  • A domain can still be layered inside, for example app.customer.presentation and app.customer.business, but the primary structure is the domain.
  • It has grown in popularity partly through domain-driven design, a modelling and analysis technique coined by Eric Evans (named in the chapter)
    • DDD emphasises designing the domain over complex workflows and technical components, so teams work closely with domain experts on one key part of the system.

  • Advantage: a change to a domain or subdomain is self-contained, so teams pinpoint exactly where to make it.
  • The same wish-list change is isolated to the namespace app.customer.wishlist, with presentation, business and persistence logic in one area
    • The chapter says maintenance and testing are easier and deployment is much less risky.

  • Examples the chapter gives: microkernel, microservices, modular monolith and service-based.
  • Microkernel is domain partitioned when plug-ins extend the application with added functionality.

Which one should I choose?

The structure must align with both the team structure and the type of change you expect.

Choose When
Technically partitioned Teams are organised by technical area (UI, back-end and database teams) so the team structure matches the layers
Most expected changes align with technical layers: several user interfaces, look-and-feel changes, swapping one database for another
Domain partitioned You start a new system using a domain-driven design approach
Teams are cross-functional and aligned to a domain, with UI, back-end and database developers on one team
Most expected changes are domain-scoped rather than technical, giving better agility
  • Caution for domain partitioning: many changes to technical layers, such as swapping a database type or changing the entire UI framework, are difficult and time-consuming.

Summary points

  • Monolithic means one deployment unit; distributed means several.
  • Monolithic gives simplicity and low cost; distributed gives scalability, elasticity, fault tolerance and agility at higher complexity and cost.
  • Technical partitioning groups by layer; domain partitioning groups by business area.
  • The layered (n-tiered) style is the chapter's classic technically partitioned example; the modular monolith and microservices are domain partitioned.
  • Match the partitioning to your teams and to where changes will land.
Terms to carry forward
  • Monolithic architecture: a single deployment unit.
  • Distributed architecture: multiple deployment units, usually services.
  • MTTR / MTTS: mean time to recovery / mean time to start.
  • Fallacies of distributed computing: eight false beliefs about networks, for example "the network is reliable".
  • Technical partitioning: components grouped by technical usage.
  • Domain partitioning: components grouped by domain area.
