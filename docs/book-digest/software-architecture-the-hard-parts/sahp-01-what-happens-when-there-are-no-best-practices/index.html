Software Architecture: The Hard Parts 1: What Happens When There Are No "Best Practices"?

Source: Neal Ford, Mark Richards, Pramod Sadalage and Zhamak Dehghani, Software Architecture: The Hard Parts (O'Reilly, 2021), chapter 1. Previous: Software Architecture: The Hard Parts 0: Preface. Next: Software Architecture: The Hard Parts 2: Discerning Coupling in Software Architecture.

Takeaways
  • Architects face problems with no general good solution, only one messy set of trade-offs against another. Their advice: do not seek the best design; strive for the least worst combination of trade-offs.
  • The book is about decision making, not technology; "hard" means both difficult and solid (foundational, the parts that are hard to change).
  • Data is central: in microservices with a bounded context, data became an architectural concern; the book separates operational (OLTP) from analytical data.
  • Decisions are recorded as ADRs (Context, Decision, Consequences) and governed with architecture fitness functions, objective automated checks on architecture characteristics.
  • Simple working definitions (service, coupling, component, sync/async, orchestration/choreography, atomicity, contract) and a running case study: the Sysops Squad saga.

1.1 No best practices

  • Technologists write books when they have found a "best practice" for a general problem
    • But many architecture problems have none: they present "one messy set of trade-offs cast against an (almost) equally messy set".
  • Developers can search online for answers
    • architects usually cannot, because their problems combine the exact environment and circumstances of their organisation. "For architects, every problem is a snowflake", often novel across the world, which is why few architecture books exist compared with framework or API books.
  • No silver bullet: Fred Brooks coined the term in 1986 ("No Silver Bullet"): no single development in technology or management technique promises even a tenfold improvement within a decade in productivity, reliability or simplicity.
  • The architect's real job is to objectively determine and assess the trade-offs on either side of a consequential decision
    • "Best" implies maximising all competing factors, so the authors avoid it.
The book's tongue-in-cheek advice

Don't try to find the best design in software architecture; instead, strive for the least worst combination of trade-offs.

  • No single architecture characteristic excels as it would alone
    • the balance of competing characteristics promotes project success. The open question is how to find that combination and document it effectively.

1.2 Why "The Hard Parts"?

  • "Hard" does double duty:
    • difficult: problems no one has faced, with long-term technology implications on top of interpersonal and political context
    • solid:
      • like hardware versus software, the hard part should change much less because it is the foundation for the soft stuff
      • architecture is structural, design is more easily changed
  • A tongue-in-cheek definition: "software architecture is the stuff that's hard to change later". That is what the book covers.

1.3 Timeless advice

  • The ecosystem keeps changing. Ten years earlier the dominant large-enterprise style was orchestration-driven service-oriented architecture
    • now few build it and microservices is favoured. Why?
    • Then, companies were merging into enterprises with integration problems, and open source was not a viable option for large companies, often for political rather than technical reasons, so architects stressed shared resources and centralised orchestration.
    • Later Linux and open source became viable, then the tipping point: Linux became operationally free with tools like Puppet and Chef, which let teams spin up environments programmatically as part of an automated build
      • That fostered the microservices revolution and container and orchestration tools such as Kubernetes.
  • One new capability leads unexpectedly to another; the ecosystem replaces itself piece by piece.
  • So the book avoids technology and implementation detail and focuses on how architects decide and weigh trade-offs, using contemporary scenarios for context.

1.4 The importance of data in architecture

  • Quote: "Data is a precious thing and will last longer than the systems themselves" (Tim Berners-Lee).
  • Data outlives systems and architecture. Data architects' instincts to build tightly coupled systems conflict with distributed architecture
    • business data must survive breaking up monoliths and still give value.
  • All software architecture is "in the service of data", making the right data available and usable across the enterprise.
  • The authors' realisation:
    • decades ago distributed systems still mostly persisted data in one relational database
    • In microservices, with the bounded context from Domain-Driven Design limiting implementation-detail coupling, data moved to an architectural concern, along with transactionality
    • Many hard parts come from the tension between data and architecture concerns (Parts I and II).
Kind of data Meaning
Operational Used to run the business: sales, transactions, inventory; if interrupted the organisation cannot function for long. OLTP: typically insert, update, delete
Analytical Used by data scientists and analysts for predictions, trends and business intelligence; typically not transactional and often not relational (graph databases, snapshots in another format); not critical day to day, but for long-term strategic direction

1.5 Architectural decision records

  • ADRs
    • were first evangelised by Michael Nygard in a blog post and later marked "adopt" in the Thoughtworks Technology Radar
    • An ADR is a short text file (usually one to two pages) describing one architecture decision, written in plain text, AsciiDoc or Markdown, or as a wiki page template
    • Fundamentals of Software Architecture devotes a chapter to them.
  • The book's format (each ADR assumed approved):
Section Content
ADR: A short noun phrase naming the decision
Context One or two sentences on the problem, with the alternatives
Decision The decision with a detailed justification
Consequences Effects after applying it, and the trade-offs considered
  • All ADRs in the book are listed in Appendix B.

1.6 Architecture fitness functions

  • Governance
    • is organised oversight of software development. Documenting a decision is one thing
    • governing its use is another, and modern engineering practice can automate it with fitness functions.
  • Background:
    • early software borrowed a manufacturing metaphor (Waterfall)
    • In the early 1990s Kent Beck and the C3 project engineers (eXtreme Programming) showed the importance of incremental feedback and automation
    • In the early 2000s the same lessons reached operations, producing DevOps
    • Before continuous integration, long integration phases hurt more as projects grew; XP pioneered continuous integration and rapid feedback
    • DevOps followed once open source Linux was "good enough" and tooling allowed programmatic machine definition.
  • Architecture governance today is often manual (code reviews, architecture review boards) and details slip through.
  • Definition
    • (Building Evolutionary Architectures, O'Reilly, 2017, by Neal Ford, Rebecca Parsons and Patrick Kua): an architectural fitness function is "any mechanism that performs an objective integrity assessment of some architecture characteristic or combination of architecture characteristics"
    • Broken down:
    • Any mechanism: testing libraries for structure, monitors for performance or scalability, chaos engineering frameworks for reliability and resiliency.
    • Objective integrity assessment:
      • you cannot ask for a "high performance" website; give a measurable value
      • Beware composite characteristics such as "agility", which is not measurable but breaks into measurable parts: deployability, testability, cycle time
      • A characteristic you cannot measure is usually defined too vaguely.
    • Scope: atomic (one characteristic in isolation, such as checking for component cycles) or holistic (combinations with synergy or conflict: security and performance, scalability and elasticity).
  • Fitness function versus unit test:
    • fitness functions validate architecture characteristics, not domain criteria
    • Ask: "Is any domain knowledge required to execute this test?" Yes means a unit, functional or acceptance test; no means a fitness function
    • Example: elasticity (withstanding a burst of users) is domain-independent, while validating a mailing address is a normal test
    • The split is not purely binary.

Examples

  • Component cycles (Figure 1-1).
    • IDEs auto-import references; developers swat the dialog away reflexively
    • A network of components that each reference the others means one component cannot be reused without bringing the rest, and the architecture drifts toward the Big Ball of Mud
    • Code reviews come too late: a week of unchecked importing has already done damage
    • The fix is a fitness function wired into the continuous build (Example 1-1, using the metrics tool JDepend):
// CycleTest.java
public class CycleTest
{
    private JDepend jdepend;

    @BeforeEach
    void init()
    {
        jdepend = new JDepend();
        jdepend.addDirectory("/path/to/project/persistence/classes");
        jdepend.addDirectory("/path/to/project/web/classes");
        jdepend.addDirectory("/path/to/project/thirdpartyjars");
    }

    @Test
    void testAllPackages()
    {
        Collection packages = jdepend.analyze();
        assertEquals("Cycles exist", false, jdepend.containsCycles());
    }
}
  • It guards the important rather than urgent. Tools such as SonarQube implement many common fitness functions turnkey.

  • Layer governance (Figure 1-2).
    • A layered architecture separates concerns, but developers may erode it for local reasons such as performance ("better to ask forgiveness than permission")
    • ArchUnit (Example 1-2) codifies the rules:
// LayerRules.java
layeredArchitecture()
    .layer("Controller").definedBy("..controller..")
    .layer("Service").definedBy("..service..")
    .layer("Persistence").definedBy("..persistence..")
    .whereLayer("Controller").mayNotBeAccessedByAnyLayer()
    .whereLayer("Service").mayOnlyBeAccessedByLayers("Controller")
    .whereLayer("Persistence").mayOnlyBeAccessedByLayers("Service")
  • .NET: NetArchTest does the same (Example 1-3):
// LayerTest.cs
// Classes in the presentation should not directly reference repositories
var result = Types.InCurrentDomain()
    .That()
    .ResideInNamespace("NetArchTest.SampleLibrary.Presentation")
    .ShouldNot()
    .HaveDependencyOn("NetArchTest.SampleLibrary.Data")
    .GetResult()
    .IsSuccessful;

  • Objective does not mean static.
    • Some fitness functions return fixed values (true/false, a threshold)
    • dynamic ones depend on context, such as performance per user as concurrent users grow, where performance may decline slightly but must not "fall off a cliff".
  • Most should be automated and run continually, but some are manual, for example a lawyer reviewing legal-sensitive code; most deployment pipelines support manual stages
    • Run them on demand (rarely) or as part of continuous integration (most common)
    • "A validation that doesn't run can't validate anything."

The Equifax data breach

  • On September 7, 2017 Equifax, a major US credit scoring agency, announced a breach, traced to an exploit of the Apache Struts web framework (CVE-2017-5638)
    • The foundation announced the vulnerability and released a patch on March 7, 2017
    • the Department of Homeland Security contacted Equifax and similar companies the next day
    • scans on March 15, 2017 did not reveal all affected systems
    • the critical patch was not applied to many older systems until July 29, 2017, when Equifax's security experts identified the hacking behaviour.
  • The authors' alternative:
    • every project runs a deployment pipeline with a security "slot" for fitness functions
    • For a zero-day, the security team inserts a test everywhere that checks for the framework and version, failing the build and notifying security
    • Pipelines wake for any change to code, database schema, deployment configuration or fitness functions.

Benefits and limits

  • Benefits:
    • architects get to code again
    • an executable specification of the architecture that anyone can validate by running the build
    • an executable checklist of important but not urgent principles, protecting against the usual cause of technical debt ("we'll come back to fix it later").
  • They cite The Checklist Manifesto (Atul Gawande, Picador): surgeons and pilots use checklists not from ignorance but because repeated tasks make accidental skipping easy.
  • Do not overuse: architects should not retreat to an ivory tower and build "an impossibly complex, interlocking set of fitness functions" that frustrate developers.

1.7 Architecture versus design

The book stays firmly on the architecture side, for three reasons:

  1. Architects must understand principles first; the second law from Fundamentals of Software Architecture: why is more important than how. Example: synchronous versus asynchronous communication has trade-offs before any implementation detail.
  2. Focusing on concepts avoids the many implementations of them.
  3. Implementing every option would make "the longest book ever written".

Simple, "verging on simplistic" definitions used throughout:

Term Definition
Service A cohesive collection of functionality deployed as an independent executable; part of an architecture quantum (Chapter 2)
Coupling Two artifacts (including services) are coupled if a change in one might require a change in the other to maintain proper functionality
Component An architectural building block doing a business or infrastructure function, usually a package (Java), namespace (C#) or directory grouping, for example Order History in app.business.order.history
Synchronous communication The caller must wait for the response before proceeding
Asynchronous communication The caller does not wait; it may be notified later through a separate channel
Orchestrated coordination A workflow is orchestrated if a service's primary responsibility is to coordinate it
Choreographed coordination No orchestrator; the services share coordination responsibilities
Atomicity A workflow is atomic if all parts maintain a consistent state at all times; the opposite is the spectrum of eventual consistency (Chapter 6)
Contract The interface between two software parts: method or function calls, integration remote calls, dependencies; anywhere two pieces join

1.8 The Sysops Squad saga

  • The authors use a running story
    • "Saga" also names transactional behaviour in distributed architectures (Chapter 12)
    • Because real problems live in existing systems, the story starts with an existing architecture.
  • Penultimate Electronics,
    • a large electronics retailer, sells support plans
    • When problems occur, customer-facing experts, the Sysops Squad, visit the customer's residence or office.
  • Four main users of the ticketing application:
User Role
Administrator Maintains internal users and the list of experts (skills, location, availability); manages billing and static reference data
Customer Registers, maintains profile, support contracts and billing; enters problem tickets and completes surveys
Sysops Squad expert Assigned tickets; fixes problems; searches the knowledge base and enters repair notes
Manager Tracks ticket operations; receives operational and analytical reports
  • Nonticketing workflows:
    • administrators add experts and their locale, availability and skills
    • customers register and hold several support plans by product
    • customers are billed monthly automatically by credit card and can view billing history
    • managers request financial, expert-performance and ticketing reports.
  • Ticketing workflow, from ticket entry to survey:
    1. A customer with a support plan enters a ticket on the website.
    2. The system picks the best expert by skills, current location, service area and availability.
    3. The ticket is uploaded to the expert's custom mobile app and the expert gets a text message.
    4. The customer is told by SMS or email (per profile) that the expert is on the way.
    5. The expert retrieves ticket information and location in the app and can search the knowledge base.
    6. After the fix, the expert marks the ticket complete and can add repair information to the knowledge base.
    7. On completion the system emails the customer a survey link.
    8. The system receives and records the completed survey.
  • The bad scenario:
    • a large monolith built many years ago
    • Customers complain that experts never show up because of lost tickets, the wrong expert arrives, and the system is not always available
    • Changes are slow and risky ("something else usually breaks")
    • The system "freezes up" or crashes, taking all functionality down for five minutes to two hours while it is restarted
    • Unless something changes, Penultimate will abandon the lucrative support business and lay off administrators, experts, managers and IT staff, including the architects.

Summary points

  • No best practices for most architecture problems: seek the least worst trade-offs and document them as ADRs.
  • Data is an architectural concern in distributed systems; distinguish operational from analytical data.
  • Govern decisions with objective, automated fitness functions (atomic or holistic), not domain tests.
  • The Sysops Squad monolith, its components and its single-schema data model are the running case for the rest of the book.
Terms to carry forward
  • Least worst combination of trade-offs: the realistic aim, not "best".
  • ADR: Context, Decision, Consequences.
  • Fitness function: objective integrity assessment of architecture characteristics (atomic, holistic, dynamic, manual).
  • Operational (OLTP) vs analytical data.
  • Architecture quantum: an independently deployable unit with its coupling (Chapter 2).
  • Sysops Squad: the book's running case study.
