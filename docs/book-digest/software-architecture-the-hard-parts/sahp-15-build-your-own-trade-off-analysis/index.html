Software Architecture: The Hard Parts 15: Build Your Own Trade-Off Analysis

Source: Neal Ford, Mark Richards, Pramod Sadalage and Zhamak Dehghani, Software Architecture: The Hard Parts (O'Reilly, 2021), chapter 15. Previous: Software Architecture: The Hard Parts 14: Managing Analytical Data. Next: Software Architecture: The Hard Parts, Appendices A to C.

Takeaways
  • Generic solutions are rarely complete for a specific architecture. The chapter turns the book's method into a three-step process: find what is entangled, analyse how it is coupled, assess the trade-offs of change, iterating until only design is left.
  • Techniques: prefer qualitative analysis (true quantitative comparison is rare), use MECE lists (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive), avoid the out-of-context trap, model relevant domain scenarios, give stakeholders the bottom line rather than overwhelming evidence, and avoid snake oil and evangelism.
  • Epilogue: the Sysops Squad turned around through business drivers, collaboration between application and database teams, and disciplined trade-off analysis recorded in tables and ADRs: "That's architecture."

15.1 The scene: the retrospective

  • Months after the business sponsors almost cancelled the Sysops Squad support-contract line, Bailey (the main business sponsor) opens a retrospective. What worked, in the team's words:
    • Business drivers as the way to frame problems; before, IT looked only at the technical side.
    • Collaboration between database and application teams (Dana).
    • Trade-off analysis, with Logan's guidance, which justified solutions from a business perspective (the sponsors say the early business justifications are what earned IT a last chance).
  • Keeping it up:
    • discipline
    • Keep creating trade-off tables for all decisions, keep documenting with ADRs, keep collaborating
    • Is that extra process? Logan: "No
    • That's architecture."

15.2 The three-step process

  1. Find what parts are entangled together.
  2. Analyse how they are coupled to one another.
  3. Assess trade-offs by determining the impact of change to interdependent systems.

Finding entangled dimensions

  • Entanglement is unique to an architecture but discoverable by experienced developers, architects, operations staff and others who know the ecosystem.

Coupling

  • Simplest definition: if someone changes X, will it possibly force Y to change?
  • Static coupling diagrams (chapter 2) have no generic tool, but a team can build one manually or by automation. For a microservice, gather:
    • operating system and container dependencies;
    • dependencies from transitive dependency management (frameworks, libraries);
    • persistence dependencies (databases, search engines, cloud environments);
    • architecture integration points needed for the service to bootstrap;
    • messaging infrastructure (such as a broker) for communicating with other quanta.
  • The diagram does not include quanta whose only coupling is workflow communication: AssignTicket and ManageTicket cooperating in a workflow with no other coupling are statically independent but dynamically coupled during the workflow
    • Teams with automated environments can generate the coupling documentation as the system builds.
  • For this book the authors looked at hundreds of distributed architecture examples (microservices and others) and found them sensitive to communication, consistency and coordination, hence the three dimensions of dynamic quantum coupling
    • This shows iterative design: "no architect is so brilliant that their first draft is always perfect"
    • Sample topologies for workflows allow a matrix of trade-offs and faster, more thorough analysis than ad hoc approaches.

Analyse coupling points

  • Model the possible combinations lightly (skip infeasible ones) and decide which forces need trade-off analysis
    • Here: coupling, complexity, responsiveness/availability and scale/elasticity, besides the three forces
    • Each design (named saga) is rated in isolation, then all are combined:

  • Two observations from the matrix: a direct inverse correlation between coupling and scale/elasticity (more services involved in a workflow means harder design for scale), and a similar, less direct one between coupling and responsiveness/availability (the more services involved, the likelier a service failure fails the workflow).
  • (The book's Table 15-1 lists the Parallel Saga's coordination as "Centralized", i.e. orchestrated.)

Assess trade-offs

  • With a platform for iterative what-if scenarios, fix the fundamental trade-off first (here synchronous versus asynchronous communication); that choice limits later ones
    • Then repeat on the decisions it forces, until the difficult (entangled) decisions are solved
    • "What's left is design."

15.3 Trade-off techniques

Qualitative versus quantitative analysis

  • Virtually none of the book's tables are quantitative, because two architectures always differ too much for true quantitative comparison
    • Statistical analysis over a large set of examples still supports a reasonable qualitative scale (the comparative scale in Table 15-2 came from many implementations of each combination).
  • Organisations can do likewise:
    • a dimensional matrix of coupled concerns, representative examples (existing systems or targeted spikes)
    • The authors recommend honing qualitative analysis, since few opportunities for true quantitative analysis exist in architecture.

MECE lists

  • Compare like with like
    • Comparing a simple message queue with an ESB (which contains a queue plus dozens of other components) is invalid
    • MECE (borrowed from technology strategy): mutually exclusive (no overlapping capabilities between items; to compare only messaging, reduce the ESB to its messaging capability) and collectively exhaustive (all the options in the decision space are covered; evaluating an ESB and a simple queue but not Kafka for high-performance messaging leaves a gap).
  • Aim: cover a category with no holes or overlaps. The ecosystem keeps evolving, so before a long-term decision check that a new capability has not changed the criteria.

The out-of-context trap

  • Keep a decision in its context, or external factors distort it
    • Example: shared service versus shared library
    • A generic matrix favoured the library overall, but when the actual context was added the decision criteria changed (Figures 15-3 and 15-4).
  • Two lessons:
    • finding the best narrow context lets an architect consider fewer options, which is how "embrace simple designs" can actually be achieved
    • and iterative design (diagramming sample solutions and playing qualitative what-if games) reveals the context a decision belongs in.

Model relevant domain cases

  • Decisions need domain drivers. Question: one payment service or one per payment type? The generic disintegrators and integrators (chapter 7) are a start; model scenarios:
    • Update the credit card service: separate services give better maintainability, testability and deployability through quantum-level isolation, but often need duplicated code to avoid static coupling between services, damaging that benefit.
    • Add a payment type (reward points): separate services highlight extensibility.
    • A multi-type payment workflow:
      • separate services need coordination, best by an orchestrator (chapter 11), which likely hurts performance and makes consistency harder
      • avoiding an orchestrator just moves the workflow logic elsewhere (semantic coupling can only be increased by implementation).
  • The real trade-off emerges: performance and data consistency (single service) versus extensibility and agility (separate services).

Prefer bottom line over overwhelming evidence

  • Architects gather much detail that is arcane to non-technical stakeholders and can overwhelm their ability to help decide
    • Reduce the analysis to a few key points (sometimes aggregates of trade-offs).
  • Example (Table 15-3), credit card processing, synchronous REST calls from an orchestrator versus asynchronous message queues:
    • Synchronous:
      • the customer must wait for the approval process to start, but it is guaranteed to start before the request ends
      • the application is rejected if the orchestrator is down.
    • Asynchronous: no wait, and submission does not depend on the orchestrator, but no guarantee the process has started.
    • Bottom line for stakeholders: is a guarantee that approval starts immediately more important than responsiveness and fault tolerance?

Avoiding snake oil and evangelism

  • Evangelism (enhancing the good, diminishing the bad) is a luxury for tech leads and developers and gets architects in trouble, because the trade-offs "always eventually return to complicate things"
    • Be wary of any tool or technique promising shocking new capabilities, and make evangelists give an honest account of good and bad: nothing in software architecture is all good.
  • Case (Figure 15-10):
    • an architect who valued extensibility on past projects wants to keep one publish-and-subscribe topic that broadcasts changes and add bid history to the workflow, versus point-to-point messaging per consumer
    • Scenario analysis:
    • Single topic
      • problems: one large contract for everyone (the stamp coupling anti-pattern, creating accidental coupling: a change by one team requires all to coordinate)
      • every consumer sees all data (security and PII, chapter 14)
      • consumers are operationally coupled (operations cannot auto-scale bid capture and tracking on queue depth without the others).
    • Individual queues
      • (bid capture, tracking, analytics, history): a contract per consumer, security and data access in each producer-consumer contract (different information and rate of change), independent monitoring and scaling
      • Not perfect, just different trade-offs.

  • Then consult interested parties (operations, enterprise architects, business analysts) on which set of trade-offs matters more.
  • Another case:
    • a tech lead wanted one author to argue against a monorepo versus trunk-based development ("it's not an argument if two sides don't exist")
    • The author declined the foil role, noting the claimed monorepo benefits needed discipline the team had not shown, agreed to try it while gathering metrics, and had the team build fitness functions against the anti-pattern of accidental coupling between projects through repository proximity.
Don't allow others to force you into evangelising something: bring it back to trade-offs.
  • The authors advise becoming the objective arbiter of trade-offs rather than chasing silver bullets.

15.4 Sysops Squad saga: epilogue

  • Generic advice is not enough
    • the team must do trade-off analysis constantly, which is an advantage: isolating dimensions and analysing trade-offs produces concrete knowledge of your own architecture
    • boil the trade-offs for a problem down to a small number you can model and test.
  • Logan's closing analogy:
    • structural engineers have math and predictive tools but their building is hard and expensive; software is "softer", and testing is the engineering rigor of software development
    • Testing with objective outcomes moves trade-off analysis from qualitative to quantitative, "from speculation to engineering"
    • The more concrete facts learned about your own ecosystem, the more precise the analysis.

Summary

  • The book's method: entangle, couple, trade off; name the forces, model scenarios, test, and give the business a plain choice recorded in an ADR.
Terms to carry forward
  • Entangled dimensions, static vs dynamic coupling (workflow-only partners).
  • Qualitative vs quantitative analysis.
  • MECE list: mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive.
  • Out-of-context trap.
  • Bottom line over overwhelming evidence.
  • Evangelism / snake oil; objective arbiter of trade-offs.
  • Fitness function as the governance backstop for an experiment.
