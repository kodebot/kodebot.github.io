The Staff Engineer's Path 1: Foreword and Introduction

Source: Tanya Reilly, The Staff Engineer's Path (O'Reilly, 2022), foreword by Camille Fournier and the Introduction. Own-words summary, much shorter than the source.

Takeaways
  • Two paths lead on from senior engineer: management (well mapped) and staff engineer (a technical leader without reports, poorly signposted). Both need many of the same skills the further you go.
  • Staff work rests on three pillars: big-picture thinking, execution, leveling up others, standing on a foundation of technical knowledge.
  • You also need "humaning" skills (communication, framing, mentorship); the book calls them the flying buttresses of a cathedral. They are learnable.
  • The book is about the technical track, not a technical book; it will not teach your domain skills.

1.1 Foreword (Camille Fournier)

  • The foreword's author wrote The Manager's Path (2016) to take management seriously and to stop it being the default for ambitious engineers.
  • The staff+ path lacked a similar guide, so engineers who did not want large teams saw no way forward except management.
    • The unspoken rule was "work out how to climb it yourself", which she calls frustrating and bias-ridden.
  • Reilly's book names the underlying skills (big-picture thinking, execution, leveling up) rather than dictating the exact mix for each level above senior.

1.2 Two paths

  • Manager:
    • a common, perhaps default step for engineers who communicate clearly, stay calm in a crisis and help colleagues. It is well studied
    • "promotion" and "leadership" are often assumed to mean becoming someone's boss.
  • Staff engineer: many companies now allow growth without reports, but the track is "muddy and poorly signposted".
    • Criteria for hiring or promotion can be vague and not actionable.
    • Reilly spoke with staff engineers unsure what was expected, and managers who did not know how to work with them.
    • Even when expectations are clear ("be a technical leader, influence without authority"), the road is not: "how? Where do you start?"
  • The author is a senior principal engineer (parallel to a senior director) with 20 years in the industry; she chose the track because the technical work gives her energy.

1.3 The three pillars

Pillar What it means Example from the text
Big-picture thinking step back, see context, think beyond the current time software that is easy to decommission; predicting what the company needs in three years
Execution messier, more ambiguous projects with more people needs political capital, influence or culture change
Leveling up raise standards and skills around you, deliberately and by being a role model teaching, mentoring, review comments
  • The foundation is technical knowledge and experience, and it stays critical.
    • Solutions must solve the problem, review comments must improve the work, and opinions must be right.
  • On top you need "humaning" skills:
    • communication and leadership
    • navigating complexity
    • putting your work in perspective
    • mentorship, sponsorship and delegation
    • framing a problem so other people care about it
    • acting like a leader whether you feel like one or not.
  • Aptitude varies by pillar:
    • someone may excel at finishing big projects yet find it intimidating to choose between two strategic directions
    • another reads where the industry is going but loses control of the room in an incident
    • another lifts everyone's skills but struggles to build consensus.
  • The book does not prescribe: "it depends on the context" is often the honest answer, so it teaches how to navigate the ambiguity and stay aligned with other leaders.

1.4 The book's three parts

Part Chapters Content
I. The Big Picture 1 What would you say you do here? 2 Three Maps 3 Creating the Big Picture your role, your work in context and organisation, and writing a technical vision or strategy
II. Execution 4 Finite Time 5 Leading Big Projects 6 Why Have We Stopped? choosing what to work on and spending credibility, leading cross-team projects, handling obstacles and endings
III. Leveling Up 7 You're a Role Model Now (Sorry) 8 Good Influence at Scale 9 What's Next? being a role model and the adult in the room, teaching, coaching and reviews, your own growth and career options
  • Reilly's warning: if you think technical skills are the only ones that matter, you may get the most from the book, because persuading others and unblocking organisational gridlock make work less annoying.
  • Staying at senior level, or moving between manager and staff tracks, is also fine; the book is for those who want to help meet organisational goals while building technical muscle.

Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the book)

  • The book says the leadership skills are learnable, which matters if you doubt your English: staff-level influence is built from framing, preparation and listening, not from fluent improvisation.
  • This week, before one meeting, write your point in three sentences (the problem, why people should care, what you propose) and send it ahead, so the room reads your words before it hears your accent.

Summary points

  • Two valid paths; the staff path is learnable though unmapped.
  • Three pillars on a technical foundation, braced by people skills.
  • The rest of the book walks the pillars in order: picture, execution, leveling up.
Terms to carry forward
  • Staff engineer (staff+): a technical leader without direct reports.
  • Three pillars: big-picture thinking, execution, leveling up.
  • Humaning skills: Reilly's term for communication, influence and people skills.
