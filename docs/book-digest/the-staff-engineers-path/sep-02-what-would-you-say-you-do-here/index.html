The Staff Engineer's Path 2: What Would You Say You Do Here?

Source: Tanya Reilly, The Staff Engineer's Path (O'Reilly, 2022), chapter 1. Own-words summary, much shorter than the source.

Takeaways
  • A staff engineer is a leader without reports in a technical role who aims to be autonomous, sets technical direction and communicates often and well.
  • Organisations need them for three jobs: seeing the big picture (avoiding local maxima), leading cross-team projects, and being a good influence.
  • Describe your role by reporting chain, scope, shape and primary focus, then write it down and align with your manager.
  • Scope too broad or too narrow both cost impact. Your job is ultimately whatever your organisation needs it to be.

2.1 What even is a staff engineer?

  • Where only management offers a way up, engineers face a stark choice: stay in the craft or grow the career.
    • Many companies now have a parallel technical track.
    • Ladders vary (levels.fyi compares them); senior is the "anchor" level, often seen as the "tenure" level where you may stop.
    • Marco Rogers, quoted: below senior people grow autonomy; above it, impact and responsibility.
  • The first rung above senior is usually staff engineer; staff+ (Will Larson's term) covers all roles above senior.
    • A senior staff engineer matches a senior manager; a principal matches a director. Moving between tracks is a sideways step.
  • Titles matter.
    • Reilly cites the Medium engineering team: titles show progress, vest authority in people who might not receive it automatically, and tell outsiders the expected competence.
    • Implicit bias is the reason: a 2015 survey found about half of 557 Black and Latina women in STEM had been mistaken for janitors or administrative staff, while white and Asian male engineers are often assumed senior and more technical.
    • A title anchors expectations so you spend less time proving yourself in each meeting, and the title you hold shapes the job offers you receive (Reilly had only three recruiters in her life offer a higher title than hers).

2.2 Why organisations need staff engineers

The big picture

  • Good decisions need context: what you are trying to do, time and money, risk tolerance, business need. Gathering it takes time.
  • Teams tend to optimise for themselves, and a local maximum (best for one team) can be a poor choice for the company.
    • Example (Figure 1-2):
      • a team picks software A because it just works, while B needs two sprints
      • A leaves legal and security with ongoing work and IT with a permanent special case. B pays back within a quarter, but only someone with a wider lens sees it.
  • Anticipating the future matters too: what will you regret in a year, wish you had started in three? Groups need shared technical strategies.
  • Why not managers or the CTO?
    • Small teams and small companies often can, and then need no staff engineers.
    • Management authority can overshadow technical judgment, because reports hesitate to argue with a manager.
    • Managing people is a full-time job, so either the team or the technical direction gets neglected.
    • Routing every decision to a CTO is inefficient and disempowering. Engineers do not set direction alone: managers who assign headcount must be part of the big decisions.
  • Architects: the book treats design and architecture as part of the staff+ role, though not every company does.

Cross-team projects

  • Reality is not the ideal (a greenfield project with teams matching the architecture, an "Inverse Conway Maneuver"): the teams already exist and have other priorities, dependencies surface midway, boundaries overlap, and the hard parts are spelunking legacy code and negotiating with busy teams.
  • Responsibilities end up owned by nobody or by two teams, information is mangled, and teams make excellent local decisions while the project stalls.
  • One person who feels ownership of the whole project helps:
    • scopes the work and writes the proposal
    • coauthors the high-level design and is the main contact
    • keeps engineering standards and asks hard questions
    • coaches the part leads
    • unblocks the project
    • and tells the story outside it.
  • TPM or staff engineer? TPMs own delivery; staff engineers own design and engineering quality (robust systems, caution about technical debt). Together they can be "a dream team".

Good influence

  • Software matters: bugs can hurt well-being and income, and have contributed to deaths in plane crashes and medical equipment failures (Reilly cites the Wikipedia list of software bugs).
  • Deadlines tempt teams to skip tests or rubber-stamp reviews; seniors should review code and designs, share architectural practice, and build tooling that makes everyone faster and safer.
  • Norms are set by the most respected engineers:
    • if the senior engineers do not write tests, nobody will
    • Cultural norms too: celebrating others' work, respect, asking clarifying questions.
  • Leadership does not fit around a senior engineer's coding load: "it isn't a distraction from the job: it is the job."

2.3 What is my job? Five axioms

  1. Not a manager, but a leader.
    • You hold the same seniority as a manager at your level and are expected to be "the grown-up in the room". Whenever it feels like "someone should do something", it may be you.
    • Larger goals need larger groups, collaboration and alignment, so you cannot skip the "squishy human stuff". You will also be a role model whether or not you want to be.
    • Without reports, you do not manage performance, vacations or expenses, nor fire or promote, though managers should value your opinion of others' skills.
    • Leadership takes forms such as: happy-path designs that protect colleagues, reviews that build confidence, teaching, setting direction, and a reputation that makes people trust your plans.
    • You can be an introvert, but you cannot be a jerk: quiet engineers can set strong direction, but a difficult engineer who is worked around is now a liability
      • Reilly points to Kind Engineering for practical advice.
  2. A technical role.
    • You need a solid foundation and high standards, and reviews that are instructive
      • You may write little code: you take messy, ambiguous problems and do just enough to make them manageable by someone else, which also grows less experienced engineers.
    • What matters is that problems get solved, not how (codebases, documents, data analysis or many one-on-ones).
  3. Aim to be autonomous.
    • Your manager shares context and you tell them what is important as much as the reverse
      • Sabrina Leandro, quoted: where do you find the magic backlog of high-impact work? "You create it!"
    • You choose how to spend your finite hours, weighing priority, time and the relationship with whoever asked.
    • Autonomy carries responsibility: if a request would be harmful, speak up (and build a reputation first for being trustworthy and correct).
  4. Set technical direction. Make sure direction gets decided, decided well and written down. You need not invent all of it; you ensure an agreed, well-understood solution exists.
  5. Communicate often and well. Almost everything involves moving information between heads: "the better you are at being understood, the easier your job will be."

2.4 Understanding your role

  • Reporting chain (no industry standard; chief architect, CTO's office, directors or managers):
    • Reporting high gives broad information and learning from a senior person, but less manager time and advocacy.
    • Reporting low gives focused attention and an advocate, but less influence, filtered information and possibly a less experienced manager
      • Have skip-level meetings with your manager's manager.

  • Scope: the domain or teams you watch and are responsible for, without formal authority.
    • Inside it, influence short- and long-term goals, know the major decisions, represent people without leverage, and grow the next generation of seniors
      • Be clear whether a team is your focus or just home base.
    • Ignore scope in a crisis: there is no "not my job" in an outage.
    • Too broad: lack of impact, bottleneck, decision fatigue, missing relationships.
    • Too narrow: lack of impact, opportunity cost, overshadowing others, overengineering.
  • Shape, questions to ask:
    • depth-first or breadth-first (match your preference to your scope)
    • which of Yonatan Zunger's four disciplines (core technical, product, project, people management) you gravitate toward, and who covers the one you hate
    • how much you want to code
    • how well you handle delayed gratification, since strategy and culture change can take months before a signal
    • whether you keep one foot on the manager track (a tech lead manager is a famously difficult hybrid; some alternate in a pendulum).
    • A hyperspecialist in a business-critical domain can succeed without influencing others, but Zunger notes influence wanes over time and such jobs are rare.
  • Archetypes (Will Larson, "Staff Archetypes"): tech leads, architects, solvers, right hands.
  • Primary focus: every choice is also a choice of what not to do.
    • At staff level, work must be important (not necessarily glamorous; often unseen, grungy work), and it must need you (do not take a task a midlevel engineer could do, or a project already crowded with wise voices).
    • Reilly's idiom from her child: "You don't plant grass in your only barrel."

2.5 Aligning on scope, shape and focus

  • Write down your understanding of your job and share it with your manager (a technique from Cian Synnott), so you find out early if your mental model matches theirs.
  • The worked example, "What Does Ali Do?", is a one-year plan for a breadth-first architect who spends about half her time on technical direction for retail sales engineering, 30 percent on a cross-team project (NewMerchandising), and the rest on cross-organisation work and community work, including one week in ten on the incident commander rotation
    • It lists goals, sample activities and what success looks like.
  • Get it "right enough"; rewrite it when priorities change.
  • Is that your job? Your job is whatever makes the organisation succeed: Reilly's examples include carrying fuel up 17 flights of stairs during a 2012 data-centre outage (Squarespace colleagues), bucket chains when an ISP machine room flooded, and racking servers in San Jose for a late Google project in 2005.

Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the book)

  • Write your own "What does [your name] do?" page this week:
    • one paragraph, three goals, what success looks like
    • Writing is where your command of the content is strongest, and a shared document removes a lot of ambiguity about your role.
  • Ask your manager one question: "Which of these would you most like me to stop doing?" It is short and plain.

Summary points

  • Leader without reports, technical, autonomous, direction-setting, communicative.
  • Know your reporting chain, scope, shape and focus, and write it down.
Terms to carry forward
  • Staff+: every role above senior.
  • Local maximum: the best decision for one team, not for the whole.
  • Scope: the domain and teams you pay attention to and share responsibility for.
  • Tech lead manager (TLM): a hybrid of technical lead and manager.
  • Hyperspecialist: a senior engineer who succeeds purely through deep expertise.
