The Staff Engineer's Path 4: Creating the Big Picture

Source: Tanya Reilly, The Staff Engineer's Path (O'Reilly, 2022), chapter 3. Own-words summary, much shorter than the source.

Takeaways
  • When the treasure map is missing or disputed, create it: a technical vision (the future state) and/or a technical strategy (the plan of action).
  • Agreement is the work. Prepare (sponsor, core group, scope, achievability), then write in a loop of ideas, drafts, interviews, thinking and decisions, aligning as you go.
  • A strategy's kernel is diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent actions (Rumelt). Good strategy is usually boring and synthesises existing ideas.
  • Launch means endorsement, staffing and a story people repeat; revisit it yearly.

4.1 Why a big picture, and what it is

  • The opening story: three simultaneous requests to announce three different plans for the same troubled system (SystemX): more staffing, deprecation, a new working group.
  • Without shared direction, decentralised teams hit a tragedy of the commons: local maxima, neglected shared concerns, missing context, and big unmade decisions that slow or block projects
    • Postponed questions become baked-in assumptions (a sign: the same topics eat code and design review time).

  • Technical vision: the future after the objectives are met.
    • Written down, it creates a shared reality; senior people often imagine different futures and miss it until they conflict.
    • Contents may include values and goals, decision principles, decisions already made, a diagram. It must be clear, opinionated and realistic.
    • Figure 3-1: visions nest at different scopes (engineering-wide, team) and inherit from larger ones.
  • Technical strategy: how you will reach the goals past the obstacles. Rumelt's kernel:
    • Diagnosis: simpler than reality, found through patterns or metaphors (it takes time).
    • Guiding policy: a short signpost for bypassing obstacles.
    • Coherent actions: what you will do and, importantly, what you will not; usually organisational as well as technical.
    • Example from the book:
      • Isaac Perez Moncho's strategy freed a strong DevOps team's time so they could automate more and free more, a positive feedback loop
      • A strategy must be realistic: one that cannot be staffed is a waste.
  • Do you need one?
    • If you can state the goal easily it may be just a design-doc goals section or a pull request description
    • make the lightest thing that works. Patrick Shields encourages small strategies for practice.
  • Resources named: Good Strategy/Bad Strategy (Rumelt), Fundamentals of Software Architecture (Richards and Ford), Will Larson's survey of engineering strategies, Technology Strategy Patterns (Hewitt), Mattie Toia's "Getting to Commitment", Patrick Shields's "Technical Strategy Power Chords".

4.2 The approach

  • Treat it as a project: "getting people to agree isn't a chore between you and the real work: the agreement is the work."
  • Embrace boring ideas.
    • Early in a career, senior engineers look like wizards who save the day with technobabble
    • usually the ideas already exist and someone must weigh them, align everyone and decide.
    • Camille Fournier:
      • good strategy is pretty boring
      • if you write something interesting it is probably wrong. Will Larson: write your brilliant ideas in a giant document, delete it and never mention them again.
  • Join an expedition in progress:
    • share the lead, follow their lead (be the "grizzled best supporting actor"), or step away
    • Avoid "ape games" (dominance and politicking); do not fight for a marginally better path at the cost of no decision
    • If you must change a dangerous direction, join and redirect.
  • Get a sponsor: an executive who justifies the work, sets criteria, breaks ties or names a DRI (directly responsible individual).
    • Bring something they want and a 50-word pitch; Melissa Binde once coached Reilly to retell her project from another angle
      • Keep sponsorship current (Sean Rees: it can wane quietly)
      • A staff+ engineer cannot directly sponsor, since sponsors decide staffing.
  • Core group:
    • two to four people with a real time commitment (for example 8 to 12 hours a week), plus a wider circle you interview and update
    • Use your topographical map to decide who must be on side and bring dissenters along
    • Say whether you are lead or first among equals.
  • Scope: enough to solve the problem, with someone from any team you will touch; avoid magical thinking (a change of CEO).
  • Achievable? Ask a veteran for a gut check. If unsolvable by you: do it anyway, recruit skills, reduce scope, shrug and move on, or update your résumé.
  • Checklist:
    • we need this
    • it will be boring and obvious
    • there is no existing effort or I have joined it
    • there is organisational support
    • we agree what we are creating
    • the problem is solvable (by me)
    • I am not lying to myself.
  • Make it official: set it up as a project with kickoff, milestones and reporting, and work out loud, because secrecy may mean weak support.

4.3 The writing

  • Iterate, but timebox; stop at diminishing returns and revisit later.
  • Initial ideas: which documents exist, what needs to change (aim big, for example 20-minute deploys), what is great as it is, what is important, and what Future You will wish you had done ("sending a cookie into the future").
  • Writing as a group: a leader's first draft (consistent but biased; flag arbitrary choices, such as "I rolled some dice") or several drafts aggregated (name an aggregator and tiebreaker first; Mojtaba Hosseini's group got emotionally attached to their own drafts).
  • Interviews: many people, including leaders, influencers and those close to the work; broad questions then a straw man; end with "What else should I have asked?"
  • Thinking time:
    • each person processes differently (Reilly writes; Carl sleeps on it)
    • beware describing the problem in terms of a chosen solution
    • repeat "Yes, but why?" (Cindy Sridharan on passion projects sold as benefits).
  • Decisions:
    • Trade-offs: use "even over" statements, such as ease of use even over performance.
    • Consensus: use the IETF's "rough consensus" and ask "Can anyone not live with A?"; do not wait for full agreement; name a tiebreaker in advance; a sponsor is a last resort.
    • Not deciding is a decision,
      • usually poor: it keeps the status quo and forces dependent decisions to hedge
      • If you wait, say for what, and timebox
      • A reasoned "decide not to decide" is allowed (for example, getting it wrong costs a lot and gets little reward).
    • Show your work: record trade-offs and alternatives so people do not relitigate.
    • Making someone unhappy is inevitable.
  • Alignment: know the final audience; update your sponsor at checkpoints.

  • Be reasonable: the Overton window (Figure 3-3).
  • Nemawashi (a Toyota Production System term): lay the groundwork so the decision meeting finds consensus already formed.
  • Alignment goes both ways: you may drop or add points.
  • Story: comprehensible, relatable, comfortable. Mark Barnes's slogan "Cloud only 2020" was "the one thing from our tech strategy that developers could quote" (Sarah Wells).
  • Final draft: make it skimmable (white space, bullets, personas, a concrete hard scenario, little jargon) and add a deck or one-pager.

4.4 The launch

  • Make it the organisation's, not just yours:
    • endorsement by the top authority, an official look (internal site, closed comments, a senior contact), and real staffing, budget and OKRs
    • Stay with it.
  • Keep it fresh: revisit in a year or sooner; explain what changed; tell a new story.

4.5 Case study: SockMatcher

  • Scenario:
    • a monolith matching odd socks, gloves and buttons with if-statements spread across billing and personalization, a three-hour deploy, an unversioned API, janky login, and pressure to add food storage containers and lids
    • Past working groups stalled as rooms of 20 who disagreed.
  • Approach:
    • study why predecessors failed (Pierre handed down a design; Geneva's coalition stalled)
    • treat organisational alignment as the crux
    • win sponsor Jesse (who needs the food-storage launch not to slip) after sponsor Jody declines
    • build a core group of Geneva and Fran
    • scope to a one-year strategy with an OKR naming the author as DRI.
  • Diagnosis, guiding policy, actions:
    • as in the diagram: make billing and personalization easy and safe to integrate with
    • add feature flags, staff, self-service modules, a pilot with the food-storage team. The microservices alternative was documented and rejected.
  • Launch:
    • some thought it boring or wrong
    • the documented alternatives meant no surprises
    • Jody's team offered the feature-flag system
    • the next product (board-game pieces) reused the new isolation.

Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the book)

  • Draft a one-page diagnosis of a problem your team keeps arguing about, in plain sentences (what is happening, why it matters), and ask two colleagues one question each: "What else should I have asked you?" Writing and interviewing play to preparation, not live improvisation.
  • Practise the 50-word pitch for a sponsor, say it aloud twice, and record it on your phone to hear the pace.
  • Try the question "Can anyone not live with this?" in a meeting: it is short and plain, and shifts a discussion from agreement to objections.

Summary points

  • Vision is the destination; strategy is the path; the kernel is diagnosis, policy, actions.
  • Sponsor, core group, scope, achievability, then make it official.
  • Interview widely, decide, document trade-offs, align early, tell a story, launch with endorsement.
Terms to carry forward
  • Technical vision / technical strategy.
  • Kernel of a strategy: diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions.
  • DRI: directly responsible individual.
  • Rough consensus: "lack of disagreement is more important than agreement" (IETF).
  • Nemawashi: laying groundwork so consensus exists before the decision.
  • Overton window: ideas tolerated without seeming extreme.
