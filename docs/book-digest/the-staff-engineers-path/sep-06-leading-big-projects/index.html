The Staff Engineer's Path 6: Leading Big Projects

Source: Tanya Reilly, The Staff Engineer's Path (O'Reilly, 2022), chapter 5. Own-words summary, much shorter than the source.

Takeaways
  • Big projects are hard because of ambiguity (unclear direction, messy humans, legacy systems), not usually technology. A great lead brings perseverance, courage and willingness to talk to people.
  • Start by managing the overwhelm, then build context: goals, customers, success metrics, sponsors, constraints, risks, history and team.
  • Structure the project: roles (RACI), recruiting, scope and milestones, honest estimates, logistics and a kickoff.
  • Drive it: explore the problem before the solution, build shared mental models and names, write designs down, avoid the standard pitfalls, communicate status honestly, and ask for help when blocked.

6.1 The start of a project

  • Assume you are the named technical lead of a project of several months across multiple teams, with no one reporting to you but expected to deliver
    • You are responsible for the whole problem, including the parts that fall between teams (Figure 5-1 shows a lead with contacts or subleads per team).
  • At the start, nobody may agree what the mandate is, who follows whom, or how other senior engineers fit.
  • If you feel overwhelmed: it is normal; Polina Giralt: "that feeling of discomfort is called learning".
    • Impostor feelings may mean one of your five resources is low; George Mauer: "99% of people don't know better than I what to do".
    • The difficulty is the point: if it were easy they would not need you. "A mistake will not destroy you. Ten mistakes will not destroy you."
  • Five things to do:
    1. Create an anchor: a private document as an external brain, with leads, rumours, to-dos.
    2. Talk to your sponsor, going in with a written statement of what you think success is, and confirm it. If you will rarely speak again, get everything up front.
    3. Decide who gets your uncertainty: not your junior engineers, who look to you for stability, but a manager, mentor or peer who says "this is hard for me too".
    4. Give yourself a win: take any step (talk, draw, write); early on you can say "I'm new to this, so tell me if I have this wrong".
    5. Use your strengths: start with code, people or documents.
  • Context to clarify:
    • Goals: why this project; if it will not achieve its goal, find out early.
    • Customer needs:
      • even internal projects have customers (Reilly's first week: "Maybe they don't. We think they do, but we have no way of knowing.", two teams on the same floor)
      • Shadow users, ask for the API they wish they had, show sketches, avoid jargon, listen to what they actually say (Gergely Orosz's collection: "You are also 'product'").
    • Success metrics:
      • code written does not mean a problem is solved; sometimes success is deleting code
      • Sarah Wells measured a 150-microservice migration to Kubernetes by time spent keeping the cluster healthy and snarky Slack messages about missing functionality
      • If you started the project, be most sceptical of your own idea.
    • Sponsors, stakeholders and customers: who wants it and pays.
    • Fixed constraints: deadlines, budget, busy teams, difficult people; describe them diplomatically since the document may be forwarded.
    • Risks: "moonshot" or "roofshot"; unknowns, dependencies, key people; fear of wasted effort is eased by frequent iterative releases.
    • History: earlier failed attempts, leftover components, resentment; "respect what came before".
    • Team: a contact per team or a sublead per area; do not waste time in power struggles with other leaders.

6.2 Giving the project structure

  • Roles: at senior levels, a very senior engineer, engineering manager and TPM blur together. Lay out responsibilities in a table.
    • Table 5-1 example:
      • the product manager sets customer needs, KPIs and user-visible decisions
      • the technical lead designs the high-level architecture and decides the technical approach, and coaches
      • the engineering manager recruits and handles performance
      • the TPM sets timelines and communicates status
      • the team codes and tests.
    • RACI
      • (responsible, accountable, consulted, informed): a staff engineer friend at Google used it to stop both endless discussion with no decider and endless relitigation
      • "The uncontroversial structure is the real superpower."
    • Lara Hogan's team-leader Venn diagram assigns the stories of what, how and why (and perhaps when).
    • If you are unsure of your ownership, ask: "Here's what I think I'm responsible for. Do you agree?" The lead implicitly fills any empty role.
  • Recruiting:
    • choose people who complement you and love the work you hate
    • Mohit Cheppudira looks for leads with optimism, conflict resolution and communication as well as technical judgment
    • Their success is yours.
  • Scope:
    • the project triangle (time, budget, scope; "fast, cheap, good: pick two"). Deliver incrementally
    • Jackie Benowitz treats milestones as beta tests, so requirements may shift
    • keep a milestone always in sight with a date
    • use workstreams and phases.
  • Estimating:
    • almost nobody is good at it. Hunt and Thomas (The Pragmatic Programmer): the only way is gaining experience on the same project
    • keep an estimate log. Tell other teams early.
  • Logistics: meetings, informal communication (a social channel, icebreakers, meme threads), status format, a documentation home page (the project's fixed point), development practices.
  • Kickoff meeting: who, goals, history, structures, next steps, what you want people to do (be explicit), how to ask questions.

6.3 Driving the project

  • Kripa Krishnan's "Avoid the Lake!": driving is active, choosing the route and reacting to hazards.
  • Exploring:
    • be suspicious of a day-one design with implementation details ("Build a GraphQL server with Node.js...")
    • goals that merely describe the implementation mean too little exploration.
    • Find what each team thinks the project is, including unspoken assumptions and different vocabularies; form an elevator pitch and a list of what you are not doing.
    • Only then approach options; keep an open mind, prefer existing solutions, and count running costs (maintaining, operating, deleting).
  • Clarifying:
    • give everyone mental models
    • De Botton's description of facts that are "as useless and fugitive as necklace beads without a connecting chain" shows why new information needs a hook.
    • Mental models: analogies and examples (Kubernetes concepts compared to a UNIX process or a Docker container); abstractions with names ("leader election").
    • Naming: Eric Evans's "ubiquitous language"; "user", "customer" and "account" differ by department; use their words or give a glossary.
    • Pictures and graphs: before and after, nesting, ladders; a stick figure for a human; a cylinder suggests a datastore and colours carry meaning; a line trending toward a goal.

6.4 Designing: write it down

  • Why share designs:
    • you do not know people understand and agree until it is written. Short is fine
    • reviewers also check the problem and assumptions about other teams. Cian Synnott: "a written design is a very cheap iteration".
  • RFC templates (Gawande, The Checklist Manifesto: "We are not built for discipline"). Minimum headings:
    • Context: title, author, dates, status.
    • Goals: the problem and why, without implementation details.
    • Design: enough to judge feasibility.
    • Security, privacy, compliance: what you protect and from whom; if none, explain.
    • Alternatives considered and prior art:
      • "If you could solve this same problem with spreadsheets, would you still want to do it?"
      • if a plausible internal option is rejected, send the design to its owners.
    • Helpful extras: background, trade-offs, risks, dependencies, operations.
  • Wrong is better than vague: vagueness avoids argument but yields no learning.
    • Be clear who does each action (active voice: "The client will encrypt the data before it is transmitted"). Rebecca Johnson's test: if you can insert "by zombies", it is passive.
    • Eva Parish: replace bare "this" and "that" with a noun even if repetitive ("this shortage").
    • Writing is learnable (Google's Technical Writing courses, Write the Docs).

6.5 Technical pitfalls

  • Brand-new problem (it is not)
    • this looks easy
    • building for the present (three years on, with acquisitions)
    • building for the distant future ("we might need it later" is among the most expensive phrases)
    • every user just needs to...
    • we will figure out the hard part later
    • solving the small problem by making the big one harder
    • it is not a rewrite (but it is)
    • operable (3 p.m. versus 3 a.m.; an on-call team of more than three, ideally six)
    • bikeshedding (Parkinson's Law of Triviality, 1957).

6.6 Coding, communicating and navigating

  • Should you code? Joy Ebertz: coding is rarely the highest-leverage work yet gives depth, lets you feel the cost of your decisions and keeps you engaged. Beware snacking.
    • Be an exemplar, not a bottleneck (choose non-critical-path work).
    • Katrina Owen: change all existing pagination tests to one pattern so future ones copy it.
    • Ross Donaldson: "scouting and cartography": find the river, build a rough bridge, let the team own and improve it.
    • Polina Giralt: pair so knowledge spreads, or write the first pattern then hand over.
    • Reviews from a staff engineer can intimidate early-career engineers: let good enough stand, and do not be the only reviewer.
    • If you are sloppy, the team will be sloppy.
  • Communicating:
    • build familiarity so people can say "I don't know what that term means"
    • share status as impact, headlines first, with "That means..."
    • do not run a watermelon project (green outside, red inside).
  • Navigating:
    • expect trouble and be curious
    • you are accountable for rerouting, escalating or breaking the news. Facts, not rumours, and say what you want people to do. "The biggest failure is not asking for [help]."

Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the book)

  • Write a one-page "what I think success is for this project" and read it back to the sponsor, as the book suggests; writing first plays to preparation.
  • Next time you give a status update, lead with one headline sentence and one "That means...". Short, plain sentences carry weight.
  • When someone uses a term you do not know, ask once: "I don't know what that term means." The book says a team where this is normal works better, and it is easier to do if you do it first.
  • Write one design in active voice with no bare "this" and "that"; this clarity helps readers whatever their first language.

Summary points

  • Lead the whole problem; manage overwhelm; know goals, customers and metrics.
  • Set up roles, milestones, estimates and a documentation home.
  • Explore before designing; write RFCs; be wrong rather than vague.
  • Report honestly and expect obstacles.
Terms to carry forward
  • RACI: responsible, accountable, consulted, informed.
  • RFC: request for comment, a design document.
  • Watermelon project: green status hiding red reality.
  • Ubiquitous language (Eric Evans): a shared vocabulary of developers and domain experts.
  • Bikeshedding: spending time on trivial decisions.
  • Moonshot / roofshot: a huge aspirational project versus a straightforward step.
