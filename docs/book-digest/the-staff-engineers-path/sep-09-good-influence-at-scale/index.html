The Staff Engineer's Path 9: Good Influence at Scale

Source: Tanya Reilly, The Staff Engineer's Path (O'Reilly, 2022), chapter 8. Own-words summary, much shorter than the source.

Takeaways
  • Raise the skills of the people around you deliberately, using four mechanisms: advice, teaching, guardrails, opportunity.
  • Each works at three tiers: individual, group (scaled by writing, classes, processes) and catalyst (structures that keep working when you step away).
  • It is a menu, not a checklist: do the individual and group work first, and join existing initiatives before creating new ones.
  • Others' growth is your growth: "plan to give away your job".

9.1 Why, and three tiers

  • Why help others improve:
    • better colleagues make your own work easier and the software better
    • the industry keeps changing and you will need people able to adopt new tools
    • it is the right thing to do ("you're sending high standards into the future")
    • and your growth depends on a bench behind you (Bryan Liles: "My job... is to be able to influence 14,000 engineers").
  • Three tiers (Figure 8-1):
    • Individual: you work in a way that grows another person.
    • Group: you bring a skill or change of approach to many people at once.
    • Catalyst: you set up frameworks or community structures whose influence continues without your effort.
  • Table 8-1 (summary):
Individual Group Catalyst
Advice mentoring, sharing knowledge, feedback tech talks, documentation, articles mentorship program, tech talk events
Teaching code and design review, coaching, pairing, shadowing classes, codelabs onboarding curriculum, teaching people to teach
Guardrails code, change and design review processes, linters, style guides frameworks, culture change
Opportunity delegating, sponsorship, cheerleading sharing the spotlight, empowering your team a culture of opportunity, juniors who change the world
  • Caution:
    • too many programs overwhelm an organisation
    • taking part in an existing onboarding curriculum usually beats starting a new one
    • guiding one team through a difficult design may matter more than tinkering with the RFC process.

9.2 Advice

  • Who asked you? Solicited advice is requested; unsolicited is not.
    • Ask "Can I offer some advice?" first, and consider whether you have the context and the relationship.
    • Unsolicited advice is valuable when someone cannot see out of a bad situation, or you hold information they lack (the platform they plan to use is being turned down)
      • A hard example: "Your slides were amazing, but you talk to your shoes when you present and it's hard to understand you" is difficult but probably kind
      • Otherwise write a blog post and do not name the person.
  • Individual advice:
    • Mentorship:
      • sharing your experience (Neha Batra), but advice can fit one person and undermine another (Lara Hogan: "Be louder in meetings!" may harm underrepresented people because they are assessed differently)
      • "just DM the director" is easy when the director is a peer.
      • Ask whether they want to vent or want advice; rubber-duck explaining and war stories are not requests for solutions.
      • Set expectations (for example weekly for six weeks) and goals. Mentees can teach you too.
    • Answering questions:
      • be accessible (office hours, easy DMs, hang out near teams) and answer the implicit question: a senior engineer answered "no" to "Will /user give the full name?" and wasted an hour before mentioning /fulluser
      • Do not infodump; ask what they are trying to do
      • In reviews, say whether a comment is information or a request to change.
    • Feedback: be kindly honest; Reilly attends colleagues' practice talks and notes both strengths and moments where she tuned out.
    • Peer reviews:
      • two audiences, the person and the manager and calibrators
      • take "what could they do better?" literally
      • use private feedback if you fear sinking a promotion
      • watch implicit bias ("consensus building" or "indecisive", "refreshingly down to earth" or "unprofessional"; Project Include has recommendations).
  • Group and catalyst:
    • Write it down once (FAQ, how-to, channel topic) and take a microphone: at an all-hands about an incident of her choice, Reilly used a circular-dependency outage story to push dependency management
      • "Testing on the Toilet" put testing advice on one-pagers at Google.
    • Catalyst: start small on documentation culture, monthly tech talks (start with at least three organisers), a mentorship program (heavy admin, usually not in an engineer's job description; convincing a manager to run it still counts).

9.3 Teaching

  • Teaching is understanding, not receiving advice. It is useful whenever someone has less context, including asking a colleague to whiteboard their systems.
  • Unlocking a topic: set a goal (draw the system and explain it; send a first pull request; handle three to five scenarios); let the student do; leave an artifact.
  • Shadowing, pairing, reverse shadowing (Figure 8-2): you do and they watch; you work together; they do and you give notes (the most learning; also a guardrail).
  • Review to teach:
    • understand the assignment (a first draft needs foundations, not nitpicks)
    • explain why as well as what
    • give an example of better
    • say what matters (a SQL injection fix versus a nitpick or "your call")
    • choose battles (several passes: if the code does not do what it should, indentation does not matter)
    • if you mean yes, say yes, and mention what is good (reviewers hesitate to approve before others). Early-career engineers may find you intimidating
    • consider pairing instead of burying them in comments. Reilly notes context in her own change descriptions ("I'm new to this language, so I welcome nitpicking").
  • Coaching teaches people to solve problems themselves; it takes practice:
    • open questions
    • active listening
    • making space (count to five)
    • Julia Milner: giving the solution triggers "Yes, but...".
  • Group:
    • a class with a goal and exercises (high first cost, amortised)
    • a codelab (guided tutorial, for example Kotlin Koans)
    • Reilly's learning path for volunteers and interns on a networking library: day one two pull requests (a joke and their name) with some back-and-forth, day two a toy client and server run in production and a local change deployed
      • "It was just code."
  • Catalyst: teach people to teach; let them own and vary the class; add it to onboarding.

9.4 Guardrails

  • Railings on a cliff path steady you; a small stumble is not fatal. Guardrails encourage autonomy.
  • Review (code, design, change): never rubber-stamp; read every line. Look for:
    • should this exist?
    • does it solve the problem?
    • how will it fail (edge cases, malformed input, network loss, load spikes)?
    • is it understandable?
    • does it fit the bigger picture (precedent, extra work for other teams, a risky change near a launch)?
    • do the right people know (names on actions, not passive voice)?
    • Be open that you do not know everything, ask questions, stay on the same side.
  • Project guardrail: a colleague stretching into a hard project is helped by someone who asks "Just out of interest, how were you planning to balance these conflicting priorities?" Say how you can help and when to ask (Lara Hogan: "Shoot me an email if person B is unresponsive for three days; I can be your muscle").
  • Group:
    • Processes:
      • a prelaunch checklist (security approval, notice to marketing and support, feature flag, monitoring, extra load); make them lightweight and the right way the easy way
      • Reilly's process preamble: "All guidelines are wrong sometimes..
      • assume [others] are reasonable people trying their best."
    • Written decisions: style guides; paved roads (a Thoughtworks Radar-style Adopt, Trial, Assess, Hold); sparing policies; vision and strategy.
    • Robots and reminders:
      • Glen Mailer's timesheet on the exit door at eye height
      • automated reminders, linters, search results that point to the right way, templates, config checkers and presubmits (Google's SRSly: for example no more than 5 percent of servers rebooted at once; it began after automation accidentally sent a data centre's disks for erasure at once).
  • Catalyst: culture change.
    • The strongest and hardest guardrail
    • code review and tests once were not normal. Solve a real problem, choose battles (instil respect for design review rather than one exact process), offer support, find allies (consult the shadow org chart).

9.5 Opportunity

  • People learn most by doing, and every project gives visibility, relationships and résumé lines.
  • Delegation: really hand it over; a "messy, unscoped project with a bit of a safety net" teaches more than a "cleanly wrapped gift" (Lara Hogan).
    • Choose someone for whom it is a stretch, not an A+ candidate; promise support and tell them why you believe in them.
    • You will not get a clone; be a guardrail and coach.
    • Molly Graham: "giving away your Legos". Redirect questions to the new owner instead of proxying answers.
  • Sponsorship
    • is more active than mentorship and spends your social capital
    • Rosalind Chow's ABCDs: Amplify, Boost, Connect, Defend
    • Choose people who want it; beware in-group favouritism (Mitch Kapor's "mirrortocracy")
    • Also connect people with information you hold (a call for papers, a team lead role).
  • Share the spotlight:
    • leave a gap or hand off the floor, bring a junior to a meeting, ask a junior to review designs. Warning: do not delegate so much that nobody knows what you do
    • managers expect visible accomplishments.
  • Catalyst:
    • teach people to look beyond obvious candidates (inclusive interview process, bias talks, open roles on an internal job board)
    • empower colleagues who will in turn offer advice, teaching, guardrails and opportunity.
  • Warning: a new promotee need not be as good as you are now, only as good as you were when you were first promoted; if later promotees seem weaker, consider whether you have grown.
  • "Other people's growth is your growth": if you can delegate, you take on bigger problems. Bryan Liles: "How you can get pushed up is by building a whole bench behind you."

Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the book)

  • Write, do not only speak:
    • your advice scales best through documentation, short how-tos and review comments, where you can edit your English and nobody hears an accent
    • The book names writing as the way to scale advice.
  • Ask a question instead of giving an answer: open questions and "count to five" in a one-on-one suit a thoughtful, quiet style; they also give you time to listen.
  • Name what matters in review comments ("must fix", "nitpick", "your call"). It is short, plain and removes ambiguity for readers of any background.
  • Sponsor one person this week: amplify one good piece of their work in a public channel (the book's "Amplifying").

Summary points

  • Advice (if welcome), teaching (for understanding), guardrails (for safety), opportunity (the biggest lever).
  • Scale from individual to group to catalyst, but start small and join existing programs.
  • Delegate really, share the spotlight, sponsor widely, and plan to give away your job.
Terms to carry forward
  • Catalyst influence: structures that continue without you.
  • Reverse shadowing: the learner does the work and the expert watches.
  • Codelab: a guided step-by-step tutorial.
  • Guardrail: review, process, automation or culture that keeps people safe while they move fast.
  • Sponsorship ABCDs: amplify, boost, connect, defend.
  • Mirrortocracy (Mitch Kapor): hiring people like yourself.
