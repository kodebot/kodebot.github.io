Building Event-Driven Microservices 1: Why Event-Driven Microservices

Source: Adam Bellemare, Building Event-Driven Microservices, 2nd edition (O'Reilly, 2025), chapter 1. Next: Building Event-Driven Microservices 2: Fundamentals of Events and Event Streams.

Takeaways
  • An event-driven microservice is a small, purpose-built service that consumes and/or produces events from event streams, keeps its own state, and is driven by an event loop. Events are not destroyed on consumption, so many consumers can read them.
  • Event-driven microservices work because they decouple what a service does from how it accesses data. Data gets its own data communication structure, instead of being an afterthought bolted on to a service.
  • Events form a durable, canonical, replayable record; producers own the format and schema; consumers build their own models and queries.
  • Align services and bounded contexts to business needs, not technical layers.
  • Request-response microservices still have a place: hybrid architectures are the norm.

1.1 What are event-driven microservices?

  • Microservices have existed under many names
    • SOAs often chain microservices through direct synchronous calls; message-passing architectures use consumable events asynchronously
    • Event-driven architecture is not new, but handling big data sets at scale and in real time is, and needs a different style.
  • Synchronous
    • means the requester waits (blocks) for a response
    • asynchronous means it continues with other work. This book focuses on asynchronous communication through events via event streams.
  • In a modern event-driven microservices architecture, systems communicate by issuing and consuming events that are not destroyed on consumption (unlike message passing, Chapter 2) but remain available for other consumers
    • That is what enables the book's patterns.
  • Microservices are small, purpose-built for business requirements, fit "conceptually within one's head" and are owned by a small team ("two pizzas" as popularised by Amazon).

  • Simplest form (Figure 1-1): consumes from two input streams, processes and reacts, produces to an output stream, with its own state store.
  • The service is entirely responsible for:
    • consuming events from its chosen input streams;
    • producing data to its chosen output streams;
    • storing and managing its own state in its own state store;
    • processing, storing and reacting to events by its own business logic;
    • maintaining scalability in processing power and state storage;
    • integrating with monitoring.
  • Consumer
    • microservices read from streams, producer microservices write to them; it is common to be both
    • Services may be stateless or stateful (Chapter 8), though most non-trivial ones need state
    • They may use request-response APIs and third-party endpoints (Chapter 17)
    • They can be built with lightweight frameworks (Chapter 13), heavyweight frameworks (Chapter 12), functions as a service (Chapter 15), SQL (Chapter 14), or plain producer and consumer clients (Chapter 11).

Benefits

Benefit Meaning
Technological flexibility each service uses the most appropriate language and technology; easy prototyping
Business requirement flexibility granular services are easy to reassign; fewer cross-team dependencies; faster reaction to change
Loose coupling coupled on domain data, not on an implementation API; schemas manage change (Chapter 4)
Continuous integration and delivery small modular services are easy to ship and roll back
High testability fewer dependencies are easier to mock
Scalability services scale up and down individually
Granularity services map to bounded contexts and are easy to rewrite

1.2 Domain-driven design and bounded contexts

  • From Eric Evans' Domain-Driven Design (see also Implementing Domain-Driven Design and "What Is the Domain Model in Domain Driven Design?"). Concepts:
    • Domain: the problem space a business occupies: rules, processes, ideas, terminology.
    • Subdomain: a component of the domain (warehouse, sales, engineering); a large one may be a domain of its own.
    • Domain model: the construct used to represent reality; its terminology is internal (another domain may use the same words to mean something else).
    • Bounded context:
      • the logical boundaries (inputs, outputs, events, requirements, processes, data models) relevant to the subdomain
      • loose coupling between contexts stops one context's changes affecting another.
  • Figure 1-2:
    • the sales domain contains the orders and shipping subdomains, each with bounded contexts (taking orders, managing inventory, queuing orders to fill, fulfilment). Some contexts have several microservices, some just one
    • the only requirement is that the services fulfil the context's purpose.
  • Aligning contexts with business requirements lets teams solve their own problems with autonomy and fewer inter-team dependencies.

  • Technical alignment
    • (point-to-point request-response services, or teams owning technical layers) spreads a business function across teams and services, coupling them across team and API boundaries
    • a bug or failure ripples to all users of the technical system (Figure 1-3)
      • Technically aligned services are still fine for non-functional needs (authentication, authorisation, logging, monitoring)
    • do not write your own for each service.
  • Trade-off of business alignment:
    • code replication
    • Developers trying to cut it may expose data directly, causing tighter coupling and complex APIs that may cost far more than repeating logic and storing similar data.

1.3 Boundaries and communication structures

  • Event-driven microservices decouple what a service does from how it accesses data, where monoliths and point-to-point request-response services bundle data and service together
    • Teams, systems and people communicate through three structures:
    • Business communication structure (Figure 1-4): teams and departments and the goals assigned to them.
    • Implementation communication structure
      • (Figure 1-5): the data and logic that solve a business problem, formalised in code, data and APIs
      • the classic example is the monolithic database application.
    • Data communication structure
      • (Figure 1-6): how the business accesses data across the organisation
      • It is usually neglected, found as a haphazard interface bolted on to the service, doing double duty as the implementation
      • Services are left to find their own data or wrap it into the same implementation.
  • Conway's law:
    • "Organizations which design systems..
    • are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations" (Melvin Conway, How Do Committees Invent?, April 1968)
    • Teams build to their boundaries, yet domain concepts span the business, so services need data from other contexts
    • Implementation structures are poor at data communication, which has two effects:
    • it discourages new, logically separate products (hard to get common data);
    • it makes it easy to colocate new functionality with existing data (no new pipelines or monitoring): the monolithic pattern
      • A carrot (keep everything in one store) and a stick (confront data access before you can build).
  • Caution: if data access is too hard, or products scope-creep because the data sits in one implementation, you lack a data communication structure, and it worsens as the organisation grows.
  • Workarounds and their drawbacks:
    • shared databases (coupling on internal models)
    • read-only replicas (still tight on private models)
    • periodic batch dumps (complexity, error prone, duplicate or missing data, multiple sources of the same data). Event streams solve these but need investment.

A traditional team scenario

  • A team has one service and one data store and gets a new requirement: Option 1, a new service (data locked in the old service, no process for launching independent services; but modular systems ease later team splits) or Option 2, add it to the existing service (data, logging, monitoring, testing, deployment and rollback already exist; risks in coupling, sustainable growth and scalability)
    • Many choose option 2, which is fine (monoliths are powerful), for two reasons: accessing another system's data reliably at scale and in real time is difficult, and new services carry overhead and risk without an established way to launch them.
  • Copying data into your service has problems:
    • point-to-point couplings multiply
    • you must not expose the source's internal model
    • replication queries can overload the source
    • failed syncs go unnoticed
    • copies of copies are mistaken for the source of truth
    • copies are always somewhat stale (a reporting service and billing service disagree).
  • A year later the team splits and asks:
    • which team owns which data, where does it live, what if several teams modify values? They decide to share the service, needing cross-team synchronisation, which slows them
    • The cause is a weak or non-existent data communication structure, not a flaw in the concept of data sharing.

1.4 Event-driven data communication

  • Benefits:
    • Events form a durable canonical record:
      • they are the data, not just signals that data is ready or a transfer from one implementation to another
      • they store data and communicate asynchronously. Nearly anything can be an event, from simple occurrences to complex stateful records (Chapter 4).
    • Limitation of producer responsibilities: producers write what they intend to share to an event stream, owning format, schema and contents, but not the querying needs of others.
    • Separation of consumer responsibilities:
      • consumers read at their own pace, react, update their models, and may emit events
      • they mix streams and build query layers themselves without relying on a producer's query API.
    • Replayable record: instead of working out how to extract data from a system, consume it from the stream; streams are the primary means of communication and a reusable source.
    • Independence from a specific implementation: teams restructure and projects come and go, but core data stays available; you can swap a stream's producer without affecting consumers.
    • Align services with business needs: mix and match events to build your own data models and choose your own technology.
  • Tip: share only the data other teams need, just as a monolith restricts access to its internal model.
  • The team revisited:
    • a new service ingests the data from event streams, processes historical events, keeps only the fields it needs, and owns its storage and life cycle
    • Single-team ownership lets teams scale and reorganise: the team splits and reassigns microservices; event-stream ownership moves with the producing service, giving clear lineage
    • Scaling concerns move to individual event-processing services and to the data communication structure itself (the stream platform)
    • It needs organisation-wide adoption and the ability to start and manage a fleet of microservices.

1.5 Request-response microservices

  • Called "request-response" rather than "synchronous" because a requester can block or can wrap the request in a Future and check later.
  • Drawbacks:
    • point-to-point couplings (fan-out, hard to trace responsibility, entrenched communication structure)
    • dependent scaling (your scale depends on every dependent service)
    • service failure handling (retry, fail, recover consistently, harder as services grow)
    • API versioning and dependency management (several API versions coexist)
    • data access tied to the implementation
    • distributed monoliths (copying monolith boundaries as remote calls)
    • testing (integration tests need operational dependents).
  • Many companies (Netflix, Lyft, Uber, Facebook) succeed with them, but success does not prove an architecture's quality: companies have also earned fortunes with tangled monoliths. Neither style is strictly better
    • the author finds event-driven offers "unparalleled flexibility and power".
  • Benefits:
    • some data patterns suit request-response (authenticating a user, an AB test report)
    • third-party integrations are almost always request-response over HTTP
    • tracing is often easier
    • web and mobile experiences rely on them
    • more developers have the experience, making hiring easier.
  • Tip: a company's architecture is rarely, if ever, entirely event-driven; hybrids are the norm. Chapter 17 covers integration patterns.

Summary points

  • Rethink how data is created and communicated across the company: a durable set of domain events gives each service its own data without coupling to another's implementation.
  • Chapters 2 and 3 cover event streams and microservices as building blocks.
Terms to carry forward
  • Event stream; consumer and producer microservice; state store.
  • Domain, subdomain, bounded context.
  • Business, implementation and data communication structures; Conway's law.
  • Request-response microservice; distributed monolith; hybrid architecture.
