Building Event-Driven Microservices 10: Building Workflows with Microservices

Source: Adam Bellemare, Building Event-Driven Microservices, 2nd edition (O'Reilly, 2025), chapter 10. Previous: Building Event-Driven Microservices 9: Deterministic Stream Processing. Next: Building Event-Driven Microservices 11: Basic Producer and Consumer Microservices.

Takeaways
  • A workflow is a set of actions forming a business process, usually spanning several microservices. Concerns: creating and modifying it, monitoring it, and distributed transactions.
  • Choreography (services react independently to events) is loosely coupled and easy to extend but hard to reorder and monitor.
  • Orchestration (a central service commands workers) gives explicit control, easier change and monitoring, at the cost of tighter coupling and an extra service.
  • Sagas (distributed transactions) are hard: avoid them unless necessary; prefer compensation (move forward to a good state).
  • Durable execution engines (Temporal and others) make orchestrators fault-tolerant, with trade-offs.

10.1 Considerations

  • A workflow is a set of actions that compose a business process, including logical branching and compensatory actions
    • Each microservice has its own bounded context and emits events to downstream consumers.
  • Questions to ask:
    • Creating and modifying workflows:
      • how are the services related
      • how to modify an existing workflow without breaking work in progress, without changing several services, without breaking monitoring and visibility?
    • Monitoring workflows: how to tell when it completed for an event, if an event failed or is stuck, and the overall health?
    • Distributed transactions: many workflows need actions to happen together or not at all; how to implement and roll back?

10.2 The choreography pattern

  • Choreographed (reactive) architectures:
    • highly decoupled microservices independently reacting to input events: no blocking or waiting, no dependence on upstream producers or downstream consumers
    • Like dancers who each know their role and perform without being told during the dance.
  • Usually implemented with events:
    • services publish business outcomes to their streams and independent consumers subscribe
    • The producer does not know its consumers or what they do with the data
    • Many workflows are logically independent and need no strict coordination, an ideal fit.
  • Figure 10-1:
    • service A produces events driving B, which produces events driving C, within a single bounded context
    • streams 1 and 2 are locked behind the context boundary while C's output stream is available to other consumers. Choreography need not have a defined bounded context: many arise organically as streams and services are added.
  • Tip:
    • very loose coupling, commonly connecting services owned by different teams
    • But finding where a workflow starts and ends is hard across team boundaries, ownership is distributed, and debugging is difficult
    • Tip: despite shortcomings, it is the most common coupling in event-driven architectures, usually easy to extend with no changes to other services.

Modifying a choreographed workflow

  • **Adding steps at the end is easy
    • inserting in the middle or reordering is problematic.** Figure 10-2: reordering so C acts before B means editing both services C and B to consume streams 1 and 2 respectively
    • The data in the streams may no longer suit, requiring breaking schema changes that affect other consumers, possibly a new schema for stream 2 and migration, deletion or leaving of old data
    • All services must finish in-flight processing before the topology is swapped, or results can be partial, wrong or failed.
  • Relationships between services are hard to see outside the workflow, more so as service counts grow
    • choreographed workflows are brittle, and small logic changes may mean rewriting many services.
  • Tip: restrict access to intermediate streams you do not want others to couple on.

Monitoring a choreographed workflow

  • Steps:
    • a strict definition of the workflow (smaller, one-team workflows are easier to track)
    • your metrics of success (an order is paid and fulfilled; a car completes assembly), measurable from systems or streams
    • a centralised state store: a monitor consumes each stream and materialises progress to find events stuck or workflows incomplete (fixing is for service owners). Figure 10-3: solid lines show the monitor reading the streams
      • dashed lines are health checks such as heartbeats
  • Large workflows (a multinational retailer's order fulfilment with dozens of services and teams) are hard to see fully
    • monitor a subset:
      • 5 or 6 of 100 critical steps, and use other monitoring and logs for depth
      • Monitoring is brittle as independent services change unpredictably (persistent break-fix)
    • decide whether a monitor is necessary at all. Easier for a few services owned by one team.

10.3 The orchestration pattern

  • Orchestration
    • centralises logic in one microservice that issues commands and awaits responses from subordinate workers, like a conductor
    • The workflow is explicitly in code in the orchestrator, not implicit
    • It tracks which parts are complete, in process or not started, and the commands sent and responses received
    • It may use streams or direct request-response (HTTP, RPC).
  • Division of labour:
    • it tells a worker what to do (book a flight, pay for an order, post an item for auction), not how or how to retry or handle failure. A payment service owns the whole success or failure (trying, say, three times) and reports only complete success or complete failure
    • the orchestrator has no say in the number of attempts (that is the payment service's bounded context).
  • Tip: the orchestrator's bounded context is strictly workflow logic with minimal business fulfilment logic; the workers hold the bulk of business logic.

A simple event-driven orchestration example

  • Figure 10-4 is the orchestrated version of Figure 10-1
    • The orchestrator materialises the events it issues to services A, B and C and updates a state store from the worker results
    • Table 10-1: input event 100 done (all three results), 101 with A and B results and C dispatched (processing), 102 dispatched to A only (processing)
    • It selects the next step from the workflow logic, and when complete or ultimately failed composes the output and publishes it.
  • The loop consumes from each input stream and, by source, processes, updates progress and sends the next command:
while (true) {
    Event[] events = consumer.consume(streams);
    for (Event event : events) {
        if (event.source == "Input Stream") {
            processInput(event);
            updateProgress(event);
            producer.send("A-Command", ...);
        } else if (event.source == "A-Response") {
            processAResponse(event);
            updateProgress(event);
            producer.send("B-Command", ...);
        } else if (event.source == "B-Response") {
            processBResponse(event);
            updateProgress(event);
            producer.send("C-Command", ...);
        } else if (event.source == "C-Response") {
            processCResponse(event);
            updateProgress(event);
            producer.send("Output", ...);
        }
    }
    consumer.commitOffsets();
}
  • (The book's listing contains a stray character after the last updateProgress(event);, removed here.) Orchestrated workflows are typically easier to change: all logic in one place, if operations are independent (if A's result feeds B you cannot reorder)
    • Edit, test, deploy one service, but do not corrupt in-progress events by changing their workflow mid-completion.
  • Tip: use queues in the orchestration framework: workers process each queue in parallel, relying on queue semantics for retries and scaling.

A simple request-response orchestration example

  • The orchestrator calls workers directly (HTTP or RPC), awaiting responses (Figure 10-5), synchronously or asynchronously with futures and promises
    • Common with third-party systems without event APIs (SaaS endpoints) and legacy services: use request-response for those and event-driven semantics elsewhere.

Modifying and monitoring an orchestration

  • The orchestrator materialises incoming and outgoing streams and request-response results
    • the workflow is defined only in it, a single point of change, often without disrupting partially processed events. But orchestration means tighter coupling: the orchestrator and workers are explicitly related.
  • Antipattern:
    • a "God" service issuing granular commands to many weak "minion" services spreads business logic between orchestrator and workers (poor encapsulation, ill-defined bounded contexts, hard to scale ownership beyond one team)
    • Delegate full responsibility to workers.
  • Monitoring is simple: the orchestrator's data store holds the complete state of work in progress; expose it by API or publish state changes per entity to a monitoring stream.

Comparing request-response and event-driven orchestration

Event-driven Request-response
same I/O monitoring and lag scaling as other event-driven services very low latency (no stream to produce to and consume from)
other services can consume the orchestrator's streams integrates third-party APIs with no event integration
generally more durable: the broker isolates orchestrator and workers from each other's intermittent failures needs dedicated functionality for scaling, URI abstraction, routing and failover
built-in retry (streams and queues do not progress until processed and a response is created)
  • Mixing is common: mostly event-driven, with request-response to third-party APIs or legacy systems.

10.4 Distributed transactions and the saga pattern

  • A distributed transaction (saga) spans two or more fully independent data stores
    • each microservice processes its part and reverses it if aborted. Warning: sagas are hard
    • avoid them unless absolutely necessary: synchronisation between systems, rollbacks, transient instance failures and network failures.
  • Fulfilment and reversal logic live in the same microservice (maintainability, and no new transaction can start if it cannot be rolled back)
    • The implementation must be idempotent or support state rollbacks so intermittent failures do not leave inconsistency
    • Implement via choreography or orchestration.

Sagas via choreography

  • Choreography is normally unidirectional
    • a saga makes it tightly coupled upstream and downstream: each service must roll back, tell upstream systems and give them enough data to roll back, and a completed saga must signal completion (commonly a feedback loop to the initiator).
  • Figure 10-6:
    • each service reads a saga stream (data for the next service) and writes an instruction stream (commit or roll back). Success (Figure 10-7): A instructs B, B instructs C
    • C commits locally and propagates the commit to B, then A
    • A writes the output. Failure (Figure 10-8): a local commit failure in C makes B and A abort their share
    • A emits the failed result and continues.
  • Monitoring a choreographed saga is as hard as any (build a dedicated system, as in Figure 10-3)
    • modification is as hard (halt new processing and finish all ongoing before swapping order; adding a service D after C is easier). Tip: lift and shift: swap the whole bounded context for a new implementation using the same input consumer group offsets.
  • They tend to arise organically in pairs of services when a business change introduces transactional needs; as service count and need for oversight grow, consider orchestration.

Sagas via orchestration

  • The orchestrator adds the logic to revert from any point by running each worker's complementary reversing action
    • Figure 10-9: a two-stage transaction using both event streams and request-response
    • Central state gives close monitoring; changing the order means editing the orchestrator's logic
    • It can add timeouts (periodically check the materialised state for timed-out services) and human interactions (a REST API, Chapter 17, for cancellation)
    • A transaction can abort on a worker's return value, a timeout or a human interrupt.
  • Figure 10-10, a rollback after a failed final service: SaaS endpoint B fails to commit and reports back
    • the orchestrator updates its store
    • , issues a rollback to A
    • A rolls back
    • and responds
    • the orchestrator marks the transaction failed
    • and emits results
    • Then it may retry, discard the related input events, or throw an exception and terminate
  • Worst case:
    • the transaction can neither complete nor roll back (for example A cannot undo its commits): generally halt all processing and treat it as an incident
    • Transactions should cleanly complete or fail, but bugs and long-lasting data store failures happen.
  • Caution:
    • each microservice remains responsible for its own state being consistent after a rollback
    • the orchestrator only issues rollback commands and awaits confirmations. Orchestrated transactions offer better visibility, flexibility and monitoring than choreographed ones, at the overhead and management cost of an orchestrator.

10.5 The compensation workflow pattern

  • Take a forward compensating action to fix the current undesirable state instead of undoing every step
    • It may be simpler, cleaner and leave customers happier
    • Ticketing and inventory systems often use it when they oversell.
  • Example:
    • a website sells only stocked items; the customer orders, pays and gets a confirmation email
    • In the warehouse the shelf is empty although the system says three remain (misplaced, stolen, a wrong quantity received, or a race between two orders)
    • A strict saga would refund, cancel, fix stock and alert, but what about the rest of the order (cancel it, or partially refund and ship some)?
  • Tip: you can build workflows with human intervention; a kind message to the customer helps; still provide a compensatory override to correct the bad state.
  • Key:
    • decide the desirable outcome from the current bad state and build a path to it: order new stock, notify of a delay, offer a discount code as apology, and let the customer cancel or wait
    • Not always suitable, and sometimes reversing is better, but digital and physical records inevitably diverge, sometimes discovered weeks or months later.

10.6 Orchestration via a durable execution engine

  • A durable execution engine (DEE) is a platform for running long-running, stateful processes with guaranteed reliability
    • also "workflows-as-code" or fault-tolerant workflow engines
    • Your code runs to completion despite failures, restarts and time-outs
    • More common since 2020
    • Examples: Cadence, Temporal, Restate, Resonate (free open source options, with possible licensing limits for larger workloads).
  • Features:
    • Automatic retries and fault tolerance (resume from the last good state).
    • Managing distributed transactions (eventual consistency, optional compensation).
    • Less boilerplate error handling.
    • Resource savings: no re-executing completed code or calls.
    • Long durations: minutes to days, waiting for human approval or external events.
  • Example from Temporal's sample Java code (trimmed in the book): Async.function calls composeGreeting twice and returns Promise<String> values
    • hello.get() + "\n" + bye.get() waits for both. The calls, promises and return values are recorded in the engine's append-only durable log
    • if it fails after "Hello" but before "Bye", the restored service skips "Hello" and goes on to "Bye".
// Temporal sample, trimmed for brevity (as in the book)
import io.temporal.workflow.Async;
import io.temporal.workflow.Promise;
// ...

/** Simple activity implementation, that concatenates two strings. */
static class GreetingActivitiesImpl implements GreetingActivities {
    @Override
    public String composeGreeting(String greeting, String name) {
        return greeting + " " + name + "!";
    }
}
// ...

@Override
public String getGreeting(String name) {
    /*
     * This is our workflow method.
     * We invoke the composeGreeting method two times using
     * io.temporal.workflow.Async#function(Func).
     * The results of each async activity method invocation returns us a
     * io.temporal.workflow.Promise which is similar to a Java Future
     */
    Promise<String> hello =
        Async.function(activities::composeGreeting, "Hello", name);
    Promise<String> bye =
        Async.function(activities::composeGreeting, "Bye", name);

    // Application waits until receiving both of the async results
    return hello.get() + "\n" + bye.get();
}
  • Embedding DEE code in a service is appealing:
    • it is plain code from an SDK, usable where it suits, with familiar tools
    • Tip: a DEE is most useful for business logic triggered by a non-repeatable source such as an HTTP or RPC request (you can reprocess a stream's events but not requests you already failed and returned).
  • It needs a dedicated durable store for execution progress
    • an immutable, durable, append-only log (event sourcing) is the common choice, deeply coupled and proprietary. It records:
    • workflow progress (last line executed, success or failure, branches, conditions and loops);
    • all relevant state you chose to append;
    • parameters of function calls and direct call APIs;
    • returns of those calls;
    • durable promises (what has been invoked, so it is not repeated on restore).
  • Warning: a DEE prevents duplicate work and calls but cannot remove race conditions (a request may be sent and the service crash before recording it): keep operations and requests idempotent.
  • Trade-offs:
    • New dependency and lock-in (will the vendor exist in 5 or 10 years; is there an open-source option you can run).
    • Learning curve (distributed systems concepts).
    • Engine-specific abstractions (workflows, activities, workers, timers, task queues, signals, await, promises).
    • Versioning challenges (may run versions in parallel, or modify code to handle old and new data on reprocessing).
    • State persistence overhead (computation and storage; possible slowdown for high-throughput, low-latency work).
    • Extra resources (buy, build, host or manage; scale with usage).
  • Often overkill, since a service can simply retry by reconsuming input streams
    • best for complex, long-running stateful workflows without reliable, retriable triggers. Note: other durable execution may suit orchestrators, such as durable function orchestrators (Chapter 15).

Summary points

  • Choreography: loose coupling between business units and independent workflows; best for simpler workflows with few dependencies, few services, and rarely changing order.
  • Orchestration:
    • explicit control at the cost of an extra service
    • better visibility and monitoring
    • handles complicated distributed transactions
    • logic is easier to modify
    • suits changing workflows with many independent services.
  • Not all workflows need distributed transactions; compensation often builds forward to a healthy state.
  • Durable execution engines build highly reliable orchestrators but bring a learning curve and operational overhead: evaluate fit.
Terms to carry forward
  • Choreography, orchestration, orchestrator, God service antipattern.
  • Saga, compensation workflow, lift and shift.
  • Durable execution engine (DEE), durable promise, append-only log.
