Building Microservices 12: Resiliency

Source: Sam Newman, Building Microservices, 2nd ed. (O'Reilly, 2021), ch. 12. Previous: Building Microservices 11: Security. Next: Building Microservices 13: Scaling.

Takeaways
  • Resilience has four parts (David Woods): robustness, rebound, graceful extensibility, sustained adaptability. Microservices mostly help robustness; the rest depends on people and culture.
  • Assume failure is everywhere; set requirements (latency, availability, durability) with the business and degrade functionality gracefully.
  • Stability patterns: timeouts, retries, bulkheads, circuit breakers, isolation, redundancy, middleware, idempotency, spreading risk.
  • CAP theorem: in a partition choose consistency (CP) or availability (AP); CA does not exist over a network. Per capability, AP is often right.
  • Chaos engineering, game days and blameless post-mortems build the learning culture resilience needs.

12.1 What is resiliency?

Drawing on resilience engineering (firefighting, air traffic control, operating theatres), David D. Woods ("Four Concepts for Resilience...", Reliability Engineering & System Safety, 2015) names four concepts:

Concept Meaning Example
Robustness Absorb expected perturbation Spin up a replacement host, retry calls, backup on-call person
Rebound Recover after a traumatic event Tested backups; a playbook (roles, point person, how fast and how to tell users)
Graceful extensibility Cope with surprise Flatter organisations with distributed responsibility
Sustained adaptability Keep adapting to changing environments and demands Not complacent; chaos engineering, enabling teams
  • Robustness
    • needs prior knowledge (foresight or hindsight) and adds complexity (moving to Kubernetes adds new pain points), so weigh the result, not only cost and benefit
    • It covers people too.
  • Rebound:
    • many teams try to eliminate outages and are unprepared when one happens
    • at scale elimination is unsustainable. Agree a plan beforehand, since thinking clearly mid-outage is hard.
  • Graceful extensibility:
    • brittle systems fail at the edge of what you expected
    • Optimisation can increase brittleness: automation cannot handle surprise, and cutting staff because of it removes the skilled people who can.
  • Sustained adaptability:
    • Woods: "No matter how good we have done before..
    • the future could be different." It needs a holistic view (a risk of small autonomous teams losing sight of the whole; chapter 15's stream-aligned and enabling teams), a culture where people share information without fear of retribution, and continued investment.
  • Microservices: they help with robustness, but resilience is "a property not of the software itself but of the people building and running the system".

12.2 Failure is everywhere

  • Hard disks fail and networks are unreliable
    • at scale failure is a statistical certainty. Embrace it: if you handle a service failing gracefully, in-place upgrades become easier (a planned outage beats an unplanned one).
  • Spend less effort stopping the inevitable and more on recovering
    • Google's servers: a local power supply per server, and Velcro-mounted drives (chapter 10)
    • If a failing server is tolerated, expensive hardware has diminishing returns: many cheap machines can beat a few costly ones.

How much is too much?

  • Requirements drive the techniques:
    • autoscaling is overkill for a report run twice a month; zero-downtime deployment is overkill for an intranet knowledge base but sensible for e-commerce
    • Define general cross-functional requirements and override per service
    • Ask users, who may not articulate them.
Requirement Guidance
Response time/latency Measure at different loads; set a percentile target with concurrency, e.g. "90th-percentile 2 s at 200 concurrent connections"
Availability Is it 24/7? Downtime percentages matter more for history than for a caller who needs reliability
Durability of data How much loss is acceptable and how long to keep data (session logs a year, financial records many years)
  • Express them as SLOs (chapter 10).

Degrading functionality

  • Spreading a page over several services (item details, price and stock, cart): if one failure takes down the whole page, you are less resilient than with one dependency
    • Decide per outage what to degrade, with the business:
      • stock unavailable, still sell and sort it out later
      • cart down, show the listing and hide the cart or show "Be Back Soon!" or a phone number
    • It is a business decision
    • For every UI or service ask "what happens if this is down?"

12.3 Stability patterns

[!note] The AdvertCorp story (details changed) A classified-ads company used a strangler fig to move ad types onto a new system while the rest were served by older applications (6,000 to 7,000 requests per second at peak, mostly cached except uncached search). Before the lunchtime peak, application nodes hit 100% CPU and the site went down. A minor downstream service (the "turnip ads" system), used by under 5% of customers and generating less revenue, had become very slow.

  • A slow failure is worse than a clean one: waiting slows everything and causes cascading failure. "In a distributed system, latency kills."
  • Causes: the HTTP connection pool had a timeout on calls but its timeout for waiting for a worker was disabled by default, so threads piled up (normally 40 connections, about 800 within five minutes); one shared pool let one slow service exhaust all workers; and traffic kept flowing to an unhealthy service, giving it no chance to recover.
  • Fixes: correct timeouts, bulkheads, circuit breakers.

Timeouts

  • Too long slows the system
    • too short fails calls that would succeed
    • none can hang everything
      • AdvertCorp had a missing worker-wait timeout and a 30 second call timeout, though users refresh long before (spawning more requests)
      • Normal response was well under a second and the page target was 4 to 6 seconds, so they set 1 s for the call and 1 s to wait for a worker (about 2 s worst case).
  • Put timeouts on all out-of-process calls, with a default; log them and tune from normal healthy times.
  • Also set a timeout for the overall operation and pass the remaining budget downstream: if a page has 1,000 ms and 300 ms are gone when calling turnip, wait at most 700 ms.

Retries

  • Some failures are transient; refreshing a page is a retry. Use response codes: 404 will not help; 503 or 504 might. Add a delay so you do not bombard a struggling service.
  • Account for retries in the budget:
    • a 500 ms timeout with three retries a second apart can wait 3.5 seconds
    • skip later retries when the overall budget is gone (a non-user-facing job can wait longer).

Bulkheads

  • Michael Nygard's Release It! (2nd ed., 2018): like a ship's sealed compartment
    • AdvertCorp should have had a connection pool per downstream service
    • Splitting functionality into separate microservices is also a bulkhead.
  • Bulkheads are arguably most important:
    • timeouts and breakers free resources once constrained, while bulkheads stop them becoming constrained
    • They also enable load shedding (rejecting requests to avoid saturation).

Circuit breakers

  • Like the home fuse:
    • after a number of failures (timeouts or a subset of 5XX), the breaker blows (opens) and requests fail fast
    • after a period a few requests are tried and, if healthy enough, the breaker resets
      • It protects the caller and gives the downstream service room to recover
      • Newman would mandate them for all synchronous downstream calls
    • implementations are widely available. Pick sensible defaults and tune.
  • When open: queue for later (asynchronous jobs), or fail fast and degrade (AdvertCorp updated the site to say turnip ads were unavailable, with one breaker per legacy system, matching the worker pools).
  • Use them manually for maintenance: open upstream breakers so callers fail fast while a service is offline, then close them. Check breaker state before starting an operation.

Isolation

  • The more service A depends on B, the more B's health affects A
    • buffering (middleware) lets downstream be offline. Isolation also reduces coordination between teams, so more autonomy.
  • Physical coupling hides in logical isolation:
    • two unrelated services on the same host (one hogs the CPU), or two logically separate databases on the same database infrastructure. Use independent hosts, VMs or containers
    • but isolation costs infrastructure, tooling and complexity (a broker to run).

Redundancy

  • More of something improves robustness (more than one person who knows the production database; more than one instance)
    • Decide how much from failure modes, impact and cost
    • On AWS there is no SLA for a single EC2 instance or a single availability zone, so use several instances across zones.

Middleware

  • Brokers give guaranteed delivery by implementing retries and timeouts well
    • For AdvertCorp request-response it would not have helped (the customer still gets no answer; stale requests pile up in the broker; the other route, having turnip broadcast ads, still fails if turnip is down)
    • Useful, but not always.

Idempotency

  • An idempotent operation has the same outcome however many times it is applied, so you can replay messages safely
    • Crediting points ("100 to account 1234") is not idempotent; adding the reason (forPurchase 4567) lets the points bank ignore duplicates
    • It also guards against two workers seeing one event.
<!-- not idempotent: repeated calls add 100 points each time -->
<credit>
  <amount>100</amount>
  <forAccount>1234</forAccount>
</credit>

<!-- idempotent: one credit per purchase -->
<credit>
  <amount>100</amount>
  <forAccount>1234</forAccount>
  <reason>
    <forPurchase>4567</forPurchase>
  </reason>
</credit>
  • It is the business operation that is idempotent, not all system state (you still log the call and record its response time)
    • HTTP GET and PUT are idempotent only if your service implements them so.

Spreading your risk

  • Do not keep all eggs in one basket:
    • services on different hosts that are virtual hosts on one physical box, VMs whose root partition sits on one SAN (Newman has had big, expensive SANs fail at least twice in ten years), one rack or one data centre
    • Use several.
  • AWS:
    • regions are distinct clouds, each with two or more availability zones
    • the compute SLA is 99.95% monthly uptime for the region as a whole, so spread across zones (some also across regions).
  • Providers limit liability under SLAs
    • know the impact of supplier failure, and have a plan B (a disaster-recovery platform with another supplier). If you need no more than four hours' downtime per quarter but your host guarantees eight, change the SLA or the solution.

12.4 CAP theorem

  • In a distributed system you trade consistency, availability and partition tolerance; in a failure mode you keep two (Eric Brewer's conjecture, since proved)
    • Consistency: the same answer from any node
    • Availability: every request gets a response
    • Partition tolerance: coping when parts cannot communicate.
  • Example: the Inventory service in two data centres, each with a database, replicating to the other. If the link breaks, what now?
    • Sacrificing consistency (AP):
      • both nodes serve requests but DC2 may be stale
      • writes must be reconciled later (harder the longer the partition). Replication is never instant, so eventual consistency is normal.
    • Sacrificing availability (CP):
      • nodes cannot coordinate, so they refuse requests
      • Multinode consistency is "really hard" (transactional reads need locks across nodes; a lock holder that vanishes...; the same reason as distributed transactions in chapter 6)
      • Do not invent it: use an off-the-shelf data store or lock service (for example Consul's strongly consistent key-value store)
      • "Friends don't let friends write their own distributed consistent data store."
    • Sacrificing partition tolerance (CA): impossible over a network (it would be a single local process).
  • AP or CP? It depends on the business impact: is a five-minute-stale inventory record fine? A bank balance? AP is simpler to build and scale.
  • Not all or nothing:
    • MusicCorp's catalogue could be AP, the inventory CP (do not sell what you lack)
    • Even a single service: Points Balance could show stale balances (AP) but make updates consistent (CP), pushing the trade-off down to individual capabilities
    • Also a spectrum: Cassandra lets each call choose to wait for one replica, a quorum or all
    • Claims of "beating" CAP just mix CP and AP.
  • The real world: with 100 copies of "Give Blood" by the Brakes and a sale leaving 99, a dropped and broken copy makes the truth 98: no consistent system knows everything about the physical world, so AP is often the right call, with an apology later.

12.5 Antifragility

  • Nassim Taleb's
    • antifragile (systems that benefit from disorder) was hyped, inspiring parts of Netflix
    • Newman now sees it as a subset of resilience (graceful extensibility and sustained adaptability), a product of thinking only about robustness and rebound.

12.6 Chaos engineering

  • Named after Netflix practices
    • confusing because many proponents sell tools. Best definition he found: "the discipline of experimenting on a system in order to build confidence in the system's capability to withstand turbulent conditions in production." The "system" includes people, processes and culture, not just software.
  • Game days:
    • planned but ideally surprise exercises for people and processes (Google's DiRT, Disaster Recovery Test, even simulated earthquakes; Kripa Krishnan, "Weathering the Unexpected", 2012)
    • In Russ Miles's Learning Chaos Engineering example, "Bob" was shut in a room to test over-reliance on one person, and had to step in when the team logged into production by mistake and began destroying data.
  • Production experiments (Netflix):
    • Chaos Monkey turns off random machines in production hours
    • part of the Simian Army: Chaos Gorilla (an entire availability zone), Latency Monkey (slow network).
  • Beyond robustness:
    • used narrowly it improves robustness; used to keep asking "what if" it supports wider resilience
    • Tools: Chaos Toolkit (open source), Reliably, Gremlin
    • "Running a chaos engineering tool doesn't make you resilient."

12.7 Blame

  • After an incident, "root cause analysis" tempts us to find a human
    • Telstra (Australia, 2016) outage: within hours the COO blamed a person for reconnecting customers to a malfunctioning node ("an embarrassing human error")
    • If one person can bring down a telco, that says more about the telco; blame creates a culture of fear, so people stop reporting problems and you cannot learn
    • There were more outages afterwards, and the COO later resigned.
  • Aim for safety to admit mistakes
    • see John Allspaw, "Blameless Post-Mortems and a Just Culture" (Etsy's Code as Craft, 2012). Resiliency requires a questioning mind and a learning culture.

Summary points

  • Resilience needs people, processes and organisation, not only software; microservices give many ways to improve robustness, none free.
  • How much resilience you want is defined by users and business owners; talk to them often.
  • "You don't know what you don't know": keep learning and questioning.
  • Next chapter: scaling, which also helps with redundancy.
Terms to carry forward
  • Robustness, rebound, graceful extensibility, sustained adaptability.
  • Timeout, retry, bulkhead, circuit breaker, load shedding, isolation, redundancy.
  • Idempotency, eventual consistency, CAP, AP, CP.
  • Chaos engineering, game day, Chaos Monkey, blameless post-mortem.
