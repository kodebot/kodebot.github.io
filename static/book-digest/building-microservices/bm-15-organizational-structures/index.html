Building Microservices 15: Organizational Structures

Source: Sam Newman, Building Microservices, 2nd ed. (O'Reilly, 2021), ch. 15. Previous: Building Microservices 14: User Interfaces. Next: Building Microservices 16: The Evolutionary Architect.

Takeaways
  • A microservice architecture without a matching organisation blunts its benefits. Conway's law: systems copy the communication structure of the organisation that builds them.
  • Aim for small, autonomous, stream-aligned teams with strong ownership of microservices (ideally full life-cycle ownership); collective ownership is fine inside a team, and at small scale.
  • Enabling teams, communities of practice and a platform (a "paved road", not a mandate) support autonomy.
  • Shared microservices are a smell; options include changing ownership, internal open source, pluggable frameworks or libraries.
  • Prefer peer review (pairing) to external approval; consider geography; people and their appetite for change matter most.

15.1 Loosely coupled organisations

  • Moving to microservices without changing structure means paying the cost without the return
    • Accelerate (Forsgren, Humble, Kim, 2018) lists what autonomous, loosely coupled teams can do:
    • make large design changes without outside permission;
    • make them without depending on other teams or creating significant work for them;
    • complete work without communicating and coordinating with outsiders;
    • deploy and release on demand, regardless of dependencies;
    • do most testing on demand without an integrated test environment;
    • deploy during business hours with negligible downtime.
  • It is a checklist for stream-aligned teams, and needs decentralised power and accountability, including architectural decisions (chapter 16).

15.2 Conway's law

  • Melvin Conway, "How Do Committees Invent?", Datamation, April 1968: "Any organization that designs a system..
    • will inevitably produce a design whose structure is a copy of the organization's communication structure." Eric S
    • Raymond's gloss in The New Hacker's Dictionary: four groups on a compiler yield a 4-pass compiler
    • The story goes that Harvard Business Review rejected the paper as unproven.
  • Evidence:
    • Loosely and tightly coupled organisations (MacCormack, Baldwin and Rusnak, "Exploring the Duality Between Product and Organizational Architectures", Research Policy, 2012): loosely coupled (distributed open source) organisations produced more modular systems than tightly coupled commercial firms.
    • Windows Vista (Nagappan, Murphy and Basili, ICSE 2008): organisational metrics, such as the number of engineers who worked on code, predicted error-proneness better than code complexity.
    • Amazon (two-pizza teams of about 8 to 10 owning the whole life cycle; why AWS arose) and Netflix (small independent teams from the start; reportedly seating by who talks to whom).

Team size

  • Developers generally say 5 to 10
    • The best study Newman found (Rodriguez et al., Journal of Systems and Software, 2012) found productivity worst for average team sizes of 9 or more
    • His untested hypothesis: distribution across time zones shrinks the optimum.
  • Adding people does not simply help
    • Accelerate: with a loosely coupled, well-encapsulated architecture and matching organisation you can grow engineering linearly or better
    • So build large organisations from small autonomous teams, moving away from centralised command and control.

Small teams, large organisation

  • Brooks's law
    • ("The Mythical Man-Month"): adding people to a late project makes it later. Parallelism needs separable work
    • coordination has a cost, and new people take time from existing ones
      • Coordination is the biggest cost at scale. John Rossman (Think Like Amazon): the two-pizza team is autonomous, with limited, well-documented interactions
    • "it's not about the team's size. It's about the team's autonomy".
  • Team Topologies defines a team API: how a team interacts with others (interfaces and working practices: pull requests, visible backlog and roadmap).

On autonomy

  • Small teams that remain dependent are just silos
    • **W
    • L
    • Gore** caps business units at 150 people, from Robin Dunbar's social group limit
    • Timpson (UK retailer) replaced its rules with two: "Look the part" and "Put money in the till"
    • Learn from Amazon's two-pizza teams or the "Spotify model" (guilds, chapters; not used by Spotify anymore, per the footnote) but do not copy without understanding why.

15.3 Strong versus collective ownership

Model Meaning
Strong One team owns a microservice and decides changes, coding standards, technology, deployment; outsiders ask or send pull requests the owner may reject
Collective Any team can change any microservice; careful coordination needed
  • Strong ownership
    • optimises autonomy and allows local variation (a functional Java style), though decisions affecting others (gRPC when everyone uses REST) need coordination
    • Full life-cycle ownership: one team designs, changes, deploys, runs and decommissions: no tickets to operations, no external sign-off
    • It can take years and needs skills, self-service tooling and cultural change (out-of-hours support)
    • Strong ownership for multi-team organisations is the most sensible start.
  • Collective ownership:
    • move people where needed, but frequent rotation demands consistency (few technology options), undermining "microservices buy you options" (James Lewis), and needs more coordination, hence organisational coupling (and per MacCormack et al., a tightly coupled design)
    • Fine for a small team.
  • Levels: inside a team use collective ownership (a poly-skilled team must); across the organisation, strong.
  • Balance:
    • more collective means more global consistency
    • more strong means more local optimisation (you can still fix some things, such as one cloud platform). Collective ownership pushes you towards consistency
    • the best organisations keep shifting towards local optimisation and re-evaluate. Someone needs visibility to balance.

15.4 Enabling teams, communities of practice and the platform

Enabling teams

  • They support stream-aligned teams on cross-cutting concerns (UI consistency, chapter 14). Example: each team picks a different programming language
    • each choice makes sense alone, but the organisation faces support, rotation and hiring problems
      • A small cross-team group connects people for these discussions
      • Architects fit here: surveying the landscape, spotting trends, connecting people, a sounding board
    • not a unit of control (sometimes called principal engineers). They also spot problems worth fixing centrally (every team struggling to create test databases).

Communities of practice (CoPs)

  • A cross-cutting group fostering peer learning (Emily Webber, Building Successful Communities of Practice, 2016)
    • Unlike enabling teams, members give a few hours a week and membership is fluid; enabling teams have ring-fenced time to act
    • They work together (a Kubernetes CoP can give the platform team feedback).

The platform

  • Stream-aligned teams test in isolation, deploy by day, and change architecture, so they need a self-service platform. Uswitch/RVU (Paul Ingles, "Convergence to Kubernetes"): "We didn't change our organisation because we wanted to use Kubernetes
    • we used Kubernetes because we wanted to change our organization." A platform (desired state management, log aggregation, service authentication) should free team bandwidth.
  • Platform team:
    • its users are developers
    • Newman prefers names like "delivery services": the job is making delivery easy, and building a platform is one way
    • Work as an internal consultancy with a product mindset.
  • The paved road:
    • state how you want things done and make it easy (for example mutual TLS delivered by a common framework) but do not enforce
    • teams can find another route. Optional use pushes the team to be useful
    • Ingles tracked OKRs (teams adopted, apps on the autoscaling service, proportion of traffic and revenue through the platform)
      • Arbitrary barriers get worked around, so explain why, then make it easy
      • Hard constraints (cloud-vendor agnostic, PII encrypted at rest) must still be communicated
      • Governing by "you must use the platform" backfires: teams bypass it without understanding what matters.

15.5 Shared microservices

Generally one team per microservice, yet sharing is common. Why:

Driver Response
Too hard to split Large monoliths (FinanceCo's monolith was shared by teams; see chapter 3); or merge teams to match architecture
Cross-cutting changes Some are unavoidable: FinanceCo moving from one account = one user to multiple users per account touched many services owned by others. Restructuring to remove one cross-cutting change can create costlier ones; accept exceptional pain
Delivery bottlenecks Catalog needs both a genre field (website team) and ringtones (mobile team) while its team is busy. Options: wait; add people (easier with standard technology); split the service (a ringtone catalogue, if 10 weeks of work are queued); or the models below

Internal open source

  • Borrow open source:
    • core committers are custodians
    • others ask or send pull requests
      • Needs gatekeeping: vetting for quality and idiomatic consistency takes time (good gatekeepers communicate; bad ones fight religious wars), so check it is worth it
      • Maturity: hard to accept outside patches until the service's spine is stable
    • open up mature services (the cart). Tooling: distributed version control, pull requests, inline comments, easy build and deployment, standard technology.

Pluggable, modular microservices

  • Many inbound pull requests signal a shared service. FinanceCo: country teams (US, Singapore) kept changing one central service.

[!warning] Many inbound pull requests A team with many pull requests from others probably owns a microservice that is shared by multiple teams.

  • Change ownership (the Customer Engagement team takes the Recommendation service from Marketing and Promotions), or when sources are many:
    • Run variations:
      • the owner provides a framework (the service with only common functionality) and each country team runs its own instance with custom code. Common code cannot be updated everywhere at once (no lockstep), and internal frameworks can bloat
      • Newman used it rarely, after exhausting other options.
    • Contribute libraries:
      • each country team contributes a library built into one central service
      • Fewer services, but the central team schedules deployments and may have to debug others' bugs.

Change reviews

  • Accelerate: approval only for high-risk changes did not correlate with performance
    • teams with peer review or no approval process did better, external approval worse (an outside body cannot judge tens of thousands of changed lines). So use peer review and avoid external review.
  • Synchronous beats asynchronous: pair programming reviews inline (driver and navigator). Otherwise review promptly and discuss face to face; days of delay mean context switching.
  • Ensemble (mob) programming
    • works for tricky problems, but still review other changes (Accelerate found positive correlation with reviewing all changes). Newman has reservations about power imbalances and inclusivity (and prefers "ensemble" to "mob")
    • see Maaret Pyhäjärvi's Ensemble Programming Guidebook.

The orphaned service

  • Small, simple services (Shopping Basket) may not change for months
    • If teams align to bounded contexts, even an idle service has a de facto owner (the consumer web sales team)
    • A rewrite is cheap if disliked, but a polyglot estate may leave the team without the skills.

15.6 Case study: realestate.com.au (REA), 2014 snapshot

  • Split into independent lines of business (LOBs), each with squads (the biggest line had four) that stayed in a LOB for long periods to learn the domain, and owned the full life cycle of their services. A core delivery services team (an enabling team) offered advice and tooling
    • heavy automation and AWS.
  • Within a LOB, services could talk any way
    • between LOBs, communication was mandated asynchronous batch (one of the architecture team's few cast-iron rules), matching coarse business boundaries and letting a LOB take services down freely
    • It grew from a handful of services in 2010 to hundreds by 2014
    • Newman stresses this is a snapshot, not a recommendation.

15.7 Geographical distribution

  • Colocated teams find synchronous communication easy
    • time zones raise coordination costs sharply (Newman, in Australia, coordinated teams in India, the UK, Brazil and the US with monthly meetings). On one project a service shared between two sites became two specialised halves with a coarse-grained API between them, matching communication paths.
  • Treat geography as a strong consideration for team and software boundaries
    • same or similar time zones help
    • a new office may decide what to split out next. Distributed organisations tend to produce more modular systems.

15.8 Conway's law in reverse

  • Can design shape the organisation? Anecdotally yes. A print firm's input, core and output three-part web system was a technical decision that later had three IT divisions grow around it
    • changing the design needed changing the organisation (over years).

15.9 People

  • "No matter how it looks at first, it's always a people problem." (Gerry Weinberg). Developers must now think about networks and failure
    • people used to throwing work over the wall may resist accountability (even contractual barriers to carrying pagers). Change gradually, starting with willing people
    • understand appetite
    • perhaps a separate team handles support briefly
    • hire people with experience to show what is possible
    • articulate responsibilities and why. "Without people on board, any change you might want to make could be doomed."

Summary points

  • Conway's law points microservices towards strong ownership; sharing or collective ownership at scale undermines benefits.
  • Where organisation and architecture misalign, tension appears.
  • Reading: Team Topologies; James Lewis, "Scale, Microservices and Flow" (YOW!, 2020).
Terms to carry forward
  • Conway's law, Brooks's law, two-pizza team, Dunbar's number, team API.
  • Strong, collective and full life-cycle ownership.
  • Enabling team, community of practice, platform, paved road.
  • Internal open source, core committer, peer review, ensemble programming.
