Clean Architecture 2: A Tale of Two Values

Source: Robert C. Martin, Clean Architecture: A Craftsman's Guide to Software Structure and Design (Pearson, 2017), chapter 2. Own-words digest with short attributed quotes. Previous: Clean Architecture 1: What Is Design and Architecture?. Next: Clean Architecture 3: Paradigm Overview.

Takeaways
  • Software gives stakeholders two values: behaviour and structure. Developers must keep both high but often focus on the lesser one.
  • The second value is architecture: software is meant to be soft, easy to change, so the cost of a change should follow its scope, not its shape.
  • Of the two, architecture is the greater value: a perfect program that cannot change becomes useless; one that does not work but is easy to change can be fixed.
  • On the Eisenhower matrix behaviour is urgent but not always important; architecture is important but never urgent. The team must fight for architecture against feature urgency.

2.1 Behaviour

  • The first value of software is its behaviour
    • Programmers are hired to make machines behave in ways that make or save money for stakeholders: develop a functional specification or requirements document with them, then write code that makes the machines meet it
    • When the machine violates the requirements, programmers reach for debuggers and fix it.
  • Many programmers believe that is their entire job. Martin says they are "sadly mistaken".

2.2 Architecture

  • The second value comes from the word "software":
    • "ware" means product; "soft" is where the second value lies
    • Software was invented to be soft: an easy way to change what machines do
    • If we wanted machine behaviour to be hard to change we would call it hardware.
  • To fulfil its purpose software must be easy to change:
    • when stakeholders change their minds about a feature, the change should be simple
    • The difficulty of a change should be proportional only to the scope of the change, and not to its shape.
  • The gap between scope and shape drives the growth in cost, and is why costs grow out of proportion to the size of the requested changes (the first year is much cheaper than the second, the second than the third).
    • To stakeholders it is a stream of changes of roughly similar scope
      • To developers it is a stream of jigsaw puzzle pieces to fit into a puzzle of ever-increasing complexity: each request is harder to fit because the shape of the system does not match the shape of the request
      • Developers feel they are forced to jam square pegs into round holes.
    • The problem is the system's architecture:
      • the more it prefers one shape over another, the harder new features fit
      • So architectures should be as shape agnostic as practical
      • (Martin uses "shape" in an unconventional way; the metaphor is the point.)

2.3 The greater value

  • Which provides the greater value:
    • working, or being easy to change? Business managers often say working
    • developers often go along
    • Martin says it is the wrong attitude, proved by examining the extremes:
    • A program that works perfectly but is impossible to change will not work when requirements change and cannot be made to: it becomes useless.
    • A program that does not work but is easy to change can be made to work and kept working as requirements change: it remains continually useful.
  • Objection:
    • no program is truly impossible to change
    • But some are practically impossible because the cost of change exceeds its benefit, and many systems reach that point in some features or configurations.
  • If you ask managers whether they want to be able to make changes, they say of course, but may qualify it: current functionality matters more than later flexibility
    • Yet when managers ask for a change and your estimate is unaffordably high, they are likely to be furious that you let the system reach the point where the change was impractical.

2.4 Eisenhower's matrix

  • President Dwight D
    • Eisenhower's matrix of importance versus urgency (Figure 2.1)
    • Quote (from a speech at Northwestern University in 1954): "I have two kinds of problems, the urgent and the important
    • The urgent are not important, and the important are never urgent." Martin: urgent things are rarely of great importance, and important things seldom of great urgency.
  • Behaviour
    • is urgent but not always particularly important
    • architecture is important but never particularly urgent. Some things are both, others neither. The four couplets as priorities:
    1. Urgent and important.
    2. Not urgent and important.
    3. Urgent and not important.
    4. Not urgent and not important.
  • Architecture, the important stuff, holds the top two positions; behaviour holds the first and third.
  • The mistake business managers and developers make: elevating position 3 to position 1: failing to separate features that are urgent but not important from those that are truly urgent and important, so the important architecture is ignored for unimportant features.
  • The dilemma: managers are not equipped to evaluate the importance of architecture (that is what developers were hired for), so it is the team's responsibility to assert the importance of architecture over the urgency of features.

2.5 Fight for the architecture

  • Doing this means wading into a fight or "struggle", which is always how these things are done: the development, management, marketing, sales and operations teams each struggle for what they believe is best for the company. Effective teams tackle it head on and squabble with the other stakeholders as equals: "as a software developer, you are a stakeholder"
    • you have a stake in the software that you need to safeguard, part of your role, duty and why you were hired.
  • Doubly important for a software architect, who by job description focuses more on the system's structure than its features and functions, and creates an architecture that lets features be easily developed, modified and extended.
  • If architecture comes last, the system becomes ever more costly to develop and change becomes practically impossible for part or all of it
    • If that happens, "the software development team did not fight hard enough for what they knew was necessary".

Summary points

  • Two values: behaviour (the machine meets requirements) and structure (the software stays easy to change); developers owe both.
  • Architecture is the greater value: change cost should depend on scope, not shape.
  • Behaviour is urgent; architecture is important. Do not promote urgent-but-unimportant features over architecture; fight for it as a stakeholder.
Terms to carry forward
  • Behaviour versus structure (architecture); scope versus shape.
  • Eisenhower matrix (urgent versus important); shape agnostic.
