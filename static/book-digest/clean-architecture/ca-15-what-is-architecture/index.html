Clean Architecture 15: What Is Architecture?

Source: Robert C. Martin, Clean Architecture: A Craftsman's Guide to Software Structure and Design (Pearson, 2017), chapter 15. Own-words digest with short attributed quotes. Previous: Clean Architecture 14: Component Coupling. Next: Clean Architecture 16: Independence.

Takeaways
  • The architect remains a programmer: architects are the best programmers and keep taking programming tasks, because they cannot do the job without experiencing the problems they create for others.
  • Architecture is the shape given to the system by those who build it: components, their arrangement and their communication. Its purpose is to support the system's life cycle (develop, deploy, operate, maintain) and minimise lifetime cost.
  • Strategy: leave as many options open as possible, for as long as possible by separating policy (business rules) from details (IO, databases, web, frameworks) and deferring decisions about details: "A good architect maximizes the number of decisions not made."
  • Architecture has little bearing on whether a system works; its troubles show in deployment, maintenance and development.

15.1 What an architect is, and what architecture is

  • The word "architecture" "conjures visions of power and mystery"; many aspiring developers dream of becoming an architect. What is it, what does an architect do and when?
  • A software architect is a programmer and continues to be one.
    • "Never fall for the lie that suggests that software architects pull back from code to focus on higher-level issues." They are the best programmers, still take programming tasks (maybe fewer than others), and guide the team toward a design that maximises productivity
    • they must experience the problems they create for the other programmers.
  • The architecture of a software system is the shape given to that system by those who build it. The shape is the division into components, the arrangement of those components, and the ways they communicate.
  • Its purpose is to facilitate the development, deployment, operation and maintenance of the system
    • Its strategy is to leave as many options open as possible, for as long as possible.
  • Surprising? The goal is not mainly making the system work:
    • the architecture "has very little bearing on whether that system works"
    • Many systems with terrible architectures work fine; their troubles lie in deployment, maintenance and ongoing development
    • Architecture's role in behaviour is critical but "passive and cosmetic, not active or essential": few behavioural options can be left open by it.
  • The primary purpose is to support the system's life cycle:
    • easy to understand, develop, maintain and deploy
    • the ultimate goal is to minimise lifetime cost and maximise programmer productivity.

15.2 Development

  • A system that is hard to develop is unlikely to have a long, healthy life, so architecture should make it easy to develop for the team(s) who build it
    • Different team structures imply different architectural decisions.
    • A small team of five can work together effectively on a monolith without well-defined components or interfaces
      • in early days the strictures of an architecture would be an impediment. "This is likely the reason why so many systems lack good architecture: they were begun with none."
    • A system built by five teams of seven developers cannot progress unless divided into well-defined components with reliably stable interfaces
      • If no other factor is considered, the architecture will evolve into five components, one per team
      • That is unlikely to be best for deployment, operation and maintenance, but it is what teams gravitate to if driven solely by the development schedule.

15.3 Deployment

  • To be effective a system must be deployable; the higher the cost of deployment the less useful the system
    • A goal is a system easily deployed with a single action
    • Deployment strategy is seldom considered during initial development, giving architectures easy to develop but very hard to deploy.
  • Example:
    • early on developers choose a micro-service architecture: firm boundaries and relatively stable interfaces make development easy, but at deployment the number of micro-services is daunting and configuring the connections and start-up timing is a huge source of errors
    • Considering deployment early might have led to fewer services, a hybrid of services and in-process components, and a more integrated way of managing the interconnections.

15.4 Operation

  • The impact on operation is less dramatic than on development, deployment and maintenance: almost any operational difficulty can be solved by throwing more hardware at the system, and inefficient architectures often work after adding storage and servers
    • Hardware is cheap and people are expensive, so architectures that impede operation cost less than those that impede development, deployment and maintenance
    • (A well-tuned architecture is still desirable: the cost equation just leans the other way.)
  • Another role: a good architecture communicates the operational needs: "Architecture should reveal operation." Use cases, features and required behaviours should be first-class entities, visible landmarks for developers, simplifying understanding and so development and maintenance.

15.5 Maintenance

  • The most costly
    • aspect of a system: a never-ending parade of features and the trail of defects and corrections consume vast human resources
    • The primary costs are spelunking (digging through existing software to find the best place and strategy for a new feature or fix) and risk (the likelihood of creating inadvertent defects).
  • A thought-through architecture vastly mitigates both: separate the system into components, isolate them with stable interfaces, and you "illuminate the pathways for future features" and greatly reduce the risk of inadvertent breakage.

15.6 Keeping options open

  • Software has two values:
    • behaviour and structure; structure is greater because it makes software soft
    • That flexibility depends on the shape of the system, the arrangement of components and how they are interconnected
    • To keep software soft, leave as many options open as possible, for as long as possible. The options to leave open are "the details that don't matter".
  • All software decomposes into policy and details.
    • Policy is all the business rules and procedures: "where the true value of the system lives"
    • Details are what enable humans, other systems and programmers to communicate with the policy but do not affect its behaviour at all: IO devices, databases, web systems, servers, frameworks, communication protocols and so forth.
  • The architect's goal:
    • a shape that treats policy as the most essential element while making the details irrelevant to it, so decisions about details can be delayed and deferred
    • Examples:
    • No need to choose a database early: high-level policy should not care whether it is relational, distributed, hierarchical or plain flat files.
    • No need to choose a web server early:
      • policy should not know it is delivered over the web or care about HTML, AJAX, JSP, JSF ("the rest of the alphabet soup")
      • you need not even decide whether the system is delivered over the web.
    • No need to adopt REST early, nor a micro-services or SOA framework: policy should be agnostic about the interface to the outside world.
    • No need for a dependency injection framework early: policy should not care how dependencies are resolved.
  • If you can develop policy without committing to its surrounding details you can defer decisions for a long time, and the longer you wait the more information you have
    • It also leaves room for experiments: with policy partly working and database-agnostic you could connect several databases to check applicability and performance, likewise web systems and frameworks, or the web itself
    • The longer options stay open the more experiments and information you have when a decision can no longer be deferred.
  • If someone else has already decided (a database, web server or framework committed by the company): "a good architect pretends that the decision has not been made, and shapes the system such that those decisions can still be deferred or changed for as long as possible." "A good architect maximizes the number of decisions not made."

15.7 Device independence

  • A story from the 1960s, when programmers were mostly mathematicians or engineers from other disciplines ("and one third or more were women")
    • One mistake was binding code directly to IO devices: to print, code used the printer's IO instructions (device dependent)
    • Martin's PDP-8 example is a PRTCHR subroutine printing one character on the teleprinter, using TSF (skip if the teleprinter is ready), JMP .-1 (loop), TLS (send the character) and JMP I PRTCHR (return): the code talks straight to hardware.
  • It worked at first:
    • card reader and punch code talked directly to those devices
    • But big batches of punched cards are hard to manage (lost, mutilated, spindled, shuffled, dropped, extra or blank cards): data integrity became a problem
    • Magnetic tape solved it (a dropped tape does not shuffle records; it is more secure, faster, easy to back up)
    • But all the software manipulated card devices and had to be rewritten, "a big job".
  • By the late 1960s the lesson was learned and device independence was invented: operating systems abstracted IO devices into software functions handling unit records that looked like cards
    • programs called OS services for abstract unit-record devices, and operators told the OS whether to connect them to card readers, tape or any other. The same program could read and write cards or tape unchanged: "The Open-Closed Principle was born (but not yet named)."

15.8 Junk mail

  • In the late 1960s Martin worked for a company printing junk mail: clients sent magnetic tapes of unit records of customer names and addresses plus 500-pound rolls of form letters (thousands each; hundreds of rolls)
    • programs extracted names, addresses and other elements and printed them exactly where needed ("Hello Mr. Martin, Congratulations! We chose YOU ... who lives on Witchwood Lane...").
  • At first an IBM 360 printed on its sole line printer (a few thousand letters per shift), tying up a very expensive machine (360s rented for tens of thousands of dollars a month)
    • So they told the OS to use magnetic tape instead of the printer, and the programs did not care because they used the OS's IO abstractions
    • The 360 wrote a full tape in about 10 minutes; tapes went to offline printers (five, running 24 hours a day, 7 days a week) printing hundreds of thousands of pieces a week.
  • "The value of device independence was enormous": they tested on the local line printer then "printed" to tape for volume
    • **"Our programs had a shape
    • That shape disconnected policy from detail."** The policy was formatting name and address records
    • the detail was the device
    • the device decision was deferred.

15.9 Physical addressing

  • In the early 1970s Martin worked on a large accounting system for a truckers union with a 25 MB disk, storing Agent, Employer and Member records of different sizes: the first cylinders were formatted with sectors fitting Agent records, the next Employer records, the last Member records
    • The software knew the disk's detailed structure (200 cylinders, 10 heads, several dozen sectors per head, which cylinders held which records) hard-wired into the code
    • An index on specially formatted cylinders held each record's ID plus cylinder, head and sector numbers
      • Members were also in a doubly linked list whose records held the cylinder/head/sector of the next and previous record.
  • Upgrading to a disk with more heads, cylinders or sectors would need a special program to copy and translate all the addresses, and changing hard-wiring "everywhere": all business rules knew the scheme in detail
    • A more experienced programmer joined, "the blood drained from his face", and advised relative addresses: treat the disk as one huge linear array of sectors addressed by sequential integers, with a small conversion routine that knows the physical structure and translates on the fly
    • They took the advice and made high-level policy agnostic about the disk's physical structure, decoupling the decision about disk structure from the application.

15.10 Conclusion

  • The two stories are examples "in the small" of a principle architects use "in the large": carefully separate details from policy, then decouple the policy from the details so thoroughly that the policy has no knowledge of them and does not depend on them in any way
    • design the policy so decisions about details can be delayed and deferred as long as possible.

Summary points

  • Architects code; architecture is shape (components, arrangement, communication) in service of the life cycle.
  • Most cost is in deployment, maintenance and development, not operation (hardware is cheap, people are expensive).
  • Separate policy from details and defer detail decisions; maximise the decisions not made.
Terms to carry forward
  • Architecture as shape; life cycle; spelunking.
  • Policy versus details; deferring decisions; device independence.
  • "Architecture should reveal operation"; "maximize the number of decisions not made".
