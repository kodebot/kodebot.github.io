Clean Architecture 32: Frameworks Are Details

Source: Robert C. Martin, Clean Architecture: A Craftsman's Guide to Software Structure and Design (Pearson, 2017), chapter 32. Own-words digest. Previous: Clean Architecture 31: The Web Is a Detail. Next: Clean Architecture 33: Case Study, Video Sales.

Takeaways
  • Frameworks are not architectures, though some try to be. Generally they are free, powerful and useful, but their authors do not have your best interests at heart: they solve their own problems.
  • The relationship is an asymmetric marriage: you commit hugely, the author commits to nothing.
  • Risks: frameworks violate the Dependency Rule, you outgrow them, they evolve unhelpfully, better ones appear.
  • Solution: "Don't marry the framework!" Keep it in an outer circle; use proxies, not framework base classes in your business objects; inject with Spring into Main.

32.1 Framework authors

  • Frameworks have become popular, "generally ... a good thing": many are free, powerful and useful. "However, frameworks are not architectures, though some try to be."
  • Most authors offer their work free to be helpful and give back, which is "laudable"
    • but "those authors do not have your best interests at heart": they don't know you or your problems
    • They write for their own problems and those of their coworkers and friends
    • Your problems likely overlap a lot (else frameworks would not be popular), and to that extent frameworks are very useful.

32.2 Asymmetric marriage

  • "The relationship between you and the framework author is extraordinarily asymmetric
    • You must make a huge commitment to the framework, but the framework author makes no commitment to you whatsoever."
  • The documentation advises you how to integrate: typically wrapping your architecture around the framework, deriving from its base classes and importing its facilities into your business objects: "couple your application to the framework as tightly as possible".
  • For the author, coupling to his or her own framework is no risk (absolute control)
    • The author wants you coupled because it is very hard to break away, and "nothing feels more validating ..
    • than a bunch of users willing to inextricably derive from the author's base classes".
  • "In effect, the author is asking you to marry the framework": a huge, long-term commitment with no corresponding commitment back: "a one-directional marriage. You take on all the risk and burden
    • the framework author takes on nothing at all."

32.3 The risks

  • The framework's architecture is often not clean.
    • Frameworks tend to violate the Dependency Rule: they ask you to inherit their code into your business objects, your Entities, the innermost circle
    • "Once in, that framework isn't coming back out
    • The wedding ring is on your finger."
  • You may outgrow it. It may help early features, but as the product matures it outgrows the framework's facilities, and "the framework [will be] fighting you more and more".
  • It may evolve in an unhelpful direction. You may be stuck upgrading to versions that do not help you; old features you used may disappear or change in ways hard to keep up with.
  • A new and better framework may come along that you wish you could switch to.

32.4 The solution

  • "Don't marry the framework!" You can use it; just don't couple to it. Keep it at arm's length, as a detail in one of the outer circles, not the inner ones.
  • If the framework wants you to derive your business objects from its base classes, "say no! Derive proxies instead, and keep those proxies in components that are plugins to your business rules." Integrate frameworks into components that plug into your core code, following the Dependency Rule.
  • Spring example:
    • it is a good dependency injection framework and you may use it to auto-wire dependencies
    • that is fine, but do not sprinkle @autowired annotations throughout business objects: "Your business objects should not know about Spring." Instead use Spring to inject dependencies into your Main component
    • Main may know Spring since it is "the dirtiest, lowest-level component in the architecture" (see Clean Architecture 26: The Main Component).

32.5 "I now pronounce you ..."

  • Some frameworks you must marry:
    • in C++ you will likely have to marry the STL
    • in Java you will almost certainly marry the standard library. That is normal but should still be a decision: you will be stuck with the framework for the rest of the application's life cycle ("for better or for worse, in sickness and in health ... forsaking all others"), "not a commitment to be entered into lightly".

32.6 Conclusion

  • Do not marry a framework right away; "see if there aren't ways to date it for a while"
    • Keep it behind an architectural boundary if at all possible, for as long as possible
    • "Perhaps you can find a way to get the milk without buying the cow."

Summary points

  • A framework is a detail; keep its types out of Entities and use cases.
  • Use proxies and plugin components; wire frameworks only in Main.
  • If you must marry (language standard libraries), do it deliberately.
Terms to carry forward
  • Asymmetric marriage; proxy instead of framework base class.
  • Framework as plugin; Spring wiring in Main.
