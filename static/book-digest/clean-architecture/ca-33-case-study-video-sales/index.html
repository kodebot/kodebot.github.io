Clean Architecture 33: Case Study, Video Sales

Source: Robert C. Martin, Clean Architecture: A Craftsman's Guide to Software Structure and Design (Pearson, 2017), chapter 33. Own-words digest. Previous: Clean Architecture 32: Frameworks Are Details. Next: Clean Architecture 34: The Missing Chapter.

Takeaways
  • A short, simple case study of the process and decisions of a good architect, for a website that sells videos (reminiscent of cleancoders.com).
  • Step one: identify actors and use cases. The four actors are the four primary sources of change (SRP), so partition the system so a change for one does not affect the others.
  • An abstract use case (View Catalog) unifies two similar ones.
  • The component architecture splits views, presenters, interactors and controllers by actor; dependencies follow the Dependency Rule; components can be grouped into deployables in any way and regrouped later.

33.1 The product

  • Software for a website that sells videos, reminiscent of cleancoders.com, where Martin sells his software tutorial videos
    • We sell videos on the web to individuals and businesses.
    • Individuals
      • pay one price to stream, and a higher price to download and own permanently
      • Business licences are streaming only, bought in batches with quantity discounts.
    • Individuals typically act as both viewers and purchasers; in businesses, people who buy the videos often differ from those who watch them.
    • Video authors supply video files, written descriptions and ancillary files (exams, problems, solutions, source code and other materials).
    • Administrators add new video series, add and delete videos to and from series, and establish prices for various licences.
  • The first step in determining the initial architecture is to identify the actors and use cases.

33.2 Use case analysis (Figure 33.1)

  • The four main actors are evident
    • By the Single Responsibility Principle, these four actors will be the four primary sources of change: every new or changed feature serves one of them
    • So partition the system so a change to one actor does not affect any other.
  • The use cases are not a complete list: for example there are no log-in or log-out use cases, omitted to manage size ("this chapter would have to turn into a book in its own right").
  • The dashed use cases in the centre are abstract:
    • one that sets a general policy another use case flesh out
    • View Catalog as Viewer and View Catalog as Purchaser both inherit from the abstract View Catalog
    • It was not strictly necessary (the product's features would not be compromised without it), but the two are so similar that Martin thought it wise to recognise the similarity and unify it early
    • (Footnote: the dashed notation is his own; a UML <<abstract>> stereotype would be more standard, but "I don't find adhering to such standards very useful nowadays".)

33.3 Component architecture (Figure 33.2)

  • With actors and use cases known, a preliminary component architecture
    • Double lines are architectural boundaries
    • It shows the typical partitioning into views, presenters, interactors and controllers, each category broken up by actor
    • Each component is a potential .jar or .dll file holding its allocated views, presenters, interactors and controllers.
  • The special Catalog View and Catalog Presenter components handle the abstract View Catalog use case: they are assumed to hold abstract classes, which the inheriting components' view and presenter classes extend.
  • Would he really split the system into all these components and deliver them as jars or DLLs? "Yes and no." He would break the compile and build environment up that way to build independent deliverables, and reserve the right to combine them into fewer deliverables
    • For example, five jars (views, presenters, interactors, controllers, utilities) so the components most likely to change independently can be deployed independently
    • or views and presenters together with interactors, controllers and utilities in another jar
    • or, more primitively, two jars: views and presenters, and everything else. Keeping these options open lets the deployment adapt to how the system changes.

33.4 Dependency management

  • The flow of control in Figure 33.2 runs right to left: input arrives at the controllers, the interactors process it into a result, the presenters format the results, and the views display the presentations.
  • But most arrows point left to right, because the architecture follows the Dependency Rule: all dependencies cross the boundary lines in one direction, always toward the components containing the higher-level policy.
  • Also, using relationships (open arrows) point with the flow of control, and inheritance relationships (closed arrows) point against it: this is the Open-Closed Principle making dependencies flow the right way, so changes to low-level details do not ripple upward to high-level policies.

33.5 Conclusion

  • The architecture has two dimensions of separation:
    • actors (SRP) and the Dependency Rule
    • "The goal of both is to separate components that change for different reasons, and at different rates": the different reasons are the actors, the different rates are the different levels of policy.
  • Once structured this way, you can mix and match deployment: group components into deliverables in any way that makes sense, and change the grouping when conditions change.

Summary points

  • Start from actors (sources of change) and use cases.
  • Split view, presenter, interactor and controller components per actor; unify shared policy in abstract use cases and components.
  • Keep boundaries and dependencies pointing inward; decide how to package for deployment last.
Terms to carry forward
  • Actor as source of change; abstract use case.
  • Views, presenters, interactors, controllers as components.
  • Two dimensions: SRP (actors) and Dependency Rule (policy levels).
