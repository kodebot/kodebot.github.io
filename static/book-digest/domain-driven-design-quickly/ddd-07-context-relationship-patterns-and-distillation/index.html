DDD Quickly 7: Context relationship patterns and distillation

Source: Abel Avram, Domain Driven Design Quickly (InfoQ, 2006), second half of "Preserving Model Integrity": Shared Kernel to Distillation (pp. 75-90). Previous: DDD Quickly 6: Preserving model integrity: bounded context, continuous integration, context map. Next: DDD Quickly 8: DDD Matters Today, an interview with Eric Evans.

[!abstract] The one-minute version Once contexts exist you choose how each pair relates, from tight to independent:

Then distillation separates the Core Domain (the real business asset, where your best people work) from Generic Subdomains (supporting concepts you can buy, outsource, borrow or build with less priority).

Shared Kernel

  • When: functional integration between contexts is limited, so Continuous Integration overhead is judged too high: teams lack the skill or political organisation for it, or a single team is too big and unwieldy, so separate contexts and teams are formed.
  • Risk of the alternative:
    • uncoordinated teams on closely related apps "can go racing forward for a while", but what they produce may not fit together
    • They end up spending more on translation layers and retrofitting than Continuous Integration would have cost, duplicating effort and losing the common Ubiquitous Language.
  • Pattern:
    • designate a subset of the domain model that two teams agree to share, including the corresponding subset of code or database design
    • This shared part has special status: do not change it without consulting the other team.
  • Integrate a functional system frequently but somewhat less often than the Continuous Integration pace within each team; during these integrations run both teams' tests.
  • Purpose:
    • reduce duplication while keeping two separate contexts
    • It needs care: both teams may change the kernel, so they must integrate changes
    • With separate copies, merge at least weekly, keep a test suite so every kernel change is tested at once, and communicate every change so the other team knows the new functionality.

Customer-Supplier

Situation: two subsystems in different contexts, one depends a lot on the other: the processing result of one feeds the other. A Shared Kernel is not conceptually correct or even technically possible (different technologies, concepts overlap too little).

Running example (e-commerce):

  • The reporting application is very interested in data the shop saves, and needs extra information (items put in the basket then dropped, links visited most) that means nothing to the shop but a lot to reporting
    • So the supplier (shop) must implement specifications needed by the customer (reporting).
  • Both use the same database, so the reporting team needs schema stability, but the schema cannot be frozen: a change is no problem for the shop, certainly one for reporting
    • The teams must communicate, perhaps work on the database together and decide when to change.
  • If the shop team has veto rights it may block changes and hurt the reporting team
    • If it acts independently it will sooner or later break agreements
    • The pattern works well when both teams are under the same management, easing decisions and creating harmony.

Pattern:

  • The reporting team plays the customer, the shop team the supplier. They meet regularly or on request "as a customer does with his supplier". The customer presents requirements
    • the supplier plans accordingly
      • All requirements are met eventually, but the supplier decides the timetable: important ones sooner, others postponed
      • The customer also needs input and knowledge from the supplier
    • the flow is one way but necessary.
  • Define the interface precisely and create a conformity test suite. In planning sessions make the customer team behave as a customer
    • negotiate and budget tasks so everyone understands commitment and schedule.
  • Jointly develop automated acceptance tests
    • that validate the expected interface, and add them to the supplier team's test suite so they run in its continuous integration
    • This frees the supplier to change things without fear of side effects on the customer.

Conformist

  • Customer-Supplier works only when both teams care about the relationship: the customer depends on the supplier, the supplier does not depend on the customer
    • If management made it work, the supplier listens
    • If management is poor or absent, the supplier slowly cares more about its own model and deadlines and less about helping the customer: time pressure wins even with good people
    • It is worse across different companies: communication is hard and the supplier's company may help sporadically or refuse.
  • If the supplier has no motivation, the customer team is helpless: altruism yields promises that are unlikely to be fulfilled
    • believing them means planning on features that never arrive
    • the project slips until the team learns to live with what it gets. A tailored interface is "not in the cards".
  • Options for the customer:
    1. Separate Ways: separate completely, if the supplier's benefits are not worth the trouble; build your own model.
    2. Translation layer / Anticorruption Layer: if the supplier's model has some value, keep a connection but protect yourself from changes; use an Anticorruption Layer especially if the supplier's model is poorly conceived.
    3. Conformity: if you must use the supplier's model and it is well done, adhere to it entirely. It is like a Shared Kernel except that the customer cannot change the kernel; it can only use it and build on top. Viable when somebody provides a rich component with an interface: include it in your model as if it were your own. If the component's interface is small, write an adapter that translates between your model and the component's; that isolates your model and gives you freedom.

Anticorruption Layer

  • Problem:
    • your new application must interact with legacy software or a separate application
    • Many legacy apps were not built with domain modelling, so their model is confused and entangled; even a good one differs from yours
    • Integration is still required.
  • Ways to interact:
    • over the network (same protocol, adhere to the external interface) or through the database (both access the same data)
    • Both transfer primitive data, but primitives carry no model information: a relational database is a web of related primitives with a lot of hidden semantics
      • you cannot write to it without understanding the meaning
    • Parts of the external model reflect in the database and make their way into yours.
  • Risk: if you let it, the external model alters your client model. So isolate with an Anticorruption Layer between your model and the external one.
  • To your model the layer looks like a natural part of it, speaking your concepts and actions
    • to the external system it speaks the external language. It is a two-way translator between two domains and languages
    • the achievement is that your model stays pure and consistent.

  • Implementation:
    • see the layer as a Service from the client's model: it abstracts the other system, lets you address it in your terms, and does the translation
      • Implement the Service as a Façade (Gamma et al., Design Patterns, 1995)
    • you will most likely also need an Adapter (converts a class's interface to the one the client understands; here it translates between two systems rather than wrapping a class).
  • One Façade and one Adapter per Service; the layer may contain several Services. Do not use a single Adapter for all Services: it would be cluttered with mixed functionality.
  • A Translator (a simple object with little functionality) does the object and data conversion
    • If the external interface is complex, add an extra Façade between the adapters and it to simplify the Adapter's protocol and separate it from the other system.

Separate Ways

  • All integration takes effort and compromise:
    • constant merging and testing, one team spending time on requirements the other needs, altering a model just to fit another system, or adding translation layers
    • Evaluate the benefit and integrate only if there is real value. If integration is more trouble than it is worth, go separate ways.
  • Applies when
    • an enterprise application is several smaller applications with little or nothing in common from a modelling perspective: one set of requirements, one application for the user, but separate models and implementations
    • Look at the requirements and see if they split into sets with little in common; if so, create separate Bounded Contexts and model independently
    • Benefit: freedom to choose the technologies.
  • They may share a thin GUI as a portal (links or buttons to each app): minor integration about organising the apps, not the models behind them.
  • Before choosing it make sure you will not come back to an integrated system: independently developed models are very difficult to integrate (they have so little in common it is not worth it).

Open Host Service

  • Integrating two subsystems usually means a translation layer, a buffer whose size depends on the relationships and how the external system was designed
    • If the external subsystem has many clients, each needs its own translation layer, all repeating the same task with similar code
    • maintenance and worry about changes grow.
  • Solution:
    • treat the external subsystem as a provider of services
    • Wrap a set of Services around it; other subsystems use them and need no translation layer
    • Difficulty: each subsystem may need a specific interaction, so a coherent set of Services can be hard.

  • Pattern:
    • define a protocol that gives access to your subsystem as a set of Services
    • open it so everyone who must integrate can use it
    • enhance and expand it for new integration requirements, except when a single team has idiosyncratic needs: then use a one-off translator for that team so the shared protocol stays simple and coherent.
  • (The book's map also pairs it with a Published Language: a documented, formalised shared language for the protocol; the text of this chapter does not expand on it.)

Distillation

Analogy: distillation separates the substances of a mixture to extract one; byproducts may also be of interest.

A large domain gives a large model even after refinement, abstraction and many refactorings. Time for distillation: define a Core Domain representing the essence of the domain; the byproducts are Generic Subdomains covering the rest. In a large system so many components are complicated and necessary that "the essence of the domain model, the real business asset, can be obscured and neglected".

Air-traffic example.

  • The Flight Plan contains the planned Route, which seems ever-present; actually it is generic: used in many domains, a generic model for routes can be designed.
  • The essence lies elsewhere
    • The monitoring system knows the planned route but also receives input from a network of radars showing the actual path, usually different
    • It computes the plane's trajectory from current flight parameters, plane characteristics and weather: a four-dimensional path describing where the plane will be in time, computed for the next minutes, dozens of minutes or hours
    • The purpose: see if the plane's path may cross another's, especially near airports where many planes circle or manoeuvre
    • if a plane strays from the plan a crash is likely. The system alerts when intersection is possible so controllers can decide fast
    • computing farther ahead gives more reaction time when planes are far apart.
  • The module that synthesises the trajectory from the available data is the heart of the system: the Core Domain. The routing model is more of a generic domain.
  • The core depends on your viewpoint:
    • a simple routing system sees Route and its dependencies as central; the air-traffic system sees Route as a generic subdomain
    • A system's core may be another's generic subdomain
    • Identify the core correctly and its relationships with other parts.

Pattern: boil the model down. Find the Core Domain, make it easy to tell apart from the mass of supporting model and code, emphasise the most valuable and specialised concepts, make the core small. Apply your top talent to the Core, recruit accordingly, spend effort finding a deep model and a supple design; justify investment in any other part by how it supports the distilled core.

  • Put the best developers on the Core.
    • Developers like technology (newest languages, infrastructure) and find business logic "boring and of little reward" ("what's the point in learning plane trajectories? When the project is done, all that knowledge is a thing of the past")
    • But business logic is the heart: mistakes in the core can lead to abandoning the whole project, and if the core does not do its job "all the technological bells and whistles will amount to nothing".
  • The core is not created in one step:
    • it needs refinement and successive refactorings
    • enforce it as the central piece, delimit its boundaries, and rethink and maybe refactor the other elements in relation to it.

Generic subdomains. Parts of the model add complexity without capturing specialised knowledge and make the core harder to see: "general principles everyone knows" or details of specialties that are not your focus. Yet they are essential.

Identify cohesive subdomains that are not the motivation of the project, factor out generic models, put them in separate Modules, and leave no trace of your specialties in them. Give them lower priority than the Core, do not assign core developers (they gain little domain knowledge), and consider off-the-shelf solutions or published models.

Examples: money, currency, exchange rate, and charting (complex in itself, used by many apps).

Four ways to implement a Generic Subdomain:

  1. Off-the-shelf solution: the whole thing is done by someone else. Still a learning curve and dependencies; buggy code waits for a fix; you must use certain compilers and library versions; integration is harder than in-house.
  2. Outsourcing: another team, maybe another company, designs and implements it; you focus on the core. You still must integrate the code and define and communicate the interface.
  3. Existing model: books of analysis patterns can inspire; they may not be copied literally but many work with small changes.
  4. In-house implementation: the best integration, at the cost of extra effort and maintenance.
