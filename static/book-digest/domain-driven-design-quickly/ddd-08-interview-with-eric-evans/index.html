DDD Quickly 8: DDD Matters Today, an interview with Eric Evans

Source: Abel Avram, Domain Driven Design Quickly (InfoQ, 2006), "DDD Matters Today: An interview with Eric Evans" (pp. 91-96), an InfoQ.com interview with the author of the original DDD book. Previous: DDD Quickly 7: Context relationship patterns and distillation. Last part of the book. The claims below are Evans' opinions from 2006.

The one-minute version

Evans' definition: DDD is the principle that we should focus on the deep issues of the users' domain, devote the best part of our minds to understanding it, and collaborate with experts to wrestle it into a conceptual form we can use to build powerful, flexible software. It does not go out of style, applies in any complex domain, and is a team practice. Platforms that give expressive ways to write business logic and less clutter help; DSLs may be the next step.

Why DDD matters (his answer)

  • It applies whenever we work in a complex, intricate domain. The long-term trend is applying software to more complex problems, deeper into the heart of businesses.
  • That trend was interrupted for a few years by the web: value lay in getting data onto the web with very simple behaviour, and even simple things were hard for a while, so that absorbed all the effort and attention moved away from rich logic.
  • Now
    • that basic web usage is assimilated, projects are more ambitious about business logic again
    • web platforms matured enough to be productive
    • SOA, used well, gives a way to isolate the domain
    • Agile influence means most projects at least intend to iterate, work closely with business partners, apply continuous integration, and communicate a lot.

Technology platforms

  • New technologies and processes should be judged on whether they help teams focus on the domain or distract from it

    • DDD is not tied to a platform, but some give more expressive ways to write business logic and have less distracting clutter.

  • Java

    • (the default choice then): expressive like OO languages, and the base language has acceptable clutter: garbage collection is "in practice essential" (versus C++, which demanded too much attention to low-level detail)
    • plain old Java objects (POJOs) can still be readable
    • Java 5 syntax improvements help.

  • Early J2EE

    • "utterly buried" that expressiveness under mountains of framework code. Following its early conventions (EJB home, get/set accessors for every variable, etc.) produced terrible objects
    • tools were so cumbersome they absorbed all the team capacity
    • changing objects after the "huge mess of generated code and XML" was so hard that people did not. It made effective domain modelling almost impossible.

  • Add the imperative to build web UIs over HTTP and HTML (not designed for that) with primitive first-generation tools: UI became so hard to create and maintain that little attention remained for complex internal functionality

    • "Ironically, at the very moment that object technology took over, sophisticated modeling and design took a heavy hit." .NET was similar.

  • Trends turned in the last four years or so:

    • the community learned to use frameworks selectively, and many (mostly open-source) lighter frameworks arrived: Hibernate and Spring handle jobs J2EE tried to, much lighter
    • AJAX attacks the UI problem with less labour
    • projects pick the J2EE pieces that bring value
      • The term POJO was coined in this era. Result: less technical effort and better isolation of business logic in POJOs
      • That "does not automatically produce a domain-driven design, but it makes it a realistic opportunity."

  • Ruby:

    • very expressive syntax, a good basic language for DDD (though he had not heard of much use in such applications)
    • Rails excited people by making web UIs as easy as in the early 1990s, but so far applied mostly to the many web applications without much domain richness
    • His hope: as UI effort shrinks, attention moves to the domain; then Ruby could be an excellent DDD platform (a few infrastructure pieces missing).

  • Domain-specific languages (DSLs): he long believed they could be the next big step for DDD; no tool gives what is needed yet, but people are experimenting more than ever.

  • At the time most people applying DDD used Java or .NET, a few Smalltalk.

The DDD community

  • StatOil (the Norwegian national oil company): architects wrote an experience report on strategic design, and applied context mapping to evaluating off-the-shelf software in build-vs-buy decisions, a use he did not expect (reports at http://domaindrivendesign.org/articles/).
  • timeandmoney
    • (http://timeandmoney.domainlanguage.com): a Java library of fundamental domain objects many projects need, exploring how far a fluent, domain-specific language can be pushed while implementing objects in Java
    • meant as a good example to learn from by using it.

Advice for learning DDD

  • "Read my book! ;-)" and try using timeandmoney on your project.
  • DDD is largely something teams do, so you may have to be an evangelist, or go find a project that is making the effort.
  • Four pitfalls of domain modelling:
  1. Stay hands-on. Modelers need to code.
  2. Focus on concrete scenarios. Abstract thinking has to be anchored in concrete cases.
  3. Don't try to apply DDD to everything. Draw a context map, decide where you will push for DDD and where not, and do not worry outside those boundaries.
  4. Experiment a lot and expect to make lots of mistakes. Modelling is a creative process.

About Eric Evans (from the book)

Author of Domain-Driven Design: Tackling Complexity in Software (Addison-Wesley, 2004).

Since the early 1990s he worked on many large business systems with objects, with many approaches and outcomes; the book synthesises that experience: modelling and design techniques that successful teams used to align complex software with business needs and keep projects agile as they grow.

He leads Domain Language, a consulting group that coaches and trains teams applying DDD. (The book ends with Domain Language's advertisement: training, mentors, coaches, strategic design consultants and an assessment; contact details omitted here.)

Terms to carry forward

strategic design · context mapping (also for build-vs-buy) · POJO era · DSL · the four modelling pitfalls.
