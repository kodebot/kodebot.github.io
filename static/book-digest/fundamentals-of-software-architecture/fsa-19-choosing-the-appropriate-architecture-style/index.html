Fundamentals of Software Architecture 19: Choosing the Appropriate Architecture Style

Source: Mark Richards and Neal Ford, Fundamentals of Software Architecture, 2nd edition (O'Reilly, 2025), chapter 19. Previous: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 18: Microservices Architecture. Next: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 20: Architectural Patterns.

Takeaways
  • Style choice is the culmination of trade-off analysis: architecture characteristics, domain, strategy. It depends, and the star charts compare characteristics, not domains, because that is where styles really differ.
  • "Fashion" shifts for six reasons: observations from the past, ecosystem changes, new capabilities, acceleration, domain changes, technology changes, external factors.
  • Know the domain, the driving characteristics, data architecture, cloud, organisation, and process, team and operations maturity before choosing.
  • Use domain/architecture isomorphism (the shape of the topology) to test fit, then decide: monolith or distributed, where data lives, sync or async (default synchronous).
  • Worked cases: Silicon Sandwiches (modular monolith or microkernel with BFF) and Going, Going, Gone (microservices, five quanta).

19.1 Shifting "fashion" in architecture

Industry preferences shift for a number of reasons. Architects should understand current trends so they can decide which to follow and when to make exceptions, however closely their organisation follows fashion.

Driver What happens Example
Observations from the past New styles fix pain points of earlier ones After code-reuse-centred architectures, architects rethought the negative trade-offs of reuse
Changes in the ecosystem Everything changes constantly and unpredictably Not long ago no one knew Kubernetes; it may be replaced by a tool not yet written
New capabilities Whole new paradigms, not tool swaps; even a minor change aligned with your goals can change everything Docker containers reshaped architects, tools and practices
Acceleration Change gets faster and more pervasive; new tools create new practices, designs and capabilities The rise of generative AI
Domain changes Businesses evolve or merge
Technology changes Organisations keep up with changes that have a clear bottom-line benefit
External factors Only peripherally related to software A tool's licence cost becomes prohibitive and forces a migration

19.2 Decision criteria

An architect designs two things: the specified domain and the structural elements (architecture characteristics) needed for success. Choose a style only with enough knowledge of:

Factor What to know
The domain Especially aspects that affect operational characteristics. Not subject-matter expertise: business analysts fill gaps
Characteristics that drive structure Do an architectural characteristics analysis. Any generic style can implement nearly any domain, except domains needing special operational characteristics (a highly scalable auction site); in most cases styles differ in how well they support characteristics, which is why Part II's star charts compare characteristics, not domains
Data architecture Architects and data developers collaborate on databases and schemas; understand the impact of data design, especially when interacting with an older data architecture
Cloud deployments The trade-offs differ from on-prem; know how much data is stored and moved (data movement can cost a lot). Sophisticated capabilities become commodities: a decade ago a highly elastic, scalable on-prem system took esoteric skills; now it is cloud configuration parameters
Organisational factors A cloud vendor's cost may block an ideal design; planned mergers and acquisitions favour open solutions and integration architectures
Process, teams and operations The development process, interaction with operations, QA. Example: an organisation lacking mature Agile engineering will struggle with a style that depends on it, such as microservices

19.3 Domain/architecture isomorphism

Isomorphism is the generic shape of an architecture: how components depend on each other in the topology. The word means "a map that preserves sets and relations among elements" (Greek isos, equal, and morph, form or shape).

  • Figure 19-1: layered versus modular monolith shows separation by layers versus by domains.
  • Figure 19-2: a monolith (components inside one container) versus a distributed architecture (separate components linked together) makes the macro-level structure clear.
  • Good matches.
    • A system needing customisability suits microkernel, with customisations as plug-ins
    • A genome analysis system needing many discrete operations may suit space-based architecture, which offers many discrete processors.
  • Poor matches.
    • Highly scalable systems struggle with large monoliths: a tightly coupled code base can hardly support many concurrent users
    • A domain with lots of semantic coupling matches poorly with a decoupled distributed style: an insurance application of multipage forms, each based on the context of previous pages is highly coupled, hard to model in microservices, and suits service-based architecture (intentionally coupled) better.

Three determinations to make:

  1. Monolith or distributed? Will one set of architecture characteristics do (monolith suitable, although other factors may suggest distributed) or do different parts need different ones (distributed)? The architecture quantum (Chapter 7) helps.
  2. Where should data live? A monolith assumes one or a few relational databases. In a distributed system, decide which services persist data and how it flows to build workflows. Consider both structure and behaviour, and iterate.
  3. Synchronous or asynchronous communication? Synchronous is more convenient but may cost scalability and reliability. Asynchronous gives performance and scale but brings headaches: data synchronisation, deadlocks, race conditions, debugging (see Chapter 15). Because synchronous has fewer design, implementation and debugging challenges: default to synchronous, use asynchronous when necessary.

The output of the process: an architecture topology (the chosen style and any hybrids), ADRs for the parts needing the most effort, and fitness functions protecting important principles and operational characteristics.

19.4 Monolith case study: Silicon Sandwiches

From the Chapter 5 kata: the characteristics analysis found a single quantum sufficient, and with a simple application and small budget a monolith appealed. There were two component designs, domain partitioned and technically partitioned.

Modular monolith

Domain-centric components, one database, deployed as one quantum: a single relational database and a single web UI (careful about mobile) to keep cost down. Each identified domain is a component.

  • If time and resources allow, separate the tables and other database assets like the domain components, which makes a later move to a distributed architecture much easier.
  • The style does not handle customisation itself, so it becomes part of the domain design: an Override endpoint where developers upload customisations, and every domain component must reference Override for each customisable characteristic
    • Checking that is a perfect job for an architectural fitness function.

Microkernel

Customisability was an identified characteristic, and domain/architecture isomorphism points to a microkernel.

  • The core is the domain components plus one relational database
    • As above, careful synchronisation of domains and data design eases a later move of the core to a distributed architecture.
  • Each customisation is a plug-in:
    • common ones in one set of plug-ins (with a corresponding database), local ones each with their own data
    • Plug-ins are not coupled to each other, so they keep their data and stay decoupled.
  • Backends for Frontends (BFF):
    • the API layer is a thin microkernel adapter
    • The API layer supplies general backend information; each BFF adapter translates it for a device
    • For example the iOS BFF customises data format, pagination, latency and other factors to what the native iOS app expects
    • Building one adapter per device allows the richest user interfaces and makes it possible to add devices later, a benefit of the style.
  • Communication within either design can be synchronous: no extreme performance or elasticity needs, and no lengthy operations.

19.5 Distributed case study: Going, Going, Gone (GGG)

From the Chapter 8 kata and its component analysis: different parts need different characteristics (availability and scalability differ between auctioneer and bidder), and the requirements state ambitious scale, elasticity and performance, plus fine-grained customisation.

  • Candidates:
    • low-level event-driven and microservices. Microservices is better at supporting variation among operational characteristics
    • purely event-driven designs typically separate pieces by whether they use orchestrated or choreographed communication, not by characteristics.
  • Performance is a challenge in microservices (too much orchestration, too aggressive data separation), but the best way to address a weak point is to design to accommodate it.

Each identified component becomes a service. Three user interfaces: Bidder (numerous), Auctioneer (one per auction) and Streamer (streams video and bids to bidders: a read-only stream, allowing optimisations impossible if updates were needed).

Service Role
Bid Capture Captures bidder entries and asynchronously sends them to Bid Tracker; no persistence needed (a conduit)
Bid Streamer Streams bids back to participants in a high-performance, read-only stream
Bid Tracker Tracks bids from both Auctioneer Capture and Bid Capture, unifies the two streams and orders bids as close to real time as possible; both inbound connections are asynchronous, so message queues buffer very different flow rates
Auctioneer Capture Captures bids for the auctioneer; separate from Bid Capture because their characteristics differ
Auction Session Manages the workflow of individual auctions
Payment Third-party payment provider handling payment after Auction Session completes the auction
Video Capture Captures the live auction video stream
Video Streamer Streams the auction video to bidders

Why asynchronous: to accommodate operational characteristics that vary between services. If Payment can process a new payment only every 500 ms and many auctions end together, synchronous calls would cause timeouts and reliability headaches; message queues make a critical but fragile part reliable.

Five quanta, roughly corresponding to the services: Payment, Auctioneer, Bidder, Bidder Streams and Bid Tracker (stacked containers show multiple instances). Quantum analysis at component design made service, data and communication boundaries easier to find.

The authors stress this is not the correct design, nor even the best possible, but it has "the least worst set of trade-offs". Choosing microservices, then using events and messages intelligently, gets the most from a generic pattern while leaving a foundation for expansion.

Summary points

  • There is no universal answer; the decision is a trade-off analysis of characteristics, domain, data, cloud, organisation and maturity.
  • Check the style's shape against the domain's shape, then settle monolith or distributed, data placement, and sync or async (default sync).
  • Record the result as a topology, ADRs and fitness functions.
  • Silicon Sandwiches suits a single-quantum monolith; GGG needs microservices with selective async messaging.
Terms to carry forward
  • Isomorphism; semantic coupling.
  • Backends for Frontends (BFF).
  • Override component; fitness function for customisation.
  • Quantum analysis for GGG.
