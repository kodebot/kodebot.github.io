Fundamentals of Software Architecture 21: Architectural Decisions

Source: Mark Richards and Neal Ford, Fundamentals of Software Architecture, 2nd edition (O'Reilly, 2025), chapter 21. Previous: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 20: Architectural Patterns. Next: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 22: Analyzing Architecture Risk.

Takeaways
  • A good architectural decision guides teams to the right technical choices: gather information, justify, document and communicate it.
  • Three progressive antipatterns: Covering Your Assets (fear of deciding), Groundhog Day (nobody knows why, so it is re-argued), Email-Driven Architecture (decisions lost in inboxes).
  • A decision is architecturally significant (Nygard) if it affects structure, non-functional characteristics, dependencies, interfaces or construction techniques.
  • ADRs: short files with Title, Status, Context, Decision, Consequences, plus Compliance and Notes. Justification (the why) is the most valuable documentation.
  • ADRs also document standards and legacy systems. Generative AI can list trade-offs but lacks the wisdom to decide.

21.1 Architectural decision antipatterns

Andrew Koenig defines an antipattern as something that seems a good idea at the start but leads into trouble; another definition is a repeatable process that produces negative results. The three common ones follow a progression: overcoming the first leads to the second, and overcoming that leads to the third. Effective decisions require overcoming all three.

Covering Your Assets

An architect avoids or defers a decision for fear of choosing wrongly. Two remedies:

  1. Wait for the last responsible moment: enough information to justify and validate the decision, but not so long that it holds up teams or causes Analysis Paralysis. Ask when the cost of deferring exceeds the risk of deciding. In Figure 21-1 early on the cost of waiting is low and the risk high (little is known); as time passes cost rises and risk falls; decide where they intersect.

  1. Collaborate with development teams to be sure the decision can be implemented as expected, since no architect knows every detail of a technology.
    • Example:
      • you decide all product reference data (description, weight, dimensions) is cached in every service that needs it, via a read-only replicated cache owned by the Catalog service, to reduce coupling and avoid interservice calls
      • The teams find some services' scalability needs more in-process memory than is available
      • Because you collaborate closely, you learn quickly and adjust.

Groundhog Day

People do not know why the architect decided, so they keep discussing it, never resolving it. Named for the 1993 film Groundhog Day, in which Bill Murray's character relives February 2 repeatedly. Cause: the decision was not justified (or not completely). Give technical and business justifications.

  • Example:
    • splitting a monolith into services to use fewer VM resources and maintain and deploy parts separately is a good technical reason, but the business asks why it should pay
    • Good business reasons: deliver functionality faster (time to market) or cut the cost of developing and releasing features.
  • Business value is also a litmus test: if a decision gives no business value, reconsider it.
  • Four common business justifications:
    • cost
    • time to market
    • user satisfaction
    • strategic positioning
  • Know what the stakeholders care about: justifying by cost savings alone is wrong if they care about time to market.

Email-Driven Architecture

The decision is lost or forgotten, or people never knew it was made, so cannot implement it. Email is a poor document repository.

  • Do not put the decision in the email body: that creates multiple systems of record, copies often omit details (including the justification), recreating Groundhog Day, and when the decision changes you cannot tell whether everyone got the revision.
  • Do state the nature and context and link to a single system of record (a wiki page or a file reference).

"Hi, Sandra, I've made an important decision regarding communication between services that directly impacts you. Please see the decision using the following link..."

  • The first half gives the context (communication between services) but not the decision.
  • "That directly impacts you" is a litmus test for whom to notify: if a decision does not affect someone, why bother them?
  • The link gives a single system of record.

21.2 Architectural significance

Many think a decision involving a specific technology is technical, not architectural. Not always: choosing a technology because it directly supports an architecture characteristic (performance, scalability) is still architectural.

Michael Nygard (author of Release It!, 2nd ed., Pragmatic Bookshelf, 2018) coined architecturally significant: decisions that affect a system's:

Aspect Meaning Example
Structure The patterns or styles used Sharing code between microservices affects their bounded context, so it affects structure
Non-functional characteristics The important architecture characteristics A technology choice that affects performance, when performance matters, is architectural even if it names a product
Dependencies Coupling points between components and services; they affect scalability, modularity, agility, testability, reliability
Interfaces How services and components are accessed and orchestrated: gateway, integration hub, service bus, adapter, API proxy; contracts, versioning and deprecation
Construction techniques Platforms, frameworks, tools and processes that, though technical, may affect the architecture

21.3 Architectural Decision Records

ADRs are one of the most effective ways to document decisions. Michael Nygard first evangelised them in a 2011 blog post, and in 2017 the Thoughtworks Technology Radar recommended the technique for widespread adoption.

An ADR is a short text file (usually one to two pages) for one decision, written in plain text, a wiki template, AsciiDoc or Markdown.

ADR Tools, an open source command-line tool by Nat Pryce (coauthor of Growing Object-Oriented Software, Guided by Tests), manages numbering, locations and superseded logic; Micha Kops gives good examples of using it.

Basic structure

Five main sections: Title, Status, Context, Decision, Consequences. The authors add two: Compliance and Notes. Extend the template if needed (an Alternatives section is a good example), but keep it consistent and concise.

Title. Numbered sequentially, a short phrase: "42. Use of Asynchronous Messaging Between Order and Payment Services". Short, concise, and descriptive enough to remove ambiguity about the nature and context.

Status. One of three:

  • Proposed: needs approval from a higher-level decision maker or governance body (such as an architecture review board).
  • Accepted: approved and ready to implement.
  • Superseded: changed and replaced by another ADR. It always assumes the prior status was Accepted; a Proposed ADR is never superseded, it is modified until Accepted.

Superseding keeps a history of what was decided, why, what replaced it and why. Example: ADR 42 (asynchronous messaging between Order and Payment) is Accepted; later changes to the Payment service's implementation and location mean REST should be used, so ADR 68 is created:

ADR 42. Use of Asynchronous Messaging Between Order and Payment Services
Status: Superseded by 68

ADR 68. Use of REST Between Order and Payment Services
Status: Accepted, supersedes 42

The trail avoids the inevitable "what about using messaging?" question about ADR 68.

ADRs and Request for Comments (RFC)

The Status section also forces the architect and their boss or lead architect to agree criteria for who may approve. Three starting points:

  • Cost: software licences, extra hardware and the level of effort (estimated hours times the company's standard FTE rate, usually known to the project owner or manager).
  • Cross-team impact.
  • Security.

Document the limits (for example "costs exceeding $5,000 must be approved by the architecture review board") so all architects know when they may self-approve.

Context. The forces at play: "What situation is forcing me to make this decision?" Describe the circumstances and concisely list the alternatives; if each alternative needs detailed analysis, add an Alternatives section. The context also describes the architecture itself.

Example: "The Order service must pass information to the Payment service to pay for an order currently being placed. This could be done using REST or asynchronous messaging."

Decision. The decision and its full justification.

Nygard recommends an affirmative, commanding voice: "We will use asynchronous messaging between services", not "I think asynchronous messaging would be the best choice", which shows only opinion and leaves unclear whether a decision was made.

Understanding why a decision was made is far more important than understanding how something works, and makes agreement likelier.

  • Example:
    • you pick gRPC between two services to cut network latency for very high responsiveness needs
    • Years later a new architect switches to REST for consistency; not knowing why gRPC was chosen, they cause latency and upstream timeouts
    • An ADR would have shown the aim was latency (at the cost of tightly coupled services).

Consequences. The overall impact, good or bad, forcing the architect to weigh whether negatives outweigh benefits; also the place to document the trade-off analysis.

  • Example:
    • asynchronous fire-and-forget review posting improves responsiveness from 3,100 ms to 25 ms (users wait only for the message to be queued)
    • A developer objects to the complexity of error handling ("what if someone posts a review with some bad words?")
    • You had already discussed it with business stakeholders and other architects and chose responsiveness over simple error handling
    • An ADR would prevent the disagreement.

Compliance. Not standard, but highly recommended: how the decision is measured and governed, manually or by an automated fitness function, including how to write it and what code-base changes it needs.

  • Example (Figure 21-3):
    • in a traditional n-tiered layered architecture, all shared objects used by Business-layer objects must reside in the Shared Services layer
    • Tools: ArchUnit (Java), NetArchTest (C#).
@Test
public void shared_services_should_reside_in_services_layer()
{
    classes()
        .that().areAnnotatedWith(SharedService.class)
        .should().resideInAPackage("..services..")
        .check(myClasses);
}
  • This needs new stories to create the @SharedService annotation and add it to all shared classes.

Notes. Also not standard, but recommended: metadata even when ADRs live in version control: original author, approval date, approved by, superseded date, last modified date, modified by, last modification.

Example: GGG ADR 76

The Going, Going, Gone system has dozens of decisions, such as:

  • splitting the bidder and auctioneer UIs
  • a hybrid event-driven and microservices architecture
  • RTP (Real-time Transport Protocol) for video capture
  • a single API Gateway
  • separate queues for messaging

Document and justify every decision, however obvious. Figure 21-4 shows one: separate point-to-point queues between Bid Capture, Bid Streamer and Bidder Tracker, rather than one publish-and-subscribe topic (or REST).

The ADR in full:

ADR 76. Separate Queues for Bid Streamer and Bidder Tracker Services

STATUS
Accepted

CONTEXT
The Bid Capture service, upon receiving a bid, must forward that bid to the
Bid Streamer service and the Bidder Tracker service. This could be done using
a single topic (pub/sub), separate queues (point-to-point) for each service,
or REST via the Online Auction API layer.

DECISION
We will use separate queues for the Bid Streamer and Bidder Tracker services.

The Bid Capture service does not need any information from the Bid Streamer
service or Bidder Tracker service (communication is only one-way).

The Bid Streamer service must receive bids in the exact order they were
accepted by the Bid Capture service. Using messaging and queues automatically
guarantees the bid order for the stream by leveraging first-in, first out
(FIFO) queues.

Multiple bids come in for the same amount (for example, "Do I hear a
hundred?"). The Bid Streamer service only needs the first bid received for
that amount, whereas the Bidder Tracker needs all bids received. Using a
topic (pub/sub) would require the Bid Streamer to ignore bids that are the
same as the prior amount, forcing the Bid Streamer to store shared state
between instances.

The Bid Streamer service stores the bids for an item in an in-memory cache,
whereas the Bidder Tracker stores bids in a database. The Bidder Tracker will
therefore be slower and might require backpressure. Using a dedicated Bidder
Tracker queue provides this dedicated backpressure point.

CONSEQUENCES
We will require clustering and high availability of the message queues.

This decision will require the Bid Capture service to send the same
information to multiple queues.

Internal bid events will bypass security checks done in the API layer.
UPDATE: Upon review at the January 14, 2025, ARB meeting, the ARB decided
that this was an acceptable trade-off and that no additional security checks
are needed for bid events between these services.

COMPLIANCE
We will use periodic manual code reviews to ensure that asynchronous pub/sub
messaging is being used between the Bid Capture service, Bid Streamer
service, and Bidder Tracker service.

NOTES
Author: Subashini Nadella
Approved: ARB Meeting Members, 14 JAN 2025
Last Updated: 14 JAN 2025

Without the ADR, others could disagree and implement it differently.

21.4 Storing ADRs

Each decision gets its own file or wiki page. Keeping ADRs in the application's Git repository lets the team version them like code, but for larger organisations the authors caution against it:

  • Not everyone who needs the decision has access to that repository.
  • It is a poor place for ADRs whose context lies outside the application (integration, enterprise, or common decisions).

Prefer a dedicated ADR Git repository open to everyone, a wiki (with a template), or a shared directory on a file server rendered by a wiki or document tool.

Directory Contents
application Decisions specific to an application or product context, subdivided into common and one subdirectory per application (app1, app2)
common Decisions for all applications, e.g. "All framework-related classes will contain an annotation (@Framework in Java) or attribute ([Framework] in C#) identifying the class as belonging to the underlying framework code"
integration ADRs on communication between applications, systems or services
enterprise Global decisions impacting all systems, e.g. "All access to a system database will only be from the owning system", preventing databases being shared across systems

In a wiki, each directory is a navigational landing page and each ADR a single page. The names are recommendations: choose names that fit, consistently across teams.

21.5 ADRs as documentation, standards and legacy tools

  • As documentation.
    • No agreed standard exists for documenting architecture (diagramming standards are emerging: Simon Brown's C4 model, The Open Group ArchiMate)
    • ADRs help: Context describes the area and alternatives, Decision gives the reasons, by far the best form of architectural documentation, and Consequences gives the trade-off analysis (such as choosing performance over scalability).
  • For standards.
    • Few developers like standards, which are sometimes more about control than purpose. Context describes what forces the standard
    • Decision states what it is and why it must exist
    • Consequences records its implications. If an architect cannot justify it, perhaps it is not a good standard
    • the more developers understand why, the likelier they follow it.
  • For existing systems.
    • ADRs still help: they explain why a decision was made and whether it was right
    • Write ADRs for the most significant decisions and question them: services sharing one database, why? Should the data be split? The original decider may have left, so the architect investigates alternatives and trade-offs to validate or invalidate the decision
    • This builds justification and "brain trust" and can expose inefficiencies and incorrect design.

21.6 Generative AI and LLMs in architectural decisions

Can architects use generative AI to make or validate decisions (messaging, streaming or event sourcing for downstream data; single versus domain databases; Payment as one service or one per payment type)? Most architects know the answer: it depends. Everything is a trade-off, and there are no "best practices" for structural questions.

  • LLMs are largely probabilistic:
    • they give the most probable answer, the "best practice"
    • Probability and best practices have no place in architectural decisions, which need trade-off analysis in a specific business and technical context.
  • Translating business concerns into characteristics:
    • a business concerned with time to market values maintainability far above performance, which drives the decision
    • The translation (time to market to maintainability, testability, deployability) is not obvious and needs years of experience.
  • Example:
    • single Payment service versus one per payment type is a trade-off of maintainability versus performance (a single service performs better, several are more maintainable)
    • If time to market dominates, separate services are right.
  • Conclusion from the authors' recent experiments:
    • the best case is having the tool outline possible trade-offs to help spot missed ones
    • "While generative AI tools have plenty of knowledge, they lack the wisdom" to make the most appropriate decision.

Summary points

  • Decide at the last responsible moment, collaborate with teams, justify with business value, communicate with a link.
  • Architecturally significant decisions touch structure, characteristics, dependencies, interfaces or construction techniques.
  • Write ADRs in affirmative voice; the justification and consequences matter most; add Compliance and Notes.
  • Store ADRs where all can reach them, organised by application, integration and enterprise scope.
  • Use AI to surface trade-offs, never to decide.
Terms to carry forward
  • Covering Your Assets; Analysis Paralysis; last responsible moment.
  • Groundhog Day; Email-Driven Architecture; system of record.
  • Architecturally significant (Nygard).
