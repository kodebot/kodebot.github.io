Fundamentals of Software Architecture 22: Analyzing Architecture Risk

Source: Mark Richards and Neal Ford, Fundamentals of Software Architecture, 2nd edition (O'Reilly, 2025), chapter 22. Previous: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 21: Architectural Decisions. Next: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 23: Diagramming Architecture.

Takeaways
  • Every architecture has operational risks (availability, scalability, data integrity) and structural risks (static coupling). Analysing them lets architects fix deficiencies and structural decay.
  • Risk matrix: impact (1 to 3) times likelihood (1 to 3). 1 to 2 low (green), 3 to 4 medium (yellow), 6 to 9 high (red). Rate impact first; if likelihood is unsure, use 3.
  • Risk assessment: criteria (the critical architecture characteristics) by context (domains), with totals, filtering for signal, and direction from fitness functions.
  • Risk storming: collaborative, three phases (identification, consensus, mitigation). Unknown technology is automatically 9.
  • Case study: a nursing-hotline system gains two databases, SLAs, two queues, an outbreak cache and three API gateways.

22.1 Risk matrix

Assessing risk is subjective: one architect says high, another medium. The risk-assessment matrix makes it more measurable with two dimensions: overall impact and likelihood. Each is rated low (1), medium (2) or high (3) and the numbers multiplied.

Product Level Colour
1, 2 Low Green
3, 4 Medium Yellow
6, 9 High Red

Shading helps grayscale rendering and people who cannot distinguish colours.

Consider **impact first, likelihood second**. If unsure of the likelihood, use **high (3)** until you can confirm.

Worked example: worry about the availability of the primary central database. Impact if it is unavailable: high (3). Likelihood: low (1), because it runs on highly available servers in a cluster. High impact times low likelihood gives 3, medium risk.

22.2 Risk assessments

A risk assessment is a summarised report of an architecture's overall risk, using criteria chosen for a context (services, subdomains or domains). Architecture characteristics are excellent criteria: why analyse performance risk when scalability, elasticity and data integrity are the critical ones? Criteria run down the left, contexts across the top.

The characteristics most critical to the architecture make great risk-assessment criteria.

Example: an ecommerce ordering system with five criteria and four domain contexts. A domain or subdomain context works well; services are too fine-grained and miss risk in communication or coordination between services.

Security 6 3 1 1 11

(The order shipment total is the column sum; the printed figure hides it.)

  • By criteria: data integrity (17) is the highest risk; availability (10) the lowest.
  • By context: customer registration (24) is the highest-risk domain; order fulfillment (8) the lowest. This guides priorities and where to spend effort.
  • Filtering for signal.
    • When presenting to stakeholders, remove low and medium cells (the noise) to show high risks (the signal)
    • Figure 22-3 does this: scalability and data integrity for catalog checkout, performance for order shipment, security and data integrity for customer registration.

  • Direction. The table is a snapshot, not a trend. Use continuous measurement through fitness functions (Chapter 6) to spot trends. Figure 22-4 adds a third dimension:

    • an upright triangle: risk is getting worse (tip points to a higher number)
    • an upside-down triangle: risk is lessening
    • a circle: not moving

    Always include a key.

Reading it: data integrity is getting worse for catalog checkout, order fulfillment and order shipping (which could indicate a database issue), while security and availability are improving for customer registration and catalog checkout.

22.3 Risk storming

No architect can judge a system's risk alone: they may overlook an area, and few know every part. Risk storming is a collaborative exercise to determine architectural risk within one dimension (context or criterion). Include senior developers and tech leads as well as architects: they bring an implementation view and learn the architecture.

Three phases: identification (individual), consensus and mitigation (collaborative). All use a comprehensive or contextual architecture diagram (Chapter 23), sent to participants by the facilitator.

Example architecture (Figure 22-5): an Elastic Load Balancer forwards requests to EC2 instances holding web servers (Nginx) and application services; those call a MySQL database, a Redis cache and a MongoDB database (logging), and Push Expansion Servers, which also use all three stores. (Deliberately vague, to illustrate the process.)

Phase 1: identification

Each participant records an unbiased view without being swayed. Steps:

  1. The facilitator invites everyone: the diagram (or where to find it), the criteria and context to analyse, and date, time and place (physical or virtual).
  2. Participants analyse the risks individually with the matrix.
  3. They classify each risk as low (1 to 2), medium (3 to 4) or high (6 to 9) on green, yellow or red sticky notes.

Usually analyse one criterion or context ("Where are our security risks?"). If staff availability forces several dimensions in one context, write the criterion next to the number. For example three people see a high (6) central-database risk, one for availability and two for performance: discuss them separately.

Whenever possible restrict a session to a single criterion or context, so participants focus and the actual risk is clear.

Phase 2: consensus

Highly collaborative: post a big diagram on a wall (or screen); participants place their sticky notes. In Figure 22-6:

Component Notes
Elastic Load Balancer two medium (3), one high (6)
Push Expansion Servers one high (9)
MySQL three medium (3)
Redis cache one high (9)
MongoDB logging three low (2)

MySQL and MongoDB need no discussion (all agree). The rest need talking through:

  • Elastic Load Balancer:
    • Austen and Logan said 3
    • Addison said 6. Addison: if it goes down the whole system is inaccessible. True, so impact is high, but the others convince Addison that clustering makes likelihood low
    • the group settles on medium (3). It could have gone the other way if Addison had seen something the others missed: that is why collaboration matters.
  • Push Expansion Servers:
    • one participant rated 9 from bad experience with such servers crashing under similar loads
    • Without that participant no one would have seen the risk until well into production.
  • Redis cache: Devon, a developer, rated it 9; asked why, Devon says "What's a Redis cache?" A technology unknown to a participant is automatically high (9).
Always assign unproven or unknown technologies the highest rating (9): the matrix cannot be used for them.

Including developers is valuable: not knowing a technology is information about overall risk. The architect may change the technology or pay for training. The phase ends when everyone agrees (Figure 22-7).

Phase 3: risk mitigation

Mitigation usually means changing areas that might otherwise have been "perfect": from a completely changed architecture to a targeted refactoring such as adding a queue for backpressure at a throughput bottleneck. It usually costs more, so involve business stakeholders with authority to decide whether the cost outweighs the risk.

Example: the team finds the central database a medium (4) availability risk. Clustering it and splitting it into separate physical databases would cost $50,000; the owner says the price outweighs the risk.

The architect proposes instead splitting it into two domain-based databases for $16,000, still reducing the risk; the stakeholders agree. Risk storming shapes both the architecture and the negotiations with stakeholders, and with risk assessments tracks and improves it.

User-story risk analysis

Risk storming also works for user stories during grooming: impact if the story is not completed in the iteration, times likelihood it will not be. The team can then spot high-risk stories, track them closely and prioritise them.

22.4 Risk-storming use case: the nursing hotline

A call-center support system where nurses advise patients. Requirements:

  • A third-party diagnostics engine serves questions and guides nurses and patients; it handles about 500 requests a second.
  • Patients either call a nurse or use a self-service website that accesses the same engine directly.
  • Support 250 concurrent nurses nationwide and up to hundreds of thousands of concurrent self-service patients.
  • Nurses access medical records through a medical records exchange; patients cannot.
  • HIPAA compliance: only nurses may access medical records; the self-service option cannot guarantee HIPAA compliance.
  • High volume during cold, flu and COVID outbreaks.
  • Calls are routed by each nurse's skill profile (languages, specialisations).

The architect, Logan, designs the architecture of Figure 22-8: three web UIs (self-service, nurses, administrative staff maintaining nurse profiles and settings); a Call Accepter and a Call Router (routes to the next available nurse by skill profile, reading profiles from the central database); and a diagnostics-system API Gateway performing security checks and directing requests.

The four main services: Case Management, Nurse Profile Management, Medical Records Interface and the external Diagnostics Engine Interface. All communication is REST except proprietary protocols to the external systems and call-center services.

It must support availability, elasticity and security. After many reviews Logan holds a risk-storming exercise.

Availability

Findings (Figure 22-9):

Area Risk Reasoning
Central database High (6) High impact (3), medium likelihood (2)
Diagnostics Engine High (9) High impact (3), unknown likelihood (3)
Medical Records Interface Low (2) Not needed to determine a medical outcome

No other parts are availability risks, since each service has multiple instances and the API Gateway is clustered.

  • Database mitigation:
    • if it went down nurses could write case notes by hand, but the call router could not function
    • Split it into two databases: a clustered one for nurse profiles and a single-instance one for case notes
    • This also helps secure the case notes.
  • External systems
    • are harder (third-party controlled)
    • The team researches published SLAs (usually legally binding) or SLOs (usually not)
    • Both have SLAs: Diagnostics Engine 99.99% availability (about 52.60 minutes of downtime a year) and Medical Records Interface 99.90% (about 8.77 hours a year)
    • That was enough to remove the risk
    • Figure 22-10 shows the two databases and the SLAs on the diagram.

Elasticity

Only 250 nurses use the engine, but the self-service portal reaches it too, and flu and COVID outbreaks will raise load. Participants unanimously rate the Diagnostics Engine interface high (9): it handles only 500 requests a second, especially with REST.

  1. Use asynchronous queues between the API Gateway and the engine interface, a backpressure point. It is good practice but nurses and patients still wait and time out.
  2. Apply the Ambulance pattern: two message channels (one for nurses, one for self-service) so nurses' requests take priority. This does not fix wait times.
  3. Cache outbreak-related questions so they never reach the engine: a new Diagnostics Outbreak Cache Server handles requests for a particular outbreak or flu question, freeing capacity for other symptoms (Figure 22-11).

Without the session, this might not have been found until flu season.

Security

HIPAA means the Medical Records Interface must allow only nurses. Logan believes the API Gateway's authentication and authorisation checks neutralise the risk.

Participants all rate the gateway high security risk (6): high impact (3) if admin staff or self-service patients reach medical records, medium likelihood (2). All three user types share one gateway, so per-call checks are not enough. They convince Logan (who had said low, 2).

Everyone agrees on separate API Gateways for admin staff, self-service users and nurses, so non-nurse calls never reach the Medical Records Interface (Figure 22-12).

The architecture changed significantly from Figure 22-8 to Figure 22-12, in ways that address availability, elasticity and security.

22.5 Summary

Risk storming is not a one-time process. Repeat it through the system's lifecycle, to find and mitigate risk before production. How often depends on the frequency of change, refactoring efforts and incremental development; it is typical to run sessions on particular dimensions after adding a major feature or at the end of every iteration.

Summary points

  • Impact times likelihood makes risk measurable; unknowns rate 9.
  • Assess criteria (key characteristics) by domain context; filter for signal; track direction with fitness functions.
  • Risk storming: individual identification, collaborative consensus, mitigation negotiated with the people who pay.
  • Repeat it as the system evolves.
Terms to carry forward
  • Risk matrix; risk assessment; signal and noise; direction of risk.
  • Risk storming; facilitator; sticky notes; unknown technology = 9.
  • SLA versus SLO; backpressure; Ambulance pattern.
