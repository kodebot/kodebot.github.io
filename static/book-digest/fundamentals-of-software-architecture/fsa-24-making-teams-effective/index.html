Fundamentals of Software Architecture 24: Making Teams Effective

Source: Mark Richards and Neal Ford, Fundamentals of Software Architecture, 2nd edition (O'Reilly, 2025), chapter 24. Previous: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 23: Diagramming Architecture. Next: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 25: Negotiation and Leadership Skills.

Takeaways
  • Architects also lead the team that implements the architecture. A siloed architecture handed off to developers is rarely implemented correctly.
  • Replace the one-way hand-off with a bidirectional, collaborative relationship on the same virtual team.
  • The architect sets the "room" of constraints: too tight (the control-freak) or too loose (the armchair architect) hurts; the effective architect gets it right.
  • Elastic Leadership (Roy Osherove): five factors (team familiarity, size, experience, project complexity, project duration) scored on a 20-point scale set how involved to be.
  • Warning signs of an oversized team: process loss (Brooks's Law), pluralistic ignorance, diffusion of responsibility.
  • Use short checklists (code completion, testing, release) and design-principle guidance (e.g. special-purpose, general-purpose, framework libraries).

24.1 Collaboration

Software architects also lead the development team through implementing the architecture. The authors have seen too many create an architecture alone in a silo; when handed off, developers struggle to implement it correctly. Making teams productive is how successful architects differentiate themselves.

The industry often treats architecture and development as entirely separate. In the traditional model (Figure 24-1):

  • Architects: analyse business requirements to extract architecture characteristics, select patterns and styles, create logical components.
  • Developers: use those artifacts to create class diagrams for components, build UI screens, and write and test code.

The problem is the unidirectional arrow through the physical and virtual barriers between architect and developer: architects' decisions do not always reach the team, and changes the team makes rarely get back to the architect, so the architecture rarely achieves its goals.

The fix (Figure 24-2) is to break down the barriers and form a strong bidirectional collaborative relationship, on the same virtual team, which also lets the architect mentor and coach developers.

Unlike rigid waterfall, today's architectures change with nearly every iteration, so tight collaboration is essential.

24.2 Constraints and boundaries

Architects significantly influence a team's success or failure. Teams left out of the loop lack knowledge of system constraints and struggle. One role of the architect is to create and communicate the constraints developers implement within: a "room" in which the team works (Figure 24-3).

  • Too many constraints (room too small): no access to the tools, libraries and practices they need. Result: frustration, and developers leave for happier, healthier environments.
  • Too loose or none
    • (room too big): too many choices force the team to be the architect, making all the important decisions
    • Result: too many proofs of concept, struggles over design decisions, unproductive, confused and frustrated developers.

The effective architect provides the right guidance and constraints.

24.3 Architect personalities

Generalising wildly, three types: control-freak (tight boundaries), armchair (loose boundaries) and effective (appropriate boundaries).

The control-freak architect

Controls every detail; decisions are too fine-grained and low-level.

  • Examples:
    • restricting useful or necessary open source and third-party libraries, tight rules on naming conventions, class designs and method lengths, even writing pseudocode for developers, "stealing the art of programming"
    • Developers get frustrated and lose respect for the architect.
  • It is easy to become one, especially moving from developer to architect: the architect creates the logical components and their interactions
    • the developers' role is to decide how to implement them with class diagrams and design patterns, and new architects find that temptation hard to resist.
  • Example:
    • a Reference Manager component for static name-value data, product codes and warehouse codes
    • The architect's job is to identify the component, its core operations (GetData, SetData, ReloadCache, NotifyOnUpdate) and who interacts with it
    • A control freak prescribes a parallel loader pattern with an internal cache and a particular data structure: maybe effective, but not the only design and not the architect's job.
  • Sometimes architects must play control freak, depending on complexity and the team's skill level, but most of the time it disrupts the team and is ineffective leadership.

The armchair architect

Hasn't coded in a very long time (if ever), ignores implementation details, is disconnected from the team, and moves on to the next project after the initial diagrams.

  • Some are simply in way over their heads (technology or domain). Source code is hard to fake; architecture diagrams, lines and boxes, are easy to fake.
  • Example: a stock-trading system architecture with two boxes (the trading system and the trade compliance engine): nothing wrong, but too high-level to be useful.
  • Loose boundaries make the team do the architect's work; velocity suffers and everyone is confused
    • It is as easy to become one as a control freak: when you have no time (or choose no time) for the team, that is an indicator
    • Other indicators:
    • not fully understanding the business domain, problem or technology
    • not enough hands-on experience developing software
    • not considering the implications of an implementation (complexity, maintenance, testing)
  • Few intend it; it "just happens" when spread too thin across projects. Remedy: get more involved in the technologies and the business domain.

The effective architect

Creates appropriate constraints and boundaries, ensures the team works well together, gives the right guidance, makes sure the team has the right tools and technologies, and removes roadblocks. There is an art to it: close collaboration and the team's respect.

24.4 How much involvement?

This is Elastic Leadership, widely evangelised by author and consultant Roy Osherove; the authors adapt it to software-architecture leadership. Five key factors (also useful for deciding how many teams or projects you can manage):

Factor More involvement Less involvement
Team familiarity New team members: the architect facilitates collaboration and reduces cliques Members know each other well and self-organise
Team size More than 12 is big, 5 or fewer is small: the larger the team, the more architect involvement Small teams
Overall experience Many juniors: more involvement and mentoring (assess technology and business-domain experience separately if the domain is complex) More seniors: the architect is a facilitator more than a mentor
Project complexity Highly complex: architect available for issues Simple, straightforward
Project duration Longer projects (two years): developers relax, so the architect keeps things on schedule and ensures the hardest tasks come first Shorter (two months): the team already feels urgency, a control freak would just delay it

Project duration is the counterintuitive one: short projects need an armchair-style architect, long projects need involvement.

Scoring. Use a fixed scale of 20 points per factor (Figure 24-4): negative values mean less involvement (extreme: armchair), positive values mean more (extreme: control freak). Imprecise, but it helps.

Factor Scenario 1 Score Scenario 2 Score
Team familiarity New team members +20 Know each other well -20
Team size Small (4) -20 Large (12) +20
Overall experience All experienced -20 Mostly junior +20
Project complexity Relatively simple -20 High +20
Project duration 2 months -20 6 months -20
Accumulated score -60 (armchair) +20 (control freak)
  • Scenario 1 (-60): limit involvement in daily interactions, facilitate but stay out of the way, answer questions and keep the team on track; let the experienced team develop quickly.
  • Scenario 2 (+20): take a mentoring and coaching role and be fairly involved day to day, but not disruptive.
  • Use the factors at the start, then reanalyse continually, because involvement changes during the project. Factors are not equal (experience may count more), so weight or adjust them.

24.5 Team warning signs

What makes a team too large? Three factors.

Process loss. Coined by Fred Brooks in The Mythical Man-Month (Addison-Wesley, 1995); also called Brooks's Law: the more people you add, the more time the project takes. Group potential is the collective effort of everyone, but actual productivity is always below potential; the gap is process loss (Figure 24-7).

  • A sign: frequent merge conflicts when pushing code, meaning people work on the same code and get in each other's way.
  • Remedy:
    • look for parallelism, with members on separate services or areas. When a project manager proposes adding a member, look for parallel work streams
    • if there are none, warn that an addition could hurt.

Pluralistic ignorance. Everyone privately rejects a norm but publicly agrees, thinking they are missing something obvious.

  • Example:
    • most of a large team agrees on messaging between two remote services
    • one person thinks it silly because a secure firewall sits between them, but agrees publicly for fear of missing something obvious
      • The larger the group, the less willing people are to confront others
    • a smaller team might have chosen REST.
  • Made famous by Hans Christian Andersen's "The Emperor's New Clothes": con artists posing as tailors persuade the king the clothes are invisible to the unworthy
    • he and his subjects pretend to see them until a child says he is naked.
  • The architect watches faces and body language for masked scepticism and acts as facilitator, perhaps asking a sceptic directly and supporting them even if they are wrong, so everyone feels safe to speak.

Diffusion of responsibility. As teams grow, communication suffers; confusion about who owns what and dropped tasks signal a team that is too large. Figure 24-8: a motorist is stranded by a broken-down car. On a small country road, everyone passing may stop; on a busy city highway, thousands pass without stopping, each assuming someone else already called for help.

The effective architect guides implementation and ensures people are healthy, happy and working together.

24.6 Leveraging checklists

Airline pilots use checklists on every flight, however experienced: one missed setting (flaps at 10 degrees before takeoff) can separate a safe flight from disaster. In The Checklist Manifesto (Picador, 2011), Dr. Atul Gawande, alarmed by hospital staph infection rates, created surgical checklists: infection rates in hospitals using them fell to near zero while control hospitals' rates kept rising.

Software developers don't need checklists for everything; know when to use them.

  • Figure 24-9 is not a checklist but a procedure
    • for creating a database table (determine column names and types; fill out the request form; obtain permission; submit the form to the database group; verify the table once created)
    • Tasks depend on each other (you cannot verify before submitting), so it should not be a checklist
    • Procedural flows of dependent tasks and simple, familiar, error-free processes do not belong in checklists.
  • Good candidates: processes with no set order or dependencies, and those where people frequently skip steps or make errors.
  • Don't overdo it:
    • the Law of Diminishing Returns
    • the more checklists, the less they are used. Keep them as small as possible while capturing all necessary steps
    • developers won't follow overly long ones. Automate whatever can be automated and remove it from the list.
  • Don't worry about stating the obvious: the obvious is what gets missed.

[!note] The Hawthorne effect The hardest part is getting developers to use checklists (some run out of time and tick everything). Remedies: talk about the difference they make (or have the team read The Checklist Manifesto); explain the reason for each item; let the team decide collaboratively what goes on them (ownership). When all else fails: the Hawthorne effect: people who know they are observed tend to change behaviour, usually for the better, and a perception is enough (non-functioning cameras; monitoring software nobody reads). Tell the team all checklists will be verified; occasional spot-checks are enough.

Three most helpful checklists:

Checklist Purpose and contents
Developer code-completion Used when a developer says "done"; also gives a "definition of done". Includes coding and formatting standards not covered by tools, frequently overlooked items (absorbed exceptions), project-specific standards, special team instructions. Example items (Figure 24-10): run code cleanup and formatting; execute the custom source validation tool; verify the audit log is written for all updates; make sure there are no absorbed exceptions; check for hardcoded values and convert them to constants; verify only public methods call setFailure(); include @ServiceEntrypoint on the service API class. Review for automation: "only public methods call setFailure()" is a simple code-crawler check, whereas "@ServiceEntrypoint on the API class" may not be, so automating shortens the list and improves signal to noise
Unit and functional testing Perhaps the best one: unusual and edge cases developers forget. Typically the longest. Whenever QA finds an issue from a test case, add it. Items: special characters in text and numeric fields; minimum and maximum value ranges; unusual and extreme cases; missing fields. Automate what can be; it bridges developer and testing teams and frees testers for business scenarios
Software release Releasing is among the most error-prone points. The most volatile checklist: after every failed build or deployment, analyse the root cause and add an entry. Items: configuration changes on servers or external configuration servers; third-party libraries added (JAR, DLL); database updates and migration scripts

24.7 Providing guidance

Design principles also shape the "room". Example: the layered stack, the third-party libraries making up the application. Developers ask which libraries are OK, which not and when they may decide. Start by having them answer, for a proposed library:

  1. Overlap: does it overlap the system's existing functionality? (Otherwise large teams create lots of duplicate functionality.)
  2. Justification: both technical and business justification, which also makes developers aware of the need for business justifications.

[!example] The impact of business justifications One author led a complex Java project with a large team. A member obsessed with Scala became so disruptive that two key members said they would leave. The author asked them to hold on, then told the enthusiast he would support Scala if they provided a business justification for the training and rewriting costs. Delighted, they left the meeting thanking him. Next day, transformed, they returned humbly: all the technical reasons, but none had business value in cost, budget or timeline; the extra cost would bring no benefit, and they had been disrupting the team. They became one of the team's best, most helpful members. Being asked for a business justification raised their awareness of the business's needs, and the two key developers stayed.

A second way is a graphic of which decisions developers can make and which they cannot (Figure 24-11):

Category Examples Who decides
Special purpose Specific needs such as rendering PDFs or scanning barcodes, not worth custom software Developer decision, without consulting the architect
General purpose Wrappers over the language API: Apache Commons, Guava (Java) Developers analyse overlap, justify and recommend; architect approval
Framework Persistence (Hibernate), inversion of control (Spring): an entire layer or structure, highly invasive Architect's responsibility; developers shouldn't even analyse these

24.8 Summary

Making teams effective is hard work, needing experience, practice and strong people skills (next chapter). The techniques here (elastic leadership, checklists, communicated design principles) work.

Some say this belongs to the development or project manager; the authors strongly disagree: architects guide the team on technical matters and lead them through implementation, and a close relationship lets them observe team dynamics and make changes that improve productivity.

Summary points

  • One virtual team, two-way communication, never a hand-off.
  • Right-size the constraints; avoid control-freak and armchair extremes.
  • Score the five factors to set involvement; reassess continually.
  • Watch for process loss, pluralistic ignorance and diffusion of responsibility.
  • Short, automatable checklists for completion, testing and release; spot-check them.
  • Guide with explicit decision categories and require business justification.
Terms to carry forward
  • Control-freak, armchair and effective architect; constraints "room".
  • Elastic Leadership; the five factors.
  • Process loss (Brooks's Law); pluralistic ignorance; diffusion of responsibility.
  • Checklist Manifesto; Hawthorne effect; Law of Diminishing Returns.
  • Layered stack: special purpose, general purpose, framework.
