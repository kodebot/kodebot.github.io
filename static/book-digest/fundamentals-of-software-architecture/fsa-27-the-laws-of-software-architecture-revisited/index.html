Fundamentals of Software Architecture 27: The Laws of Software Architecture, Revisited

Source: Mark Richards and Neal Ford, Fundamentals of Software Architecture, 2nd edition (O'Reilly, 2025), chapter 27. Previous: Fundamentals of Software Architecture 26: Architectural Intersections. Next: Fundamentals of Software Architecture: Appendix, Discussion Questions.

Takeaways
  • The three laws (Chapter 1): everything is a trade-off; why is more important than how; most decisions lie on a spectrum between extremes. The authors hoped to find many universal laws, found two in the first edition and a third in the second.
  • First Law: the job is trade-off analysis, not finding silver bullets. Be an objective arbiter, not an evangelist (yesterday's best practice becomes tomorrow's antipattern).
  • Worked analyses: shared library versus shared service, and queue versus topic.
  • Corollary 1: a decision with no trade-offs just has hidden ones (code reuse requires abstraction and low volatility). Corollary 2: you can't do it just once.
  • Second Law: record the why in ADRs; beware the Out of Context antipattern (unweighted criteria).
  • Third Law: an architecture decision is one where every option has significant trade-offs.
  • Practise with katas; there are no right answers, only trade-offs.

27.1 The three laws

  1. Everything in software architecture is a trade-off.
  2. Why is more important than how.
  3. Most architecture decisions aren't binary but rather exist on a spectrum between extremes.

Almost every example in the book illustrates them. The authors hoped to find many universal things and codify them as laws; they identified two in the first edition and one more while writing the second.

27.2 First Law: everything in software architecture is a trade-off

Many think the architect's job is to find silver bullets and become a hero; that rarely happens (architects rarely get credit for good decisions but always get blamed for bad ones). The real job is analysing trade-offs. Cultivate a reputation as an objective arbiter of trade-offs, not an evangelist, for two reasons:

  • Evangelism is dangerous long-term:
    • yesterday's best practice tends to become tomorrow's antipattern
    • Decisions rest on current factors and incomplete knowledge, and the ecosystem's constant churn eventually weakens or invalidates them
      • social capital invested in evangelising a solution damages your reputation when it must change
    • Stay clear-eyed and objective, and don't attach your credibility to a decision that didn't age well.
  • Decision makers want sober objectivity, not enthusiastic advocacy. The go-to person for objective trade-off analysis is a valuable asset when decisions are critical.

Shared library versus shared service

For shared behaviour in distributed architectures (microservices, EDA): compile a shared library into each service at build time, or call a shared service at runtime (Figure 27-1)?

"It depends." On what? You must list the contextualised trade-offs, specific to the organisation and solution (its landscape, team capabilities, budgets). Factors for this one:

Factor Why it matters
Heterogeneous code A service is easier: callers use it over the network regardless of platform. A library needs a version per technology stack, kept in sync, adding complexity
High code volatility (churn) A service exposes new functionality as soon as it is deployed; a library change requires recompiling and redeploying every service
Ability to version changes Library is easier: version differences resolve at compile time. A service resolves versions at runtime, complicating interaction
Overall change risk Library is better: once compiled into the service you can be confident it works. A service can change without compile-time verification, raising runtime-fault risk
Performance Library: in-process calls versus slower network calls (latency)
Fault tolerance Library: runtime service access suffers network issues (Chapter 9); a compiled, tested, deployed library is stable
Scalability Library: service calls suffer latency, which diminishes scalability

The library wins on count for these factors in this context, though more weighting may be needed (second corollary): you and the team now understand the forces at play.

Queue versus topic

Your team builds a distributed architecture that sends trade information to Notification and Analytics services. Queue or topic? (The book's text at one point names the second consumer Reporting; the figures use Analytics.)

  • Queues (point to point, Figure 27-2):
    • the publisher knows who receives
    • to reach several consumers it sends one message to a separate queue per consumer. Different consumers can get different information
    • the Trading service knows every recipient, making it harder for a rogue service to listen in (good when security is high priority)
    • queues are independent, so monitor and scale them separately. But Trading is tightly coupled to its consumers: adding Compliance means reworking Trading to send to a third queue.
  • Topics (broadcast, Figure 27-3):
    • Trading posts one message; every subscriber receives it
    • The publisher doesn't know or care who consumes, and new consumers can be added any time with no change to others or the producer
    • Downsides: every consumer gets the same message (possible stamp coupling, Figure 9-9), and since everyone reads the entire message there are security concerns.

(The source table also lists "good support for extensibility and evolvability" under the queue column, which reads as a layout slip.) Then return to the organisational goals: if security matters more, choose queues; if the organisation is growing fast and other services want trades, extensibility may win and point to topics.

First Corollary: missing trade-offs

If you think you've discovered something that isn't a trade-off, more likely you just haven't identified the trade-off...yet.

  • Code reuse
    • looks purely beneficial (less code, less duplication)
    • Two factors decide how effective it is, and architects find only the first: abstraction (usable from multiple call points) and low volatility
    • Reusing code that always changes creates churn across the system: callers must coordinate around each change and verify even non-breaking ones, so teams chase breaking changes (a key lesson from orchestration-driven SOA, Chapter 17: "swimming through quicksand").
  • The hidden trade-off:
    • effective reuse needs good abstraction and low volatility. So the best reuse targets are plumbing (frameworks, libraries, platforms)
    • the domain changes fastest, so domain concepts are terrible reuse candidates (the DDD bounded-context principle: no context reuses another's implementation details).
Why we can't have nice things: trade-offs!

Second Corollary: you can't do it just once

You cannot decide once and for all to use choreography for every workflow. Dozens or hundreds of variables (complexity, team experience, budget, team topology, schedule pressure) feed each decision, and subtle differences push the answer either way.

Sweeping, semipermanent decisions rest on assumptions that may not hold later. Treat it as job security: the real job is trade-off analysis, not permanent, perfect decisions.

27.3 Second Law: why is more important than how

Architects can look at a system and say how it works, but it often isn't clear why the previous architect chose one option over another, because the decision criteria weren't recorded.

Hence architecture diagrams plus ADRs (Chapter 21): every trade-off analysis generates a great deal of context that doesn't appear in the solution.

Document the analysis, with the known compromises and limitations, so future architects (who might be you) don't redo the analysis just to understand.

Out of Context antipattern

The architect understands the trade-offs but not how to weight them for the current context. In the library-versus-service analysis the library "won" on count, but do all criteria weigh equally?

Suppose a team has code on several platforms and doesn't care much about performance or scale, only a clean way to manage shared behaviour: the first two criteria dominate, and the shared service wins (and the team already knows what to mitigate).

Experience builds the criteria, but weighting finds the fit; generic trade-off analysis isn't very useful until applied in a specific context.

27.4 Third Law: the spectrum between extremes

Most architecture decisions aren't binary but rather exist on a spectrum between extremes.

Clean binary decisions would be nice, but software architecture doesn't live there. It is why comprehensive definitions are hard: architecture versus design, orchestration versus choreography, topics versus queues. The criteria lie along a messy spectrum. Following Chapter 1's spectrum between architecture and design, the law gives a way to recognise architectural decisions:

A software architecture decision is one where each of the options has significant trade-offs.

If everything is a trade-off, an architectural decision has trade-offs for every option. Don't reduce everything to a binary; "it depends" means it depends on where on the spectrum the criterion falls. Architects decide in a swamp of uncertainty, often on incomplete information, and even with full information the choice may sit between two extremes. "Welcome to software architecture!"

27.5 Parting words of advice

  • Fred Brooks: "How do we get great designers? Great designers design, of course."
  • Ted Neward: "So how are we supposed to get great architects, if they only get the chance to architect fewer than a half-dozen times in their career?"

Practice is the proven way to build skill. Keep honing the craft and widen your technology breadth. The authors made architecture katas (on the companion website) modelled on the book's examples. Is there an answer guide?

No. Neal Ford: "There are not right or wrong answers in architecture, only trade-offs." When the authors first used katas in live training they kept students' drawings to build an answer repository, then gave up: the drawings captured how (topologies) but not why, as teams explained trade-offs in class but had no time to write ADRs.

Keeping just the how meant only half the story.

Parting advice: always learn, always practice, and go do some architecture!

Summary points

  • Everything is a trade-off; be an objective arbiter; list the context-specific factors and weight them.
  • Hidden trade-offs exist: reuse needs abstraction and low volatility, and reuse means coupling.
  • Redo the analysis each time; record the why.
  • Decisions are spectrums; an architectural decision has significant trade-offs in every option.
  • Practise on katas; there are no right answers.
Terms to carry forward
