Grokking Software Architecture 2: The Architect's Toolkit (a guide to defensible decisions)

Source: Matt Erman, Grokking Software Architecture (Manning, MEAP V07), ch. 2. Previous: Grokking Software Architecture 1: The Architect's Compass (it's all about tradeoffs). Next: Grokking Software Architecture 3: The Principles of Sound Structure.

Takeaways
  • Toolkit for the 5-step process: -ilities + constraints (Inquiry), Tradeoff Triangle and weighted decision model (Options), ADR (Decision).
  • No perfect architecture; every choice has a price (time, money, energy, flexibility). Write down what you optimise for and what you explicitly do not.
  • The architect is Explainer-in-Chief, Decision Scribe, Boundary Keeper, Risk Scout.
  • CAP theorem: during a network partition you must pick Consistency or Availability.
  • Day-one skills: ask, do not guess; state your box (constraints); read the history (ADRs).

2.2 The myth of "perfect" architecture

Chasing it means never shipping. Experienced engineers pick the option whose downsides they can live with today, knowing every shortcut is a technical-debt loan repaid with interest.

  • Prove a decision was right with metrics:
    • maintenance time saved, falling bug rates, shorter onboarding
    • E.g
    • critical incidents 5 per month to 1
      • time to deliver a feature 2 weeks to 3 days (see Software Architecture Metrics, Ciceri, Ford et al.: https://www.amazon.com/Software-Architecture-Metrics-Studies-Improve/dp/1098112237).
  • Red flags of "perfect" thinking:
    • Abstraction Fever: "add another layer, just in case" (harder to debug for a problem you do not have).
    • Future-Proof Fallacy: "support any database imaginable" (complexity now for theoretical flexibility, and no database supported well).
    • "Over the Rainbow" Syndrome: "optimise performance later" (no plan, no quality goals defined).
  • Habit: state "for this feature we optimise for speed to market; we are intentionally not optimising for sub-50 ms latency."

2.3 The architect's four roles

  • Explainer-in-Chief:
    • explain the why constantly (why this database, pattern, not the others)
    • Architecture is 50% communication; the best technical idea does not auto-win, the most persuasively communicated does (you campaign like a politician)
    • A precise shared vocabulary ("Adapter pattern", "Idempotency", "Circuit Breaker") ends weeks of design debate
    • Exercise: explain the Adapter pattern to a non-technical person ("a universal plug connecting an American laptop to a European outlet").
  • Decision Scribe: prevent "decision amnesia" (forgetting why six months ago) with Architecture Decision Records (see 2.7), including why you rejected alternatives.
  • Boundary Keeper:
    • enforce Separation of Concerns: no business logic leaking into UI code, database queries not taking over the system (like the Matrix agents)
    • In modern teams you are more a Technical Advisor helping teams own their boundaries by showing the cost of logic leaks
    • Result: maintainable, testable, changes do not cause unexpected breaks.
  • Risk Scout: find scary unknowns early ("can this library handle our scale? is this cloud service right?") with small experiments called spikes (chapter 15).

2.4 The "-ilities": your vocabulary of quality

Each quality has a price: 99.999% ("five nines") availability is exponentially costlier than 99.9%; sub-50 ms performance needs far more investment than 800 ms. Ask the business: "which matter most and how much are we willing to pay?"

Quality The question
Reliability right thing even when things go wrong, working under pressure?
Maintainability how easy to find a bug, change, understand ("don't make me think")?
Scalability if users 10x overnight, scale gracefully or die?
Performance latency (response time) and throughput (work per unit time)
Security protect data, prevent unauthorised access, sanitise and validate inputs?
Testability how easily can you prove it works?
Observability can you tell what is happening inside from outputs (logs, metrics, traces)?

Extended family: Portability (e.g. AWS to Azure), Interoperability, Usability (no 350-page manual), Availability (often a percentage like five nines), Resilience (bounces back when a service fails).

Never guess priorities; get them from the business:

  • New feature, you must ASK ("optimise Performance but low Maintainability and longer, or Speed to Ship but slow: which for V1?").
  • Implicit task (e.g. a "site crashes under load" bug), you must STATE to confirm ("this improves Reliability but is complex: 2 days and delays the other feature; top priority?").
  • Exercise: per feature pick two non-negotiable -ilities, one you deliberately do not optimise, and state the consequence (e.g. recommendation engine: prioritise Performance and Scalability, de-prioritise Maintainability for V1, revisit after launch).

Maintainability in action (JavaScript)

Before: one processOrder(cartItems) summing prices, applying a hardcoded 10% discount (* 0.10) and 8% tax (* 0.08): three jobs, magic numbers.

After: constants DISCOUNT_RATE = 0.10, TAX_RATE = 0.08 and small functions calculateSubtotal (reduce), applyDiscount(amount, rate) (amount * (1 - rate)), addTax(amount, rate) (amount * (1 + rate)), composed in processOrder.

Smell test: if describing a function needs the word "and", it is hard to maintain (the "S" in SOLID, ch. 3). Still hardcoded, so a rate change needs a deployment; a better design stores it in a database.

Testability in action: Dependency Injection

  • Before:
    • ReportGeneratorBefore constructs new DatabaseConnection("live_connection_string") inside its constructor, so it is tightly coupled (components so interdependent that a change ripples; like pistons and crankshaft of an engine)
    • A unit test cannot control the data: the real connection returns 2 rows, the test expects 3, so it fails.
  • After:

    • Dependency Injection, an object receives its dependencies instead of creating them:

      constructor(dbConnection) {
  this.dbConnection = dbConnection;
}

    • The test injects fakeDb = { getData: (query) => ["row1","row2","row3"] } and passes

    • The class now depends on an abstraction (any object with getData).

  • Table:
    • coupling tight to loose
    • testability low (needs a real DB, slow, brittle) to high (fake data, fast, reliable)
    • flexibility low to high (swap dbConnection for any matching object).

Performance in action: caching

A cache is a high-speed temporary store (RAM: Redis, Memcached) serving a subset of data far faster than the database. Before: getDashboardSummary(userId) runs getProfile, getOrders, getActivity at ~500 ms each = 1500 ms; 100 simultaneous users = 300 slow queries.

After: check cache.get("dashboard:"+userId) (~5 ms); on a miss run the three queries, cache.set(key, data, CACHE_TTL_SECONDS = 600) (10 min). First load 1505 ms, all others 5 ms (about 300x faster).

  • Cost:
    • more complexity (a new component to deploy and monitor) and weaker consistency (a new order may not show for up to 10 minutes)
    • Make it visible: "is a 10-minute delay acceptable for 300x faster?" (usually yes for a dashboard).
  • The book uses Scaffolding: concepts introduced here ("what and why") return in ch. 3 ("how").

2.5 Constraints: the box

Constraints are the walls of the box; ignoring them gives designs that cannot be built.

  • Time
    • (real deadline, what if late?)
    • People (team, skills, learning curve)
    • Money (cloud, licences, team time)
    • Technology (existing systems, mandated cloud or language)
    • Risk (appetite: life-support system vs a social app for pet pictures).
  • Vocabulary:
    • -ilities = NFRs = CFRs (the qualities wanted, "what good looks like")
    • constraints (the limits)
    • architecture = choose a design whose most-valued qualities fit safely inside the limits you have.
  • "Good enough for now" example (Node.js/Express):
    • export user data as CSV by tomorrow (audit)
    • The dream is a background worker, message queue and notification service (a week of work)
    • The optimal solution generates the CSV directly in the request: app.get('/export-user-data', ...) fetches db.fetchUserData(userId), 404 if missing, builds id,name,email headers and a row, sets Content-Type: text/csv and Content-Disposition: attachment; filename="user_data_<id>.csv"
    • Flaws (not scalable; times out on large data) but it meets the need in hours
    • Dialling down -ilities is a privilege of the industry: in finance or healthcare, Security, Reliability and Data Integrity are permanent, non-negotiable constraints
    • A great ADR candidate.
  • Pro move: "say the quiet part out loud": "team of three juniors, tiny budget, six weeks" eliminates whole classes of options. Constraints are a focusing force, not the enemy.

2.6 Case study: the 35-headed Hydra

The book's anchor case: rebuilding a large enterprise app, a commercial credit scorecard for a large regional bank (used to decide lending of millions). 35 scorecards (hotels, construction, auto dealers, retail...) with unique, overlapping rules; built 20 years earlier; one shared-rule change meant hunting through dozens of files.

  • Risk: unsupported third-party component libraries with unpatchable security vulnerabilities affecting nearly every UI page; unacceptable for a low-risk-appetite bank.
  • Technology: dead-end stack, no easy modern replacements.
  • Time: a "simple" one-line change took 1 to 2 days; a new scorecard was a multi-week project.
  • People: team split between firefighting the old system and building the new; constant context switching.
  • Money: huge Total Cost of Ownership (TCO): lifetime cost including maintenance, hosting, support and opportunity cost (features not built).
  • Had the original teams mapped constraints and documented tradeoffs in ADRs they would have seen the warning signs years earlier.

2.7 Architecture Decision Records (ADRs)

A defensible decision is one you can explain in two minutes to a non-technical person and that still makes sense in six months. ADR concept popularised by Michael Nygard (https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions); repository of resources: https://github.com/joelparkerhenderson/architecture-decision-record.

  • ADR vs design doc (RFC, tech spec):
    • a design doc is a broad, heavily debated blueprint of a feature, often obsolete soon
    • an ADR covers one significant decision, is short, and lives forever in the repo (compared again in ch. 15).
  • When to write one, the S.O.S. test (any "yes"):
    • Significant (changes how other parts work: swap a library, change the database)
    • Onerous to change (nightmare to rewrite in six months)
    • Surprising (nonstandard; a future dev asks "why?"). Otherwise a code comment. (Fowler: architecture is "the important stuff... whatever that is".)
  • The 8 parts:
    • Metadata (date, status such as Accepted or Rejected, participants)
    • Context (problem and forces)
    • Options considered (2-3 realistic)
    • Criteria (which -ilities and constraints drive it)
    • Tradeoffs (gain and price of each)
    • Decision (choice and why, linked to criteria)
    • Risks and mitigations
    • Review date or trigger
      • ("after launch", "above 10,000 users")
      • Popular formal template: MADR (https://github.com/adr/madr/blob/develop/template/adr-template.md)
      • Consistency beats the exact template
  • Real example, ADR-001 "Choose Session Storage for Web App"
    • (users had to log in on every screen): date Nov 2, 2023, Accepted, participants S
      • Smith (Lead), A
      • Jones, P
      • Lee
      • Options: in-memory (fastest and simplest, but lost on restart and not shared across servers)
      • Redis (very low latency, shared, but a new service to run)
      • database table
        • (durable and simple ops, but higher latency and extra load on the primary DB)
        • Criteria: availability, performance (latency), deployment simplicity, cost
        • Decision: Redis (prioritising high availability and low latency)
        • Risk: Redis outage logs out all users
    • mitigation: configure Redis for high availability and sensible TTL session timeouts
      • Review: after multi-region deployment or above 10,000 concurrent sessions
      • File naming docs/adr/001-session-storage.md (sequential number to reference "see ADR-001", slug to find it)
    • keep ADRs in the repo so structural history sits beside the code.
  • Why ADRs save money:
    • faster onboarding (hours instead of days of a senior's time)
    • no decision churn
    • prevents knowledge drain when people leave
    • cheaper mistakes (a one-page comparison vs three weeks building the wrong thing).
  • Best practices:
    • keep them lightweight
    • central and accessible (wiki or docs/adr)
    • only significant decisions (too many dilute value). Introduce by writing one for your next task and linking it in your PR.
  • AI help:
    • feed meeting notes to an assistant for an 8-part draft, or point it at docs/adr/ to query past rules
    • but a human must verify facts, own tradeoffs, sign
    • beware documentation fatigue.
  • Lifecycle from document to code:
    • Ticket links the ADR (e.g. "must comply with docs/adr/004-stripe-integration.md")
    • Code respects it
    • Fitness functions (automated architecture tests, e.g. ArchUnit, NDepend) fail the build if "UI cannot talk to the Database" is violated (https://www.thoughtworks.com/insights/articles/fitness-function-driven-development; more in ch. 4)
    • Pull Request is the enforcer (architectural compliance review). "The ADR is the law, the ticket is the assignment, the PR is the police officer."

2.8 The Tradeoff Triangle

Make priorities visible in 30 seconds: draw a triangle, label corners with three competing priorities (classic: Speed to Ship, Cost to Operate, Ease to Change), put a dot where today's decision lands, explain it aloud ("V1: dot close to Speed to Ship; OK with higher operating cost for a quarter and less ease of change until feedback").

Corners can differ per decision (Security, User Experience, Performance). Use it to turn a vague argument into a priority check.

  • NFRs / CFRs (non-/cross-functional requirements):
    • how well a system performs, not what it does (a car's function is to drive; NFRs are top speed, safety rating, fuel efficiency)
    • Some teams say "cross-functional" so the business does not de-prioritise them
    • Day-one trio: Reliability, Security, Scalability (the foundation).

2.8.1 Weighted Decision Model

Turns "feels" into a scored grid: list options (2-3); define criteria; assign weights summing to 1.0; score 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent), multiply by weight and sum. Session storage with Availability = 0.6, Performance = 0.3, Simplicity = 0.1:

Criterion Weight In-memory Redis Database
Availability 0.6 1 (0.6) 5 (3.0) 4 (2.4)
Performance 0.3 5 (1.5) 4 (1.2) 2 (0.6)
Simplicity 0.1 5 (0.5) 3 (0.3) 4 (0.4)
Total 1.0 2.60 4.50 (winner) 3.40

If priorities shift to Performance the winner may flip. Book repo script: https://github.com/realworldbooks/Grokking_Software_Architecture (Chapter 02, Weighted_Decision_Model). Benefits: forces explicit criteria, shows the effect of changing priorities, yields a one-line rationale for the ADR.

Downside: ratings are subjective (one engineer's 5 is another's 3); the real value is the conversation about priorities.

2.9 The Dork Side: CAP theorem

Ticketing system, servers in New York and London syncing the remaining ticket count; a shark bites the transatlantic cable = a partition (a communication break dividing nodes into groups that cannot talk). A New York customer buys a ticket:

  • Choose Consistency: refuse until London confirms: data stays perfectly consistent but the user sees a spinner (Availability sacrificed).
  • Choose Availability: sell it; but if a London user bought the last ticket one second earlier you sold it twice (consistency sacrificed).
  • CAP theorem:
    • formulated by Eric Brewer, unveiled in 2000 at the PODC symposium keynote "Towards Robust Distributed Systems" (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221343719_Towards_robust_distributed_systems)
    • formalised by Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch in 2002. A distributed system can provide only two of three:
    • Consistency: all users share a common view; new writes are seen by all later requests.
    • Availability: always responds without error, possibly with slightly stale data.
    • Partition tolerance: keeps operating though servers split into groups that cannot communicate.
  • It is a specific Tradeoff Triangle
    • Over a network partitions are inevitable, so P is a requirement and the real choice is C vs A
    • Exception:
      • within one highly reliable network (e.g. a single AWS region) architects can pick CA
      • across the internet or between data centres assume partitions and choose CP or AP
    • In microservices, different services can choose differently (e.g. PaymentService CP, ShoppingCartService AP; ch. 12).
  • What engineers need:
    • recognise the tradeoff, ask "are we okay with slightly stale data for better availability?" Bank transfer leans to Consistency (delay beats a wrong balance)
    • social feed leans to Availability (stale post beats an error page).
  • Friendster (ch. 1): chose C at all costs, sacrificed A (40-second loads); Facebook and others chose A: a billion-dollar decision.

2.10 Mission, checklist, recap

  • Day-one three: Ask, do not guess (which -ilities matter); State your box (constraints); Read the history (where ADRs live).
  • Mission:
    • name a small decision (e.g. default cache timeout, API retries)
    • pick two -ilities and one to sacrifice
    • sketch the Speed/Cost/Change triangle
    • compare 2-3 pros and cons per option
    • complete the ADR template and commit it (e.g. docs/adr/0001-cache-ttl-policy.md)
    • post a one-liner ("decided on a 5-minute cache TTL for user profiles, optimising Performance, accepting slight staleness")
      • Win condition: a teammate understands your choice in under two minutes.
  • Recap:
    • no perfect architecture
    • -ilities are the vocabulary and criteria
    • constraints are focusing forces
    • the Triangle exposes competing priorities
    • ADRs record significant choices
    • CAP forces a C vs A tradeoff
    • the architect is Explainer-in-Chief, Decision Scribe, Boundary Keeper, Risk Scout. Mapping to the 5 steps: -ilities and constraints for Inquiry, Triangle for Options, ADR for Decision.
