HBR Managing Across Cultures 2: Managing Multicultural Teams

Source: Jeanne Brett, Kristin Behfar and Mary C. Kern, "Managing Multicultural Teams", Harvard Business Review, November 2006 (reprint R0611D), reprinted in HBR's 10 Must Reads on Managing Across Cultures (Harvard Business Review Press, 2016). Own-words summary, much shorter than the source.

Takeaways
  • Four cultural barriers cause conflict on multicultural teams: direct versus indirect communication, accents and fluency, attitudes to hierarchy, and decision-making norms.
  • Four ways to respond: adaptation (ideal), structural intervention, managerial intervention and exit.
  • Direct intervention by the manager often backfires: it sidelines members and teaches the team to wait for orders. Adaptation lets the team solve and learn from the problem itself.
  • Identify the cause as culture, not personality; then fit the response to the team's circumstances.

2.1 Why intervention can backfire

  • A software team of Indians and Americans could not agree a delivery date (Americans: two to three weeks; Indians: two to three months), and the Indian members were reluctant to report setbacks, so Americans found out only when work arrived. The project manager stepped in
    • both sides came to rely on him for minute operational details, the schedule collapsed and the team never learned to work together.
  • Their research (interviews with managers and members worldwide, plus work on dispute resolution and teamwork): "the wrong kind of managerial intervention may sideline valuable members ..
    • or, worse, create resistance"
    • The goal is to intervene so the team gets back on track and can handle future challenges itself.

2.2 Four barriers

  • Direct versus indirect communication.
    • Western communication is typically direct and explicit
    • elsewhere meaning is embedded in how the message is presented. In negotiation, Westerners ask "Do you prefer option A or B?"
    • others infer preferences from changes, or the lack of them, in a proposal. The non-Westerner understands the Westerner more easily than the reverse.
    • An American manager on a US and Japanese customer-data project: "I thought they were saying yes but they just meant 'I'm listening to you.'" When she emailed flaws directly to her American boss and the Japanese team, her boss appreciated it but the Japanese colleagues were embarrassed and gave her less access
      • they put her office in a storage room
      • The project launched problem-free, but she had limited her ability to find other problems
      • Asking indirectly ("what would happen if this part failed?") would have worked better.
  • Accents and fluency.
    • The language of business is English, but accents, limited fluency and translation problems can cause misunderstanding and shape perceived status and competence
    • A Latin American consultant felt the American partner led interviews though they were equals and she had "very good questions"
    • An American said a teammate dismissed Japanese consultants because they were less fluent, risking overestimating opportunities
    • Non-fluent members may be the most expert yet unable to convey it, and may lose motivation or fear evaluations
    • Language can also relieve tension: when Koreans caucused in Korean during English negotiations, US and Latin American buyers pretended to caucus in Spanish (about sport and weather), and both sides cut back sidebar talks.
  • Hierarchy and authority.
    • Flat teams make hierarchical-culture members uncomfortable
    • deference helps if most of the team shares it and hurts if most are egalitarian
      • A Mexican-heritage bank manager, taught to be humble and phrase things as questions, was seen by Americans as wavering
      • In a Korean-US due diligence, Americans complained to Korean senior management over their counterparts' heads, offending the seniors and mortifying the juniors
    • high-level US managers had to visit Korea to repair it.
  • Decision-making norms.
    • US managers decide quickly with relatively little analysis
      • A Brazilian negotiator reported Koreans reopening points one to three on day two ("my boss almost had an attack")
      • Remedies: minor concessions on process
    • Americans keep impatient bosses away from meetings and give frequent brief updates
    • others say explicitly what they need ("We have to see the big picture before we talk details").

2.3 Four strategies

  • Adaptation
    • works when members acknowledge and name cultural differences and take responsibility for living with them; it needs less manager time and members learn
    • An American engineer in Ireland, shocked by Israeli colleagues' argumentative style, imposed some structure to stay prepared and noticed they argued with each other too and worked well: "the confrontation was not personal but cultural"
    • A US and UK team used fusion: not quite as fast as the Americans, not quite as thorough as the British.
  • Structural intervention
    • (reorganisation or reassignment) helps with obvious subgroups or defensive, stereotyping members
    • A leader of a dispersed research team (continental Europe, UK, US) met face to face twice a year to agree values, noticed everyone "shut down" when he spoke, and hired a consultant to run meetings
    • A retail-expansion team leader split the group into mixed subgroups (changing membership each time), because Japanese women consultants would not speak in a large group or before their male superior
      • subgrouping needs someone to mediate and reassemble the pieces.
  • Managerial intervention
    • (a manager deciding without the team) teaches little, but works to set norms early or to escalate with respect for both hierarchies: an American refinery-safety expert, stuck in China, used her Beijing office's senior managers to reach the Chinese refinery's superiors
    • A software manager told staff with pronounced accents they were chosen for task expertise, not fluency, and to tell customers: "I realize I have an accent
    • If you don't understand what I'm saying, just stop me and ask questions."
  • Exit
    • is a last resort (voluntary or by request) when emotions are high and "too much face" is lost
    • a Greek and a Polish senior consultant clashed over how to approach problems and she left.
  • Choose by situation:
    • does the project allow flexibility, are resources available, is the team permanent or temporary, can the manager change the team? Treat problems as culture, not personality
    • managers who intervene at a stalemate rarely empower the team for the next one.
  • Teams solving it themselves:
    • a call-centre team (North and Latin American Spanish speakers) confronted a colleague whose calls took twice as long because she chatted (normal in her culture)
    • she admitted she did not know how to end calls politely, and teammates broke into her overlong calls offering to take over
      • An Indian manager with a Singapore teammate, unsure that Japanese colleagues who said yes would deliver, rejected going over their heads, and instead took an eBusiness road show to the whole Japanese IT team with success stories
    • the team asked to feature in the next one.

2.4 Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the article)

  • Name your own style up front to your team: "I speak plainly; I'd rather say a problem early than hide it" (adaptation works when differences are named).
  • Use the accent line. The article gives a tested sentence: "I realize I have an accent. If you don't understand, just stop me and ask questions." It turns a worry into a norm.
  • Ask, don't escalate, when a British colleague's "interesting idea" may mean no: ask "what would need to change for you to support this?" (a version of the article's indirect questioning).
  • Frame decisions in terms of what you need ("I need the main risk before details").
Terms to carry forward
  • Four barriers; four strategies (adaptation, structural, managerial, exit).
  • Fusion; enabling situational conditions.
