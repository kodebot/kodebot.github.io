HBR Managing Across Cultures 3: L'Oréal Masters Multiculturalism

Source: Hae-Jung Hong and Yves L. Doz, "L'Oréal Masters Multiculturalism", Harvard Business Review, June 2013 (reprint R1306J), reprinted in HBR's 10 Must Reads on Managing Across Cultures (Harvard Business Review Press, 2016). Own-words summary, much shorter than the source.

Takeaways
  • Every global business has an unresolved tension between scale (uniformity) and local responsiveness. L'Oréal resolved it by internationalising its management, not its structure.
  • In its critical activity, new-product development, it places managers from mixed cultural backgrounds (over a third of team managers, for more than 10 years) at the centre of the teams.
  • They play five roles: spot opportunities, prevent loss in translation, integrate outsiders, mediate with bosses, and bridge subsidiaries and headquarters.
  • The article attributes L'Oréal's success in emerging markets to this strategy (the claim is top management's, as reported).

3.1 The tension

  • Scale demands some uniformity
    • serving national markets demands adaptation (an Italian washing machine differs from a Swedish one; restaurants seem local but Starbucks and Wagamama succeed globally).
  • The tension peaks when product development needs tacit knowledge (collective, revealed in action)
    • it works best within national boundaries. Crossing borders reduces it to words and numbers, which local misinterpretation can distort unseen.
  • L'Oréal:
    • a portfolio of brands from many cultures (French L'Oréal Paris, Garnier, Lancôme; American Maybelline, Kiehl's, SoftSheen-Carson; British The Body Shop; Italian Giorgio Armani; Japanese Shu Uemura), offices in 130+ countries
    • in 2012 over half of sales came from new markets (a third in 2009)
    • 2012 sales grew 18.4 percent in Asia Pacific and 17.6 percent in Africa and the Middle East without major acquisitions
      • It invests 3.5 percent of revenue in R&D against Revlon's 1.7 percent and Estée Lauder's about 1 percent
    • about 20 percent of products each year are new.
  • Success was built by a management team "strongly rooted in its home culture": only five CEOs in a century, all but one with long tenures and promoted from within
    • Lindsay Owen-Jones (CEO 1988 to 2006), an Englishman, was described by the founding family as Français dans l'âme.

3.2 The solution

  • Structural fixes (autonomous subsidiaries, global business units, or refocusing) would not fit
    • infusing foreign executives would disrupt the tight community
    • global teams suffer the Tower of Babel syndrome, with members talking past each other, even over "universal" knowledge like mathematics.
  • Instead, since the late 1990s L'Oréal Paris put executives from mixed cultural backgrounds in new-product development: about 40 teams of three or four people, two of whom may be multicultural (e.g. a Lebanese-Spanish-American on hair colour and a French-Irish-Cambodian on hair care, sharing an office). Development takes at least a year of exchanges with subsidiaries and French functional units
    • concepts are pitched to regional directors at Réunion Internationale, the annual Paris event.
  • Recruits
    • come from three pools: seasoned subsidiary staff (5+ years in sales and marketing), managers from other global firms, and international business-school graduates (a 12-month programme in Paris, New York, Singapore or Rio, then Cedep). After 2 to 3 years at headquarters, many return to regions as directors
    • some rise to senior roles (a Hong Kong-British-French manager led East Asian facial products).

3.3 Five roles

  • Recognise new-product opportunities.
    • "Their background is a kind of master class in holding more than one idea at the same time." The French-Irish-Cambodian manager noticed Asian tinted creams also lift, while European ones were either tinted (makeup) or lifting (skin care), and launched a tinted lifting cream in France
    • An Indian-American-French manager: "I have a stock of references in different languages."
  • Prevent loss in translation.
    • Even shared formulas get different meanings: a French manager's product test failed after a German lab colleague heard something different, forcing a costly rerun
    • a multicultural manager now decodes headquarters-to-Germany messages.
  • Integrate outsiders.
    • Teams of one culture fall into stereotyping a newcomer. A Hong Kong-British-French director defended a newcomer from Shanghai called "very rude", found she was direct, without bad intention, advised her quietly, and told the group what he saw
    • "Things became much better."
  • Mediate with bosses. A manager with a French boss who never starts meetings on time tells his team why he is late and when he is next free, so conflicts are handled "more tolerantly".
  • Bridge subsidiaries and headquarters.
    • Paris asked India to find a rare plant for an organic shampoo
    • "we'll do our best" (heard as yes) actually meant no, followed by "We need confirmation that this ingredient can really please consumers". The Indian-American-French leader read it, avoided open conflict and found another ingredient.
  • Learning from others:
    • roles 1 and 2 can be learned through training; roles 3 to 5 depend on skills from early life and are harder
    • Useful: training with multiculturals and repatriates, coaching, evaluation by an informed HR specialist, language and semantics training, leadership in a foreign language
    • Avoid short rotations: without deep exposure, managers focus on what is common and cultural sensitivity is dulled.
  • For HR:
    • appoint a manager to nurture multiculturals, tailor training, secure top-management commitment and grant some autonomy
    • The wider lesson: companies should "learn how to learn from their peripheries", reversing the old home-to-subsidiary knowledge flow.

3.4 Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the article)

  • See your dual vantage as an asset.
    • The article's multiculturals are valued for reading both sides ("We'll do our best" meant no). As an Indian leading British engineers, you may decode both directions
    • say so when a misunderstanding looks cultural.
  • Translate your own phrases: before a review, ask "What did you hear me ask for?" so loss in translation is caught early.
  • Name the behaviour, not the person: like the "very rude" case, ask whether a colleague's directness or understatement is style, not intent.
Terms to carry forward
  • Global-local tension; tacit knowledge; Tower of Babel syndrome.
  • Multicultural managers and their five roles.
