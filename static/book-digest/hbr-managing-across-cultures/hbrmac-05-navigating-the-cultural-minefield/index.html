HBR Managing Across Cultures 5: Navigating the Cultural Minefield

Source: Erin Meyer, "Navigating the Cultural Minefield", Harvard Business Review, May 2014 (reprint R1405K), reprinted in HBR's 10 Must Reads on Managing Across Cultures (Harvard Business Review Press, 2016). Own-words summary, much shorter than the source.

Takeaways
  • Stereotyping a culture on one or two dimensions ("the Japanese are hierarchical") misleads. Meyer's Culture Map uses eight scales: Communicating, Evaluating, Persuading, Leading, Deciding, Trusting, Disagreeing, Scheduling.
  • A position means nothing alone: what matters is the gap between two cultures on a scale. The same behaviour reads as egalitarian or hierarchical depending on who is looking.
  • Four rules: don't underestimate the challenge, apply multiple perspectives, find the positive in other approaches, adjust and readjust your position.
  • The map describes a society's average, not any individual. You may differ from your culture's profile.

5.1 The problem and the tool

  • The opening story.
    • Aaron grew up in Tel Aviv with Russian-born parents, spoke Russian, and had led teams in Israel and Canada
    • six months after taking over a Moscow plant he still struggled. Meyer, after 16 years studying cultural effects on business, says most writing is too crude to help: managers lean on clichés, then are surprised when a French colleague bluntly criticises them or a Japanese client wants everyone's buy-in.
  • The Culture Map has eight scales built on decades of academic work plus her own interviews with thousands of executives:
    • Communicating.
      • Low-context cultures prize precise, simple, explicit messages taken at face value, with repetition and writing welcome
      • High-context cultures are layered and nuanced, with messages implied and "reading between the lines" needed
      • The metric is Edward Hall's.
    • Evaluating.
      • Frank versus diplomatic negative feedback (Meyer's own scale). It differs from Communicating: the French are more implicit communicators than Americans yet more direct in criticism
      • Spaniards and Mexicans share a context level but the Spanish are much more frank.
    • Persuading.
      • Specific thinking (a Western executive splits an argument into distinct parts) versus holistic thinking (Asian managers show how the parts fit, after Richard Nisbett). Southern European and Germanic cultures favour principles-first (deductive) arguments
      • American and British managers are more swayed by applications-first (inductive) logic (Meyer's own addition).
    • Leading. Egalitarian to hierarchical; respect and deference to authority (Geert Hofstede's power distance; the GLOBE study of 62 societies).
    • Deciding.
      • How consensus-minded a culture is, which is not the same as hierarchy: Germans are more hierarchical than Americans yet more likely to build group agreement
      • the Japanese are both strongly hierarchical and strongly consensus-minded.
    • Trusting. Task-based (cognitive) trust built through reliable work, versus relationship-based (affective) trust built through personal connection.
    • Disagreeing. Whether open disagreement is seen as healthy or as harming relationships (Meyer's own scale).
    • Scheduling. Linear and structured (monochronic) versus flexible and reactive (polychronic), after Hall.
  • Aaron's fix.
    • Plotting Israel against Russia, he saw similarity on scheduling, disagreement and trust but a big gap on Leading: Russians strongly value hierarchy
    • His habit of emailing lower-level staff without going through their bosses angered middle managers; stopping it helped a lot
    • "In Russia, when I try to push decision making down ..
    • it often suggests weak leadership."

5.2 The four rules

  1. Don't underestimate the challenge. Styles are lifelong habits. Carlos, a Mexican marketing director for Dos Equis at Heineken's Amsterdam headquarters, found his Dutch team challenging his rollout process in meetings ("Where is the respect?"). He realised he had to "unlearn many of the techniques" that made him successful in Mexico.
  2. Apply multiple perspectives. Leading a Brazilian, Korean and Indian team means knowing how each views the others. Position is relative: Mexico sits between the egalitarian Netherlands and hierarchical China, so a Chinese manager in Monterrey found Mexicans uncomfortably egalitarian (first names, public disagreement), the same thing Carlos saw in the Dutch.
  3. Find the positive in other approaches. Steve, an Australian in China, first disliked "the boss is always right", then valued the Confucian reciprocity: the higher person must also protect and care for those below, and a clear instruction can be met by a willing team. Carlos came to value the ideas and problems caught because the Dutch challenged him. A leader who understands the styles can pick the best subteam for each task.
  4. Adjust, and readjust, your position. Widen your comfort zone across all eight scales. Aaron watched successful Russian leaders and became more of a director; back in Israel he was accused of centralising too much because he had carried his Russian style home.

5.3 Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the article)

  • Plot yourself and your team on Leading, Persuading and Evaluating before your next one-to-one; ask each person which they expect: a clear instruction, or an open discussion?
  • Lead a technical case applications-first
    • (an example from this system, then the principle) if your British engineers respond better to concrete cases
    • Meyer says British and American managers lean that way.
  • State your own style out loud ("I'll disagree openly because I want the best design; it isn't personal"): the article's rule 2 is that others read your style through their own position.
  • For delivery practice, use speaking-practice/ drills on a three-sentence structure (point, reason, example).
Terms to carry forward
  • Culture Map and its eight scales.
  • Low-context versus high-context communication.
  • Principles-first versus applications-first persuasion.
  • Task-based versus relationship-based trust.
