Lead Developer Career Guide 3: Learning lead developer skills

Source: Shelley Benhoff, Lead Developer Career Guide (Manning), ch. 3. Previous: Lead Developer Career Guide 2: Lead developer career trajectory. Next: Lead Developer Career Guide 4: Learning any developer skill.

Takeaways
  • Leads must balance technical and soft skills, so prioritise and timebox learning (15 to 30 minutes counts).
  • Keep tech current via release notes, newsletters, communities, influencers; practise soft skills on the job (conflict, empathy, feedback) and embrace failure.
  • Ten leadership styles; leads mostly use supportive ones (transactional, servant, coaching, transformational) and keep autocratic/commanding for emergencies.
  • Introverts can lead; use personality tools lightly (MBTI has limits; Big Five and DISC are alternatives) and observe your team's personalities.
  • Presentations: practise, record, arrive early; simple slides; engage the audience (treat it as a performance).

3.1 Prioritising learning

  • Outdated skills mean missed upgrades (security problems or bugs)
    • leads often suggest new technology for the whole organisation. The author has used 15+ languages and frameworks
    • she reads blogs, white papers and announcements and studies architectural patterns of a language before starting.
  • Soft skills change too:
    • from command-and-control (industrial era) to the participation era, where people want meaning and purpose
    • emotional intelligence is now a key leadership skill.
  • Fear of failure leads to no decision, which is itself a decision and hurts morale (some companies defer decisions to the last responsible moment to weigh options).

Technical skills

  • When you hit an unknown error and a search yields new errors, switch to documentation or step-by-step blogs.
  • Subscribe to email notifications for the technologies you use and read release notes ("how will this affect our projects?").
  • Tech communities
    • (run by companies give direct employee support): give as much as you get
    • awards are a byproduct, never the aim
      • Examples: 100 Days of Code (www.100daysofcode.com), 100 Devs (https://leonnoel.com/100devs/), Black Tech Twitter (https://mng.bz/1aGQ), Dev.to, GitHub Community, GitLab Community, Hashnode, Hacker News, Women Who Code.
  • Influencers and developer advocates:
    • search keywords on X/Twitter, LinkedIn, YouTube
    • attend events
    • choose people with a track record of quality content
    • be wary of pure self-promotion
    • join the conversation.
  • Pass what you learn to the team and get their opinions on upgrades.

Soft skills (table 3.1)

Skill Why Resources
Communication go-between for dev and non-dev teams The Art of Communicating (Thich Nhat Hanh); Crucial Conversations (Patterson, Grenny, McMillan, Switzler)
Mentoring guide skills and careers The Mentor's Guide (Lois Zachary); Becoming an Effective Mentoring Leader (Rothwell, Chee)
Emotional intelligence self-awareness, managing behaviour The Emotionally Intelligent Manager (Caruso, Salovey); The Ride of a Lifetime (Bob Iger)
Empathy see others' views; quality relationships Applied Empathy (Michael Ventura); Empathy Works (Sophie Wade)
Conflict resolution positive environment, retain talent Difficult Conversations (Stone, Patton, Heen); The Anatomy of Peace (Arbinger Institute)
Providing feedback positive and negative feedback to grow the team Radical Candor (Kim Scott); Thanks for the Feedback (Stone, Heen)
  • Develop:
    • reflect on strengths and weaknesses (the author does a personal year-end retrospective)
    • seek feedback from colleagues, managers and team
    • read, attend workshops or courses
    • lead by example
    • surround yourself with mentors
    • jump in rather than being tentative: there is no script.

Practising on the job

  • Embrace failure: "You should fail. Without failure, you'll never know what success looks like" (Hetal Dave, Sitecore MVP).
  • Conflict resolution: listen to all sides, keep an open mind, avoid arriving with a fixed solution; the goal is the team's success, not yours.
  • Recognise unspoken conflict
    • via body language, tone, behaviour changes (people not listening and going their own way, crossed arms, indifference)
    • address it constructively (her e-book: Conflict Management Playbook).
  • Emotional intelligence and empathy:
    • apologise when wrong
    • ask how people see their work
    • reducing technical debt must not burn out the team
    • people have lives outside work
    • schedule one-on-ones
    • share your own struggles first.
  • Trust yields honesty, frequent feedback and innovation.

Setting aside time for learning

  • Don't expect a fixed daily hour; protect personal time; use weekly learning objectives (timeboxed); aim for 70%+ of goals; "moonshot" goals shouldn't make you feel bad.
  • Example weekly objectives: create a GitHub repository; build a simple site (heading, paragraph, dropdown); add CSS; commit it.
  • Protect blocks:
    • end overrunning meetings (read body language; suggest follow-up or async), turn off notifications (15 to 30 focused minutes beat distracted multitasking)
    • The example calendar has daily standup at 9:00 plus "heads down on tasks" and study blocks.
  • Say no: ask "what can I cut to fit this?" or delegate to someone equally capable.

3.2 Leadership styles

  • Up to 30 styles exist; test several for different situations and personalities; understand your own traits and observe your team's.
Style Pros Cons
Autocratic (decides alone) quick, efficient low morale, little creativity
Democratic (seeks input) job satisfaction, commitment slower decisions
Transformational (inspires toward a shared vision) motivates not good for quick decisions
Transactional (clear goals, rewards or consequences) achieves specific goals little long-term commitment
Servant (focus on members' needs) strong culture, satisfaction slow in quick-decision situations
Visionary (compelling future) long-term success poor in crises
Coaching (guidance and development) skills growth slow in quick-decision situations
Laissez-faire (hands off) creativity poor for goals or crises
Affiliative (relationships, harmony) harmonious team poor for goals or crises
Commanding (takes charge) emergencies low morale, no innovation
  • Use of autocratic and commanding: not regularly (don't decide without the team), but have the skill for emergencies (regain order, make a plan). Expect system failures.
  • Leads are in a support role:
    • transactional and servant
    • mentoring = coaching and transformational. Visionary is typically a CEO but applies at team level for managers and directors.
  • Books:
    • Drive (Pink), Good to Great (Collins), Leadership and Self-Deception (Arbinger), Start with Why (Sinek), The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People (Covey), The Five Levels of Leadership (Maxwell), The Power of Servant-Leadership (Greenleaf)
    • Podcasts: Leadership and Business (Harvard Business Review), The Leadership Lab (Center for Creative Leadership).

Your personality type

  • Introverts can be effective leaders (careful listening, deep thinking, clear communication); leadership is about abilities and skills, not type.
  • Assessments:
    • EQ (emotional intelligence)
    • DISC (dominance, influence, steadiness, compliance)
    • Five-Factor Model (openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism)
    • Leadership Styles Inventory (LSI)
    • MBTI (personality preferences).
  • Using results:
    • identify dominant traits
    • reflect on how they shape your style (high agreeableness: consensus builder)
    • consider strengths and challenges
    • pick development actions
    • get feedback
    • adapt.
  • A trait is a building block; a type groups traits (as in MBTI). Helpful traits: confident, decisive, adaptable; found in all types.

Observing your team's personalities

  • Tailor communication:
    • ask an introvert for their opinion in meetings
    • written or one-on-one for introverts, group discussion for extroverts
    • praise more often for those motivated by recognition.
  • MBTI
    • has 16 types from four dichotomies (Introversion/Extraversion, Sensing/Intuition, Thinking/Feeling, Judging/Perceiving) but critics cite weak scientific validity and pigeonholing
    • consider Big Five (empirical support) or DISC (workplace behaviour).
  • Observe:
    • independent or team
    • analytical or intuitive (data vs hands-on)
    • considers all options or acts fast
    • response to feedback (give criticism to defensive people in private: public shame kills morale; offer coaching)
    • handling of workload (structured or flexible)
    • values and motivations.

3.3 Presentation skills

Desired outcomes: clear concise message; one or a few takeaways; increased understanding; inspiration to act; changed minds; engagement; credibility. Used for demos, walkthroughs, training and mentoring; a bad presentation costs trust.

Examples the author recommends: ".NET Overview & Roadmap" (Hanselman and Hunter: https://mng.bz/PNZ8), "The Art of Computers" (https://mng.bz/JNZz), "Everything You Thought You Already Knew about Orchestration" (Laura Frank Tacho: https://mng.bz/w5xB), IBM "Forward thinking: What's next for AI" (https://mng.bz/q0ON).

Calming nerves

  • Be nervous: it means you care.
  • Practise (timing), visualise success, focus on the audience (they want you to succeed), record yourself, get feedback from a friend.
  • Arrive early and set up equipment; have someone who knows the room kit; for video, ask the host to join 15 minutes early.
  • On the day: breathing exercises, meditation, positive self-talk, tense and release muscle groups.

Effective slides

  • Simple and focused:
    • agenda at start, summary and next steps at end
    • few key points
    • headings and bullets
    • follow the company style guide
    • too much text and people read instead of listening.
  • Readable fonts and large sizes; relevant high-quality images; code examples in a readable font and layout; limited consistent colour palette.
  • For non-developers: state the problem or goal first; avoid jargon; use analogies (a car engine, a smart-home device) and examples.

Great performance

  • Engage: questions, feedback, discussion, stories; ask leading questions at the start ("what do you hope to learn?") to tailor on the fly.
  • Improvisation helps: take an improv class; if beginners are in the room, explain fully.
  • Confidence:
    • you are the expert
    • ignore imposter syndrome
    • seek feedback from colleagues
    • it's fine not to know: say you'll research it
    • keep a "parking lot" of open questions and share answers with everyone.

3.4 Case study: Maureen Josephine (software engineer, Google Developer Expert in Flutter and Dart; McKinsey Next Generation Women Leader Award 2020)

  • Communication:
    • no one knows what you need unless you say it
    • receive feedback without taking it personally. At her first internship she didn't ask questions, got stuck on assigned reading and almost quit
    • then learnt to be proactive.
  • Presentations:
    • be confident
    • research so facts are right
    • make slides about two weeks ahead
    • massage jaw and cheeks, affirmations, look good
    • ask the audience what they know first (her first talk in Hamburg).
  • Personalities: culture matters (jokes can offend); you can't treat everyone alike when giving feedback; put yourself in their shoes.
  • Busy schedules:
    • keep discipline
    • use a schedule that suits you (morning or evening), as little as 30 minutes a day but consistent
    • know the end goal
    • practise and share (articles, videos, Stack Overflow answers: she found her own answer years later)
    • learn the specific problem at work first, deep dive later.

Summary points

Balance technical and soft skills; lifelong learning; ten leadership styles with supportive ones prioritised and higher-level or commanding ones ready; presentation nerves are normal and practice helps; slides should be simple, focused, clear and high-quality.
