Lead Developer Career Guide 7: Working with project teams

Source: Shelley Benhoff, Lead Developer Career Guide (Manning), ch. 7. Previous: Lead Developer Career Guide 6: Optimizing the development process. Next: Lead Developer Career Guide 8: Speaking with clients.

Takeaways
  • Cross-train in project management: lead developers lead the dev team; project managers orchestrate timelines and resources.
  • Waterfall is linear and fits fixed requirements; agile (Scrum, Kanban) is iterative and fits shifting priorities.
  • Estimates: hourly for precise, well-understood work; story points (relative complexity, not hours) for uncertain work. Include the team, surface assumptions, define scope.
  • Facilitate communication: connect people, ask for help, avoid miscommunication (jargon, no active listening, culture, no feedback).
  • Improve project management: support PMs, integrate deployments with Jira or Trello, track the defect rate.

7.1 Cross-training project management skills

  • Learn by shadowing co-workers or working with a mentor
    • benefits: well-rounded decisions, anticipating problems, better communication with stakeholders, career prospects, job satisfaction.
  • Distinct but complementary roles:
    • the lead developer leads the dev team and delivers high-quality code
    • the project manager orchestrates the project, timelines and resources (strategic vision).

Waterfall

  • Linear, sequential: requirements gathering → design → implementation → testing → maintenance; each stage is completed (and signed off by stakeholders) before the next.
  • Fits well-defined requirements and clear scope. Pros: detailed early plan, tight requirements limit scope creep, problems found early per stage.
  • Example given in the book: Microsoft Windows development (the book asserts this without a source) (unverified).
  • Author's view: requirements are rarely fully known, priorities shift, and any change restarts all phases, so productivity drops; use agile when priorities constantly change.

Agile

  • Values individuals and interactions over processes and tools and responding to change over following a plan
    • flexible, iterative, collaborative
    • frameworks Scrum and Kanban.

Scrum

  • Three roles: product owner (defines and prioritises the backlog), scrum master (ensures the process, removes obstacles), development team (delivers a potentially releasable increment each sprint).
  • Four activities: sprint planning, daily scrum, sprint review, sprint retrospective.
Term Definition
Sprint fixed time-boxed iteration working a subset of the backlog
Backlog prioritised list of features, enhancements, fixes
Product owner manages and prioritises the backlog, ensures value
Scrum master ensures process, removes obstacles, facilitates communication
Sprint planning start of sprint: review backlog, select items, define a goal
Daily scrum 15-minute meeting: progress, obstacles, plan for the day
Sprint review end of sprint: demo completed work to stakeholders, get feedback
Sprint retrospective end of sprint: reflect, find improvements, plan next sprint
User story short description of a feature from the user's perspective
Velocity work completed per sprint or over several
  • Author's observation:
    • many companies skip the review and retrospective (poor transformation from waterfall)
    • having a daily scrum doesn't make an organisation agile,
      • since without them continuous improvement doesn't happen
      • Companies using the full set got standardised processes, higher productivity and less technical debt.

Kanban

  • Pull-based, three principles: visualise the workflow, limit work in progress (WIP), manage flow.
  • Kanban board: columns per stage, each with a WIP limit that stops new work until existing work finishes; metrics lead time and cycle time.
  • Author's team (HoffsTech) uses Kanban:
    • WIP limits avoid overwork and keep task order
    • in Scrum, tasks slip sprint to sprint and get reprioritised on the fly (re-acclimatisation costs productivity).
  • Choose by project and team; both share agile principles.

Agile vs waterfall

Criteria Agile Waterfall
Definition iterative, flexible; collaboration, adaptability, satisfaction linear, sequential; planning, documentation, control
Focus customer satisfaction, delivering value meeting requirements, staying in budget
Planning continuous comprehensive up front
Requirements change as the project progresses fixed at the start
Testing continuous at the end of each phase
Feedback regular from customers and stakeholders limited
Risk constantly monitored at the start
Cycle shorter (two to four weeks) longer (three to six months or more)

Agile works best when change is factored into time and money planning. Author: agile suits software better, but many organisations succeed with waterfall.

7.2 Providing accurate estimates

  • Underestimating
    • leads to overtime and burnout (overtime only briefly boosts productivity; week after week it falls), missed deadlines, lost trust
    • Overestimating wastes time and resources.
  • Two methods: hourly or story points.

Hourly estimates

For high precision (fixed budget, tight deadlines) and consultancy billing.

  1. Understand requirements and scope.
  2. Break down tasks.
  3. Use historical data (compare original estimates with actuals, note avoidable problems).
  4. Consider all factors (e.g. availability of collaborators).
  5. Review and revise, allowing for the unexpected.

Author's practice: break tasks down with the dev team and project manager; assign each feature to one developer and bug fixes to others so everyone has experience of each feature; clarify requirements with active listening and by repeating them back to the client (it also uncovers inaccurate requests); estimate typical page types (homepage, search results) from past projects.

Story points

  • A unit for relative effort (complexity, team skill, resources), reviewed and revised during the project.
  • Methods
    • Planning Poker: members show cards; discussion converges on consensus.
    • T-shirt sizing: XS to XL; simple, good for small stories.
    • Fibonacci: numbers (1 lowest, 13 highest) because effort grows with complexity and interdependence.
  • Common: T-shirt or Fibonacci combined with Planning Poker; the author keeps a 1 to 5 scale with documented guidelines.
Points Effort Search-feature task
1 lowest create an empty search page framework
2 low to medium display search filters
3 medium display search results
4 medium to high implement standard search functionality
5 highest implement search filters
  • Biggest mistake:
    • 1 hour = 1 story point. Story points measure complexity
    • if you want hours, use hourly estimates. It stems from poor agile transformation, reusing waterfall reporting tools.
Hourly Story points
hours based on historical data, complexity, risk relative effort from complexity, uncertainty, risk
concrete metric easy to turn into cost or schedule effort, not time
affected by productivity, skills, environment less affected by external factors
specific, well-understood tasks (bug fixes, small enhancements) uncertain or unclear tasks (research, new functionality)
precise time and cost for clients prioritises by effort and complexity
predictable with an established team breaks work down for a new team or project

Stick to one method organisation-wide; most of her companies used Planning Poker with T-shirt sizing.

Including the team

  • Define scope:
    • objectives, deliverables, tasks, boundaries
    • ask developers and project managers for scope, dependencies and prerequisites
    • communicate your understanding to the PM first. Mistake: she added features for quality without telling the PM, putting tasks out of scope and costing time and budget.
  • Goal-oriented requirements: ask "what are the best possible solutions to the client's requirements?" and what the team could deliver in a sprint (e.g. goal: automate a manual process; requirements: integrate with an API to automate reporting).
  • Anticipate problems:
    • ask the team to share concerns specifically (technology integration, compatibility, miscommunication)
    • plan extra resources or time, revise the plan or scope, contingency plans.
  • Surface assumptions (taken as true without proof): ask team members for their thought process and specific questions:
    • Are you assuming we'll have all necessary resources?
    • Are you assuming requirements won't change?
    • Are you assuming no technical difficulties?
    • Are you assuming the client understands what they want versus what they need?
  • Questions to gather estimates:
    • What steps are required?
    • Any dependencies or prerequisites?
    • Technical challenges or obstacles?
    • External factors (client requests, resource availability)?
    • When might we see progress toward our goals?
    • What do you need from the team to deliver what the customer needs?
  • Then prioritise the backlog with the PM and decide sprint contents, matching tasks to skills.

7.3 Facilitating communication

Facilitating means creating a space where people communicate freely without fear of judgment. The author's first lead role: she spotted information silos and reached out to other teams (development and training), avoiding duplicated work and uncovering pipeline problems that caused documentation errors.

Properly facilitated Not facilitated
Teamwork share ideas and feedback, work to goals silos, unclear roles, duplicated or missed tasks
Productivity understand goals, ask for help easily wasted effort on finished or changed tasks, delays
Innovation creative thinking hesitant to share ideas
Morale valued, heard isolated, frustrated, burnout
Quality catch problems early costly rework, missed errors

Connecting team members

  • Tell the team "the only stupid question is the one you don't ask".
  • Build relationships with DevOps, QA, project management so the team knows whom to contact.
  • Create collaboration opportunities across departments (cross-training, professional development).
  • Pair developers with UX/UI
    • so they understand the purpose and how users interact
    • in her Sitecore CMS work, UX/UI produced screenshots of every interface with the field-to-database mapping for training and documentation.
  • Thank outside people who help.

Asking for help

  • Admit you don't have all the answers; vulnerability gives the team a safe space; learn from anyone.
  • Example: comparing hosting providers for a client, she asked her team; a junior who had taken an Azure course got her up to speed, enabling a report with options.
  • Ask for feedback on communication and decision-making. Her imposter syndrome made her fear negative feedback; asking surfaced pain points to fix proactively.

Avoiding miscommunication

  • Unclear expectations: set them at the start and review as priorities change.
  • Jargon and acronyms: define terms; keep a project glossary (non-developers appreciate it, and non-native speakers too); non-developers on projects do have technical understanding.
  • Lack of active listening: attention to verbal and non-verbal cues, clarify, paraphrase, summarise, reflect; repeat a PM's feature description back to them.
  • Culture and language barriers: simpler language, avoid idioms and cultural references, avoid contractions and too many acronyms, be patient.
  • Lack of transparency and feedback loops:
    • worse is no communication. Her example: an over-engineered process of automated Slack deployment messages with no per-project context confused the team
    • she proposed the lead monitor the deployment and update the team on the next step, what to expect, and who does what
      • After she implemented it, deployments were faster and had fewer errors.

7.4 Improving project management processes

A good process defines objectives, timelines, milestones and budget, reduces delay, cost overruns and scope creep, and gives transparency and accountability through regular progress reporting.

Supporting project managers

  • Give accurate, timely technical information for decisions, and translate to non-technical stakeholders.
  • Foster team culture (recognition, coaching, mentoring, continuous learning).
  • Suggest improvements positively, with specific solutions, clearly articulated; be open to the PM's constraints.
  • Wagile / Scrumfall:
    • a mismatched half-waterfall, half-agile process after a failed transformation
      • She suggested sprint retrospectives including stakeholders and shared Scrum.org resources (www.scrum.org)
    • through consistent discussion they became fully agile.

Integrating deployments with project management systems

  • Central tracking: see when deployments start, finish, succeed.
  • Jira:
    • the deployment tool creates a deployment ticket (target environment, package, notes) that updates automatically on completion
    • At one company using Jira with Azure DevOps deployments, she showed the Jira release feature and automated adding Jira tickets to each deployment, cutting human error.
  • GitHub and Jira:
    • include the Jira ticket number in the branch name, so tickets update on commits and pull requests, keeping release history
    • connect to Slack and Microsoft Teams for updates.
  • Trello: a card per deployment (date, environment, notes) moved to "Completed".
  • Don't over-engineer: automation lacks context, so send a human message when handing off (e.g. starting the testing phase). "Never expect an automated process to communicate for you."

Assessing defect rate

  • Agile: error rate = (defects ÷ stories completed in the sprint) × 100. Example: 2 defects over 10 stories = 20%.
  • Waterfall: error rate = (defects ÷ lines of code) × 100. Example: 150 defects in 1,000 lines = 15%.
  • Reduce:
    • find root causes, involve the team, never rush code reviews (even small ones), test thoroughly: both automated unit tests and manual testing (design and UX can't be automated)
    • too much automation hurts communication.

7.5 Case study: Chloe Condon (developer advocate for Google Cloud and AI; formerly Microsoft and Sentry.io; BA in Drama; Hackbright graduate)

  • Avoiding miscommunication: in meetings, assume neither that anyone is technical nor that no one is; explain what the product does, how, why and how developers would use it.
  • Asking for help:
    • acronyms differ per company, so she is "the squeaky wheel" asking what they mean (assuming others don't know either)
    • Coming from acting (no culture of asking), she learnt fast because many products are unreleased and undocumented
    • How to ask: "I'm trying to do X, here are the three things I've tried
    • Can you help?" Be specific: pulling an engineer away is costly.
  • Supporting project managers:
    • accurate estimates with a buffer of a couple of days or a week
    • a weekly cadence of check-ins
    • proactive updates
    • state your communication preference (chat over email)
    • the check-ins give accountability and show priority.
  • Connecting people:
    • do it immediately with context ("Jill needs help with X. Jack, this is Jill...")
    • ask permission before introducing externally
    • send a video walkthrough rather than only documentation
    • much of development is knowing how to search (changing one word, like the "repository" search meme) and prompt-tuning
    • teach teammates to find internal documentation.

Summary points

Estimate in hours or story points; inaccurate estimates cause missed deadlines, overruns, burnout and lost trust; facilitate communication with regular meetings, collaboration tools and clear protocols; agile is iterative and adaptive, waterfall is sequential.
