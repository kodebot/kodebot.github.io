Lead Developer Career Guide 11: Leading with emotional intelligence

Source: Shelley Benhoff, Lead Developer Career Guide (Manning), ch. 11. Previous: Lead Developer Career Guide 10: Taking the lead. Next: Lead Developer Career Guide 12: Being a successful lead developer.

Takeaways
  • Emotional intelligence (EI): recognise, understand and manage your own emotions while perceiving and empathising with others'. It drives trust, conflict resolution, stress handling and retention.
  • Five components (self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, social skills) and the four-branch model (perceive, use, understand, manage).
  • Self-awareness is the base; manage working relationships with respect, honesty, support, compromise, forgiveness.
  • Radical empathy extends empathy to people you disagree with, and to the social context behind their experience.
  • Difficult conversations: prepare, listen more than you talk, follow up every two weeks.
  • Imposter syndrome: acknowledge it, celebrate wins, share feelings, practise self-compassion, drop perfectionism.

11.1 What is emotional intelligence?

[!quote] Definition (book) The ability to recognise, understand, and manage one's emotions effectively while also perceiving and empathising with the emotions of others.

Why leads need it: it fosters supportive culture (listening, appreciation, constructive feedback), keeps conflicts from escalating, handles tight deadlines and emergencies with composure, and builds loyalty (people stay when the lead gives a safe space to learn). Leads must sense when someone is having a bad day.

Five components (figure 11.1): self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, social skills (communication, collaboration, conflict resolution). The book doesn't attribute this model (it matches Daniel Goleman's mixed model; unverified in the text).

Aspect With EI Without EI
Self-awareness recognises triggers, stays composed ruled by emotions: conflict, poor decisions
Conflict resolution diplomatic, win-win escalates, damages morale
Stress management focused, protects the team overwhelmed, hurts productivity
Empathy genuine care, supports through challenges insensitive: frustration, resentment
Building relationships trust and positive environment no rapport, little cooperation
Communication active listening, open dialogue disregards opinions, low morale
Retention supportive, low turnover toxic, high turnover

The four-branch model

  • Origin:
    • the term was introduced by psychologists Peter Salovey and John D. Mayer and popularised by Daniel Goleman
    • the book dates the four-branch model to Salovey and Mayer, 1990 (unverified: the 1990 paper introduced the term; the four-branch ability model is usually dated 1997).
  • Four branches:
    • Perceiving emotions: read facial expressions, body language, vocal cues (foundation of empathy).
    • Using emotions: harness them for thinking, decisions, prioritising, creativity.
    • Understanding emotions: grasp complex emotions, causes and consequences.
    • Managing emotions: regulate your own and help others regulate theirs, avoiding impulsive reactions.

Recognising emotions (figure 11.2)

Emotion How to recognise it Example
Pride satisfaction at milestones or quality code team members congratulate each other after launching a feature
Empowerment ownership, seeking growth, suggesting improvements a developer proposes a new deployment approach and offers to lead it
Optimism positive outlook, focus on solutions after setbacks the team discusses lessons and determination
Frustration visible irritation a developer sighs loudly while debugging a persistent issue
Apathy indifference, detachment, avoiding activities vague responses, no ideas offered
Stress anxious, overwhelmed, fatigued, lower productivity a usually punctual developer misses deadlines, tense posture, tired eyes
  • Negative emotions are tied to behaviours, not words.
  • Story: restructuring.
    • Team members grew anxious, stopped offering ideas and missed deadlines, but didn't say so
      • She read their actions, held weekly one-on-ones, voiced empathy, managed her own emotions as an example
    • some people were lost, but the team recovered.

Teaching yourself

  • Trust:
    • care about colleagues' wellbeing and growth
      • Ask "how do you feel about the conflict?" not only "what happened?"
      • Share your own struggles first: a mentor asked her input on his anxiety about presentations and deadlines
    • that trickled down.
  • Consider the emotional effect of decisions.
    • Example: the team wanted to fix the whole codebase before new features
    • given the schedule they compromised by fixing bugs that directly affected current features, with a plan to eventually fix the rest.
  • Bad EI leads:
    • formal, no small talk, chastised people on bad days, high turnover
      • Good EI lead: noticed her leadership potential and struggles behind a brave face
      • Watch actions over words: someone saying "fine" but missing deadlines
    • don't pry, but support by listening and appreciating successes.

Teaching your team

  • Model it, give specific, supportive feedback on EI skills, create practice opportunities (role-play, simulations), offer training (Harvard Division of Continuing Education, LinkedIn Learning).
  • Role-play exercise (figure 11.3):
    • introduction (coding challenge, constraints, assignments), adaptation (unexpected requirement changes), collaboration (status updates, solutions), reflection (feedback, understanding)
    • Focus on delegation, progress updates, problem handling.
  • Behaviour she calls out: "I think my idea is best". In meetings: "Let's listen to everyone's ideas." Ideas can combine; "multiple people can be right at the same time".
  • Resources:
    • Emotional Intelligence 2.0 (Bradberry, Greaves, TalentSmart, 2009)
    • Emotional Intelligence: Why It Can Matter More Than IQ (Goleman, Bantam, 2006)
    • HBR Emotional Intelligence Boxed Set (HBR, 2018).

11.2 Using emotional intelligence

Steps (figure 11.4): be aware of your emotions (body language, tone), understand your triggers, learn to self-regulate (deep breathing, meditation, exercise), empathise, communicate effectively (concisely), build strong relationships.

  • Under pressure you may cross your arms or sound tense; recognising it lets you control it.
  • Her trigger:
    • feeling responsible for the team's success, risking burnout. Tell your manager when overwhelmed
    • burnout symptoms: fatigue, trouble concentrating, loss of control. A good manager spots it early.
  • Quick self-regulation: close your eyes, count to 10 breathing deeply; body-scan meditation; Calm app (10 minutes or less).
  • Concise emotional expression
Unnecessary emotional language Clear and professional
"I can't believe this is happening! This is a disaster, and it's all your fault!" "I want to discuss the situation with the missed deadline. It's causing a significant delay in the project, and we need to address it urgently."

Becoming self-aware

  • Internal self-awareness: your emotions, values, beliefs, personality. External self-awareness: how others perceive you.
  • Lets you choose when to be directive or participative (an experienced self-motivated team suits hands-off servant leadership; a less experienced team needs more direction).
  • Story:
    • some people want "all business"
    • she asked herself whether they were uncomfortable being personal in general or with her, noticed shifting and no eye contact, apologised for overstepping and said they needn't answer.
  • Self-reflection: analyse reactions under pressure or when giving feedback; ask why you reacted that way. Empathy supports self-awareness.
  • Warning: self-awareness uncovers toxic behaviours; she had been hard on underperformers because her own emotions compromised her decisions.

Managing working relationships

  • Be respectful
    • (no disrespectful jokes)
    • honest (direct about problems)
    • there for the team
    • clear about expectations and open to feedback
    • willing to compromise (meet halfway)
    • forgiving (don't forget, but don't hold it against them).
  • Conflict:
    • managers who told her to handle conflict herself made her feel unimportant, and it stayed unresolved
    • she left. As a lead she holds a one-on-one immediately when someone struggles and brings the conflicting parties together, listening without judgement.
  • Story:
    • a team member fell ill and missed work
    • another was upset by slipping deadlines. She apologised for lacking a contingency plan, put the focus on herself rather than forcing disclosure, and they planned together
    • the ill colleague returned to a welcoming team.

Personal growth

  • EI helps in personal life:
    • listen, don't give unsolicited advice (people may just need to vent)
    • share your experience without insisting they follow it
    • clarify your values for decisions. She had toxic relationships when young, recognised her role, repaired some and cut ties with others.

11.3 Learning radical empathy

Aspect Empathy Radical empathy
Focus individual experiences and emotions individual experience plus broader social, cultural, systemic factors
Response emotional support and validation action on the underlying problems; positive change
Scope one person or small group patterns of marginalisation and oppression affecting groups
Goal comfort, understanding a more compassionate and just society; advocating change
Effect individuals feel heard transformative effect on communities (social justice and equity)
  • Radical empathy
    • is understanding and sharing the feelings of others even when we don't agree with them (figure 11.5), an extension of empathy toward people you disagree with or dislike
    • The book's framing is its own.
  • Benefits:
    • reduces miscommunication-driven conflict, de-escalates by listening rather than defending, finds common ground
    • Leads who practise it seem approachable and set the team's culture.
  • Her story:
    • she has a moderate stutter (an invisible disability)
    • managers or clients sighed, talked over her, and once she lost a job
      • She used radical empathy to understand their ignorance
      • Two managers didn't avoid the topic, assured her they wouldn't discriminate, asked questions to support her, and created focus groups to address systemic discrimination.

Understanding others

  • Cognitive bias: systematic deviation from rationality that unconsciously favours information confirming our beliefs. Radical empathy helps recognise it and open dialogue.
  • Be open to being challenged; don't surround yourself with yes-people; diverse perspectives drive innovation.
  • Story:
    • a team member kept pushing back and always asserted they were right
    • the team avoided them
      • A late one-on-one revealed cultural differences (some cultures are blunt; others so diplomatic that criticism sounds like a compliment)
      • She helped them express points professionally
    • they later moved into leadership.
  • Shift from adversaries to humans. Celebrate diversity: one employer's annual cultural festival (exhibits, cuisines) opened dialogue.

Connecting on a deeper level

  • Listen to challenges and aspirations without judgement; recognise personal milestones (parenthood, marriage, fitness goals).
  • Story:
    • runners on her team encouraged her to try
    • she has completed 27 races (5Ks, 10Ks, half-marathons)
    • they organised a virtual 5K for the whole company with a Slack photo channel.

11.4 Having difficult conversations

Case: a developer became irritable and missed deadlines; teammates complained. She told the team the colleague needed support, asked "how are you?" first, learnt of personal and mental-health struggles, worked with HR on mental-health resources and leave options, and assured the colleague of team support on return.

Process (figure 11.6): prepare → listen → explore solutions → resolve → follow-up.

  • Manage your emotions (deep breaths, short breaks)
    • never be defensive
    • watch for escalation (tension, heightened emotion, avoidance), then redirect to the main problem and validate feelings even if you disagree
    • aim for a resolution all agree on.

Preparing

  • Tone of the invitation:
    • "Hey [Name], I would like to discuss the recent problems with the project
    • I value your contributions, and we'll work together to provide you the support you need to succeed." ("Hey" is less formal than "Hello" or "Dear [Name]"; use "I" statements; end positively; it's a collaboration, not a reprimand.)
  • Gather data and specific examples,
    • stick to observable behaviour
    • no assumptions or hearsay. Her metrics for bugs: estimated vs actual time, number of times a task is sent back from QA.
  • Consider alternative causes of failed testing:
    • the developer didn't test in the QA environment (deployment delays, task switching, a local environment differing from QA), inaccurate business or technical requirements, deployment queue bottlenecks, conflicts with new features
    • Come with ideas but listen.

Listening more than you talk

  • Pay attention to tone, body language, emotions; paraphrase to confirm understanding; ask probing, open-ended questions.
  • Bad example:
    • a lead dismissed her best-practice approach ("no time", "theirs is better")
    • theirs caused errors in the existing system and QA, added technical debt, and her solution was eventually implemented after time was lost.
  • Open-ended questions for a lower-productivity developer:
    • How do you feel about your current performance and progress on the project?
    • Can you share your perspective on the feedback you've received so far?
    • What challenges or obstacles do you think are affecting your performance?
    • How do you believe we can work together to address any areas of improvement?
    • What support or resources would help?
    • Can you tell me about your approach to problem solving and decision making in recent tasks?
    • What aspects of your role do you find most satisfying?
    • In what areas do you think you've excelled?
    • What specific feedback or assistance do you need to enhance your coding or design skills?
    • What work-life balance adjustments would help your performance?
  • Don't interrupt; give undivided attention, eye contact, notifications off.

Following up

  • Schedule the first follow-up at least two weeks after,
    • then every two weeks, until everyone says the conflict is resolved (done well, two to three follow-ups suffice). Ask whether they felt the conflict was resolved
    • if no, ask what they need and plan another mediation.
  • Acknowledge positive changes,
    • offer training, mentorship or coaching
    • slow starters often face a steep curve (cutting-edge technology, corporate language)
    • set new goals each time
    • keep your door open.
  • Reflect on your own approach afterwards.

11.5 Avoiding imposter syndrome

[!quote] Definition (book) A lingering feeling of self-doubt where, despite all your successes, you can't shake the worry that you're not truly deserving, fearing others will discover you're not as capable as they think.

It affects everyone, including the author. Effects on her: comparing herself to others (unaware of their full picture), avoiding opportunities where she thinks she won't succeed; she succeeds when she challenges herself and focuses on how her success helps the team.

Strategy Description
Acknowledge and accept common among high achievers; doesn't define your capabilities
Celebrate achievements record successes, revisit during doubt
Set realistic goals break tasks down; avoid needing perfection
Embrace feedback an opportunity for growth
Share your feelings trusted colleagues, mentors, friends
Keep a brag document positive feedback, successful outcomes, challenges overcome
Reframe negative thoughts replace self-deprecation with strengths and progress
Seek mentorship mentors who've experienced it
Mindfulness and self-compassion meditation, breathing
Join supportive communities camaraderie

It often arises from unrealistic expectations of knowing everything and reluctance to show vulnerability.

Self-compassion

  • Mistakes are growth opportunities.
    • Her first failure as lead: a poor technical decision caused a big delay
    • she was harsh on herself, feared losing her job, and performance dropped
      • A Walt Disney quote helped her (the book quotes "I think it's important to have a good hard failure when you're young...") (quote as printed in the book; no source given, unverified)
      • She then brought the team together for a better approach and learnt never to make an important decision alone.
  • Celebrate successes, big and small (reducing a little technical debt), and recall an accomplishment when negative thoughts arrive. Reach out to mentors.

Sharing your feelings

  • Humanises you, builds trust and a culture of openness
    • assesses readiness for lead. She saw executives speak openly about mental health, making a supportive workplace that hired top talent through great employee reviews.
  • Start with small relatable anecdotes; frame difficulties as lessons; hold regular sessions (retrospectives, team meetings, one-on-ones).
  • Story:
    • a combative developer
    • through several one-on-ones and sharing her own experiences they agreed to express feelings assertively not aggressively
    • they later took leadership positions.

Discouraging perfectionism

  • Perfectionism feeds imposter syndrome
    • Redefine success as progress and growth; celebrate milestones (she celebrates small fixes with a pat on the back, a gift, or her favourite restaurant).
  • View mistakes as stepping stones. Her story: she broke best practices to finish an API integration quickly, causing bugs and technical debt, then avoided API integrations, missing a chance to learn.
  • Journal
    • challenges
    • the mistake often turns out smaller
      • Aim for knowledge retention over output: pressure shifts from your code to the quality of learning materials and your recall, speeding similar tasks next time.
  • "It's okay not to have all the answers, and it's okay for your code to be a work in progress."

11.6 Case study: Gabriela Martinez (senior software engineer at Microsoft Research; mentor with Technovation and Women Who Code)

  • Empathy at work:
    • see others' points of view across roles and levels
    • empathise with the end user
      • ("How can I make this easier for the user?"), going through test cases in their shoes
      • Even when the end user is a system (an API), create a persona (e.g. a support engineer connecting to another API).
  • Self-awareness: ask "What do I need to improve to deliver value to my work and working relationships?"; get honest views from a mentor or manager; be honest with yourself.
  • Difficult conversations: disagreement is good; respectful, honest communication is valuable even if unwelcome; ensure team values align with the organisation's.
  • Advice: it starts with you (honesty and self-awareness reflect in all interactions); be curious about improving interactions; use a trusted mentor; company workshops.
  • Resources she likes:
    • Start with Why (Sinek, Portfolio, 2009)
    • Switch (Chip and Dan Heath, Crown Currency, 2010)
    • Think Again (Adam Grant, Penguin, 2023)
    • Emotional Intelligence (Goleman, Bantam, 2006)
    • Dare to Lead (Brené Brown, Random House, 2018)
    • Lean In (Sheryl Sandberg, Knopf, 2013)
    • talk: Esther Perel, "The Power of Relational Intelligence".

Summary points

EI recognises, understands and manages emotions; self-awareness helps leads see their effect on others; radical empathy extends understanding to those we disagree with; open-ended questions and listening uncover underlying problems; follow-up ensures progress.
