Lead Developer Career Guide 12: Being a successful lead developer

Source: Shelley Benhoff, Lead Developer Career Guide (Manning, foreword by Steve Buchanan), ch. 12. Previous: Lead Developer Career Guide 11: Leading with emotional intelligence. This is the final chapter.

Takeaways
  • Readiness is never certain; you set yourself up for success: self-assess, shadow leads, build architecture skills, gather several mentors.
  • Cultivate a positive environment: support DEI, a learning culture, feedback, and no burnout; don't be a "yes" person to management.
  • First project: understand the business context, delegate, plan contingencies, never trade away quality, communicate constantly, keep learning.
  • Work with management: build budgets (with 10 to 20% contingency), help hire, document before recommending dismissal (PIP, HR), set AI tool policies (pilot first).

The author "never thought I was ready" and dived in; she wishes she had prepared. Signs you may be ready: you naturally coordinate and help others, enjoy team success, are working on communication, empathy and conflict resolution. Doubting your readiness reflects conscientiousness.

12.1 Assessing your skill level

  • Technical proficiency: languages, architecture, methodologies; online courses with assessments or a certification exam.
  • Communication:
    • convey complex ideas to technical and non-technical stakeholders
    • Her preparation: she taught non-developer colleagues technical concepts at a high level "without getting into the weeds", encouraged questions, and her leadership noticed and promoted her
    • A lead is a bridge translating jargon.
  • Self-reflection helps you decide when to step up.

Shadowing other lead developers

  • What: follow a seasoned lead, observing day-to-day activity, decisions, and the environment they cultivate; compare how you'd have handled situations (even negative ones).
  • No lead in your organisation? Connect via social media and developer communities.
  • What you see:
    • Emergencies:
      • troubleshooting and decisions under pressure
        • Her own case: a team of three (junior, senior, herself) had a 14-hour production deployment (failed tests, a server disk space problem)
      • she walked through troubleshooting and delegated
      • the senior documented it afterwards.
    • Adaptability: a lead suggested a technology that was relatively new but had existed for a few years, to avoid something quickly outdated (a search filter feature in an enterprise project).
  • How to start: say you want to shadow; she asked her first lead, who agreed; another was nervous (probably imposter syndrome), and she didn't push.
  • While shadowing:
    • be a sponge
    • define goals with the lead (figure 12.1: making effective technical decisions, writing proper technical documentation, communicating with stakeholders and clients, being comfortable with leadership skills)
    • watch prioritising, time management, and interactions
    • participate and network.
  • Ask questions; "if someone makes you feel stupid for asking a question, they have no place in leadership"; get feedback afterwards (what went well, what to improve); reflect.

Building technical architecture from scratch

  • Aim for a reusable prototype so no project starts from scratch, but understand scratch builds: they expose the whole lifecycle (planning, requirements, design, implementation, testing, deployment).
  • Her first lead role
    • had no prototype: she had to work out architecture, set up CI/CD, and support the PM while learning to lead
    • Before that she had only built features on existing architecture (third-party API integrations).
  • Start with "Hello, World",
    • simplest approach, then grow
    • Use team knowledge (reverse engineer a build with colleagues who know a feature type) and follow best practices for clean code.
  • Considerations: requirements, scalability, security, maintainability; demonstrates strategic thinking and long-term planning.

Cultivating a positive working environment

Figure 12.2:

  • Support DEI (appreciating diverse perspectives is the teamwork mindset).

  • Embrace a culture of learning (growth opportunities motivate).

  • Avoid burnout (rest is part of the job).

  • Encourage feedback (incorporate it so people feel valued).

  • Keep soft skills current:

    • she follows the Harvard Business Review and leadership books
    • DEI evolved: she cites facial recognition failing on dark-skinned people because of non-representative test subjects (her recollection; no source given), and terminology shifts (master/slave to main/secondary; blacklist/whitelist to disallowed/allowed).

  • Work-life balance:

    • she once expected overtime from others (inherited from poor leaders). Her example: a lead asked a colleague to work the weekend of a family funeral
    • she logged in from the funeral
    • the author spoke up for the first time. The lead acted that way because upper management treated them the same.

  • Mentors in upper management matter or you may drown in toxic culture. Don't be a "yes" person: protect the team and speak up for needs.

12.2 Taking the leap

"You'll never know for certain" (like parents who didn't feel ready). Set yourself up: she studied leadership, volunteered to lead meetings, mentored teammates; her manager noticed and pushed her; she expects to fail sometimes and needs mentors then.

Gathering a group of mentors

  • Best advice she got: gather mentors before becoming lead. Use them for guidance, feedback, honest assessments, and a sounding board.
  • More than one: one mentor risks you copying them; experiences differ.
  • Clarify needs and goals first.

Figure 12.3: how to gather mentors

  • Attend industry events (leaders, experienced developers, entrepreneurs).
  • Utilise social media (follow, engage with content, join discussions).
  • Seek training programmes (workshops and online courses connect you with experts).
  • Respect their time (explain why you admire their work and how their expertise would help you).

She met most of her mentors via social media and online events; she has taught technical training for 15+ years and mentored 100+ people; "all they have to do is ask".

Reaching out

  • Research them
    • keep the email concise and personalised (a project, article or talk you liked)
    • say clearly what you want (career growth, technical challenges, leadership development)
    • thank them.
  • Example email:
    • Subject "Inquiry about Mentorship Opportunity"
    • introduces yourself and your interest area, cites specific work of theirs, names your challenges (team collaboration strategies, adopting new technologies), notes their time is valuable and asks for a brief virtual coffee chat, thanks them.
  • Follow up politely after a week or two; don't be pushy; if unavailable, try others. Mentorship is a two-way street.

Starting your first project

  • Understand the project:
    • technical requirements plus business goals, audience, timelines, constraints
    • talk to stakeholders, product managers, designers. She met the PM first on her first (enterprise web) project and tailored the technical requirements to business objectives.
  • Delegate by strengths; give ownership. Her mistake: overextending herself on delegatable tasks; others she saw had high turnover because people felt undervalued.
  • Anticipate roadblocks
    • (technical hurdles, resource constraints, changing requirements) with contingency plans
    • Her trap: meeting strict deadlines no matter what to impress leadership.
  • Never compromise quality: coding standards, code reviews, testing; work with PMs so the team isn't overworked; estimate with the team.
  • Communicate: regular check-ins, project management tools (Slack, Jira, email, face to face).
  • Keep growing: solicit feedback, reflect, learn (conferences, courses, publications).

Setting yourself up for success

Her first role: she had few goals or guidance, wasn't confident, and the team didn't trust her key decisions. She recovered through communication and radical candor, asking for feedback from the team and supervisors and incorporating it; the team felt empowered, grew, and some were promoted.

Figure 12.4

  • Build strong relationships (strengths, weaknesses, motivations; a culture where ideas are exchanged).
  • Learn to delegate.
  • Set clear goals and expectations (roles, communicate progress and changes).
  • Manage time effectively (project management tools, calendars, task lists).
  • Seek feedback (training, certifications, experienced leaders).

12.3 Working with management

Engaging with management on budgets, hiring and firing, and AI policy keeps projects funded and aligned. Without lead developer input, management may make technically infeasible decisions, causing delays, overruns and low morale.

Planning a budget

  1. Understand scope and technical requirements; clarify features, performance criteria, deployment needs.
  2. Detailed cost estimates
    • Software tools, development environments, licences.
    • Servers, hosting, specialised hardware.
    • Number and level of developers.
    • Testing, including third-party services.
    • Post-launch support, bug fixes, upgrades.
  3. Prioritise features with PMs by value, cost and effect; decide what is in the first phase.
  4. Contingency budget: typically 10% to 20% of the total depending on complexity and risk. Her biggest hit: turnover (losing project knowledge plus hiring and training costs) on multi-year projects; risk management software can quantify risks.

Hiring developers

  • The engineering manager is the hiring manager, but leads help.
  • Résumé review:
    • relevant experience, scale and complexity of projects
    • problem-solving examples, teamwork and collaboration tools
    • continuous learning (courses, certifications, communities), attention to detail (formatting, spelling). Good résumés highlight relevant information for easy skimming.
  • Interview:
    • Coding exercises, system design (e.g. a scalable e-commerce platform), debugging scenarios (code with a bug).
    • A coding test of one hour maximum before the interview.
Question Relevance
Describe a challenging project you worked on. How did you handle it? problem solving, resilience, stress handling
How do you keep up with the latest developments in technology? commitment to continuous learning
Describe a time you worked closely with others. How did you ensure successful collaboration? interpersonal skills, teamwork
What is your approach to debugging and testing code? methodical approach to quality
How do you prioritise tasks when you have multiple deadlines? time management and prioritisation

Firing developers

  • A lead may help decide but may not attend the firing meeting (to preserve relationships). Dismissal comes only after working with the developer to resolve problems.
  • Process:
    • set clear, measurable objectives and standards with regular feedback
    • if they consistently fail, a formal Performance Improvement Plan (PIP) (expectations, timeline), with ongoing support and coaching
    • document every interaction
    • consult HR regularly for policy and legal compliance
    • only then discuss dismissal with upper management and HR with documented evidence.
  • Problems that might warrant it: missed deadlines, poor quality, negative effect on morale, breaches of company policy.
  • Building the case: specific instances (dates, project names, descriptions), effect on team performance and morale, the PIP and the response, engage HR early, plan for team backlash or morale problems, professionalism and empathy.

Setting policies for AI tools

  • Evaluate alignment with projects, productivity gains (automating mundane tasks), output quality and error rates, integration with current workflows.
  • Risks: over-reliance degrading problem-solving; security and privacy (data handling, data protection regulations); long-term costs (training, integration, maintenance).

Summary points

Shadowing gives insight into the lead role; building architecture from scratch is crucial but is eased by team knowledge and best practices; a positive working environment and diverse mentors prepare you; building trust, delegating, communicating, organising and seeking feedback make the transition work; budgets rely on technical estimates; hiring weighs experience, problem solving, teamwork and learning; dismissal needs a PIP, documentation and HR; AI adoption should be piloted with ROI and risk analysis.
