Learning DDD 1: Analyzing Business Domains

Source: Vlad Khononov, Learning Domain-Driven Design (O'Reilly, 2021), ch. 1. Next: Learning DDD 2: Discovering Domain Knowledge.

Takeaways
  • To build an effective solution you must understand the problem: the organisation's business strategy and the value the software should deliver.
  • A business domain is the company's main area of activity; it is made of subdomains, fine-grained areas of activity.
  • Three subdomain types: core (competitive advantage, complex, volatile), generic (solved, complex, buy or adopt), supporting (obvious, simple, in-house or outsource).
  • Find subdomains from departments, then distil until you reach sets of coherent use cases; distil core subdomains the most.
  • Domain experts are the business's subject matter experts, not analysts or engineers.

Why business analysis in a coding book

  • The chapter is about how companies work:
    • why they exist, what goals they pursue and their strategies
    • Students ask "we write software, not run businesses", the author's answer is a resounding yes.
  • Chapters ahead show how these concepts affect software design.

What is a business domain?

  • A business domain is a company's main area of activity, generally the service it provides to clients: FedEx (courier delivery), Starbucks (coffee), Walmart (retail).
  • A company can have several domains: Amazon (retail and cloud computing), Uber (rideshare, food delivery, bicycle sharing).
  • Companies change domains often: Nokia worked in wood processing, rubber manufacturing, telecommunications and mobile communications.

What is a subdomain?

  • To reach its goals a company operates in several subdomains, each a fine-grained area of business activity. All together they form the business domain.
  • One subdomain is not enough; they must interact. Starbucks needs coffee know-how plus real estate, hiring and finance; none of these alone makes a profitable company.

Types of subdomains

  • Like architectural components (databases, front ends, back ends), subdomains have different strategic and business value. DDD distinguishes core, generic and supporting.

Core subdomains

  • A core subdomain is what the company does differently from competitors: inventing products or services, or reducing costs by optimising processes.
  • Uber first offered ridesharing, then optimised it (matching riders heading the same way). Core subdomains affect the bottom line
    • they rest on inventions, smart optimisations, business know-how or other IP.
  • Google example: Google Ads is a separate business domain, but the Search ranking algorithm is a core subdomain, because search quality drives traffic to the ads.
  • Complexity:
    • a simple core subdomain gives only short-lived advantage, so core subdomains are naturally complex
    • Uber disrupted the "monolithic" taxi industry
    • There should be high entry barriers: hard to copy.
  • Not necessarily technical:
    • A jewellery maker selling online: the design is core, the online shop is not (use an off-the-shelf engine, do not outsource design).
    • A company doing manual fraud detection: the core subdomain is the analysts' work; the software that shows documents and tracks comments is not.

[!note] Core subdomain versus core domain Evans's original book uses both terms interchangeably. The author prefers "core subdomain": it is a subdomain (avoids confusion with business domains), and (chapter 11) subdomains change type over time, so "a generic subdomain evolved into a core subdomain" reads more clearly.

Generic subdomains

  • Business activities all companies perform in the same way. Often complex and hard like core ones, but no competitive edge: battle-tested implementations are widely available.
  • Example: authentication and authorisation. Use an existing, widely tested solution rather than invent one.
  • Jewellery maker: the online shop is generic; using the same platform as competitors does not hurt its advantage.

Supporting subdomains

  • They support the business but give no competitive advantage. Example: an online ad company whose core is matching ads, optimising effectiveness and cutting ad-space cost
    • cataloguing creative materials (banners, landing pages) does not affect profit but is essential to its systems.
  • Defining trait: simple business logic, mostly data entry screens and ETL, that is CRUD interfaces. No high entry barriers needed.

Competitive advantage

  • Only core gives it
    • Generic solutions are the same ones competitors use
    • Supporting subdomains have low entry barriers
      • a company would not mind copying and would prefer them to be generic, ready-made (supporting can turn generic, chapter 11; a case study is in appendix A).
  • Tackling complex problems adds business value, including optimising operations: same service at lower cost is also an advantage.

Complexity

  • Choose tools and techniques that match the complexity of the business requirements, so identifying subdomains is essential.
  • Supporting: simple, obvious logic, often validating inputs or converting data between structures.
  • Generic: complicated (encryption, authentication). They are "known unknowns": you know you do not know, and the knowledge is available (best practices, a consultant).
  • Core: complex and as hard to copy as possible.
  • Tests when unsure:
    • Core versus supporting: could it be turned into a side business? Would someone pay for it alone? Then it is core.
    • Supporting versus generic: is it simpler and cheaper to hack your own than to integrate an external one? Then it is supporting.
    • For software-related core: does the logic look like CRUD data entry (supporting) or complex algorithms and business processes with rules and invariants (core)?

    • it depends on whether integrating a generic solution is simpler or cheaper than building from scratch.

Volatility

  • Core changes often: if solved on the first attempt it is not a good advantage. Solutions are emergent (try, refine, optimise), and the work is never done.
  • Supporting rarely changes; evolving it gives minuscule value compared with the same effort on core.
  • Generic can change too: security patches, bug fixes, entirely new solutions.

Solution strategy

  • All subdomains are needed ("foundational building blocks"), but we pick implementation strategies per type.
  • Core:
    • in-house, never bought or adopted (that undermines the advantage) and never outsourced; cutting corners risks unmaintainable code
    • Assign the most skilled talent, which also speeds evolution
    • Requires the most advanced engineering techniques since requirements change constantly.
  • Generic: hard but solved, so buy off the shelf or adopt open source.
  • Supporting:
    • no ready-made solution, so build, but cut corners: rapid application development frameworks suffice, no elaborate patterns, and no accidental complexity
    • Good for training up-and-coming engineers; save experienced people for core
    • The simple logic makes it a good outsourcing candidate.

Identifying subdomain boundaries

  • Types come from the company's business strategy
    • In most projects subdomains are "already there", but boundaries are not always easy to see
    • Ask a CEO for the subdomain list and you get a blank stare: do the analysis yourself.
  • Starting point:
    • departments and organisational units
    • An online retailer has warehouse, customer service, picking, shipping, quality control, channel management and so on
    • These are coarse.

Distilling subdomains

  • Customer service seems supporting or generic (often outsourced) but inside it are a help desk system and a telephone system (generic), shift management and scheduling (supporting), and an ingenious routing algorithm that sends incidents to agents with success on similar cases (core, since it improves customer experience and needs non-trivial similarity analysis)
    • Figure 1-2 shows this.

Subdomains as coherent use cases

  • Technically a subdomain is a set of interrelated, coherent use cases: same actor, same business entities, closely related data
    • The credit card payment gateway use case diagram (Figure 1-3) forms the credit card payment subdomain.
  • Use this definition as the guide for when to stop drilling down.
  • Pursue laser-focused boundaries for core subdomains (most important, volatile, complex), so generic and supporting functionality is extracted and effort focuses on the essentials.
  • Relax it for supporting and generic:
    • stop when drilling gives no new insight for design, for example when all finer parts have the same type
    • Figure 1-4: distilling the help desk system shows only generic parts and a coarse off-the-shelf tool will be used, so no need.

Focus on the essentials

  • Many business functions drive advantage but have nothing to do with software (the jeweller's design)
    • Identify and acknowledge them and focus on aspects relevant to the system you build.

Domain analysis examples

  • Two fictitious companies
    • Exercise: identify the three subdomain types while reading
    • Real life is similar: some requirements are implicit
    • A short description cannot reveal all subdomains.

Gigmaster

  • A ticket sales and distribution company
    • The mobile app analyses users' music libraries, streaming accounts and social profiles to find nearby shows
    • Users are privacy-conscious: all personal information is encrypted, and the recommendation algorithm works only on anonymised data
    • A module lets users log past gigs attended (even tickets not bought via Gigmaster) to improve recommendations.
  • Business domain: ticket sales.
Type Subdomains
Core Recommendation engine; data anonymisation; mobile app (inferred: user experience is crucial)
Generic Encryption; accounting (sales business); clearing (charging customers); authentication and authorisation
Supporting Integration with music streaming services; integration with social networks; attended-gigs module
  • Design decisions:
    • Recommendation engine, anonymisation and mobile app: in-house, with the most advanced tools and techniques (they change most often).
    • Encryption, accounting, clearing, authentication: off the shelf or open source.
    • Streaming and social integrations and the attended-gigs module: can be outsourced.

BusVNext

  • A public transportation company giving bus rides as comfortable as a cab, managing fleets in major cities
    • A customer orders via the mobile app and a nearby bus's route is adjusted on the fly to pick them up at the scheduled time.
  • The routing algorithm is a variant of the travelling salesman problem, continuously adjusted: since long waits are the main reason for cancelled rides, it prioritises fast pickups even if drop-offs are delayed
    • It integrates third-party traffic conditions and real-time alerts
    • Occasional discounts attract customers and level demand across peak and off-peak.
  • Business domain: public transportation (optimised bus rides).
Type Subdomains
Core Routing; analysis (ride data analysed for customer behaviour insights); mobile app user experience (customer and driver apps); fleet management (maintenance and technical issues cause losses)
Generic Traffic conditions; accounting; billing; authorisation
Supporting Promos and discounts management (a simple CRUD interface for coupon codes)
  • Design decisions:
    • Routing, analysis, fleet management and app usability: in-house, most elaborate techniques and patterns.
    • Promotions management: can be outsourced.
    • Traffic conditions, user authorisation and financial records or transactions: external providers.

Who are the domain experts?

  • Domain experts are subject matter experts who know the intricacies of the business to be modelled in code: knowledge authorities.
  • They are neither the analysts gathering requirements nor the engineers designing the system
    • They represent the business: they identified the problem and all business knowledge originates with them
    • Analysts and engineers turn their mental models into requirements and code.
  • Rule of thumb: the people coming up with requirements or the software's end users.
  • Scope varies:
    • some know the whole business domain, others specialise in particular subdomains
    • In an online advertising agency: campaign managers, media buyers, analysts and other stakeholders.

Conclusion

  • Everything starts with the business domain and its subdomains:
    • Core: the interesting problems (what is done differently from competitors).
    • Generic: the solved problems (all companies do the same; use existing solutions).
    • Supporting: the problems with obvious solutions (built in-house, no advantage).
  • Domain experts are critical to a project's success.

Exercises

  1. Which subdomains provide no competitive advantage? (Core / Generic / Supporting / B and C / A and C)
  2. For which subdomain might all competitors use the same solutions? (Core / Generic / Supporting)
  3. Which subdomain is expected to change most often? (Core / Generic / Supporting / no difference)
  4. Who are business domain experts? (developers specialising in a technology / managers controlling resources / individual contributors who understand the business rules and processes / the lead software architect)
Answers (book appendix B)
  1. D (A and C): only core subdomains give competitive advantage.
  2. B: all companies in the industry can use the same solutions for generic subdomains.
  3. A: core subdomains change and evolve as competitors catch up; they need iterative design.
  4. C: domain experts deeply understand the domain's processes, rules and needs.

Summary points

  • Understand the business strategy before designing software.
  • Classify subdomains as core, generic or supporting and pick the engineering effort per type.
  • Distil to coherent use cases, most carefully for core.
  • Use domain experts as the source of business knowledge.
Terms to carry forward
  • Business domain / subdomain: company's area of activity and its fine-grained parts.
  • Core / generic / supporting subdomain: competitive advantage / solved and common / simple and obvious.
  • Distilling: drilling into a subdomain to find finer-grained ones.
  • Coherent use cases: the stopping rule for subdomain boundaries.
  • Domain expert: business subject matter expert and source of knowledge.
