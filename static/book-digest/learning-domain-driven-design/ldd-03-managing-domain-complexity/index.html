Learning DDD 3: Managing Domain Complexity

Source: Vlad Khononov, Learning Domain-Driven Design (O'Reilly, 2021), ch. 3. Previous: Learning DDD 2: Discovering Domain Knowledge. Next: Learning DDD 4: Integrating Bounded Contexts.

Takeaways
  • Domain experts' mental models can be inconsistent across departments (the term lead means a different thing to marketing and to sales).
  • A single enterprise-wide model ends up good for nothing; DDD splits the ubiquitous language into bounded contexts, each with one consistent meaning per term.
  • A ubiquitous language is only ubiquitous inside its bounded context; it is not universal.
  • Subdomains are discovered, bounded contexts are designed. Size is a strategic choice: wide is hard to keep consistent, tiny adds integration overhead.
  • A bounded context is a model, physical, lifecycle and ownership boundary: one team owns it, one team can own several.

Inconsistent models

  • The language should be clear, consistent, free of ambiguity and extraneous detail. But on an organisational scale, experts' mental models can themselves differ.
  • Telemarketing company (from chapter 2): marketing generates leads through online ads; sales solicits prospects (Figure 3-1). The term lead differs:
    • Marketing: a notification that someone is interested in a product; the event of receiving the prospect's contact details.
    • Sales: a much more complex entity: the whole lifecycle of the sales process, a long-running process, not an event.
  • A catch-22: the language must be consistent (one meaning per term) and also reflect experts' mental models, which conflict.
  • Humans resolve it by context, but code does not cope with ambiguity. Pushing the sales model into marketing adds needless complexity (over-engineered)
    • simplifying sales to the marketing view is under-engineered.
  • Traditional fix:
    • one model for all problems, giving enormous entity relationship diagrams that span office walls (Figure 3-2)
    • "Jack of all trades, master of none": always the complexity of filtering extraneous detail, finding what you need, and above all keeping data consistent.
  • Another fix:
    • prefix the term (marketing lead, sales lead)
    • Drawbacks: cognitive load (which model when? mistakes are easier the closer the implementations are) and the implementation no longer matches the ubiquitous language, since nobody uses prefixes in conversation.

What is a bounded context?

  • The DDD solution is simple: divide the ubiquitous language into smaller languages, each assigned to the explicit context where it applies: its bounded context.
  • Here: marketing and sales contexts. Lead exists in both with a single meaning in each (Figure 3-3).
  • Terminology conflicts and implicit contexts are inherent in any decent-sized business; the pattern makes them explicit and integral to the model.

Model boundaries

  • A model is a construct with a purpose. It cannot exist without a boundary: it would expand into a copy of the real world. Defining bounded contexts is intrinsic to modelling.
  • As with maps:
    • a subway map is useless for nautical navigation, and one context's language can be irrelevant to another
    • Bounded contexts define where a language and its model apply: they are the consistency boundaries of ubiquitous languages.

Ubiquitous language refined

  • It is not ubiquitous across the organisation and not universal
    • It is ubiquitous only inside its bounded context, and cannot be defined or used without an explicit context of applicability.

Scope of a bounded context

  • Language consistency gives only the widest boundary. You can decompose further into smaller contexts (Figure 3-4).
  • Scope is a strategic design decision: wide (following the domain's inherent contexts) or narrow. Size alone is not the criterion; models must be useful.
    • The wider, the harder to keep consistent.
    • The smaller, the more integration overhead.
  • Reasons to extract a finer context: new teams, non-functional requirements (separate development lifecycles) and independent scaling of a function.
  • Beware
    • splitting a coherent function across contexts: changes would hit several contexts and force simultaneous deployment
    • Use the chapter 1 rule: find coherent use cases operating on the same data and keep them together.
  • Boundaries are optimised further in chapters 8 and 10.

Bounded contexts versus subdomains

Subdomains Bounded contexts
Origin Discovered by analysing the business Designed as part of the solution
Defined by Business strategy, requirements (set by the business) Software engineers' strategic design
Purpose Understand how the organisation competes Divide the domain into manageable problem domains
  • Interplay:
    • A single model over the whole domain (a monolithic bounded context, Figure 3-5) could work for a small system.
    • On conflicting models, split by the experts' mental models (Figure 3-6).
    • If still too large, decompose further, for example one bounded context per subdomain (Figure 3-7).
  • All of these are design decisions. One-to-one can be reasonable, but other decompositions can fit better.
  • A model solves a specific problem, so multiple models of the same subdomain can be useful (like different maps of the earth). Forcing one-to-one would remove that flexibility.
  • Footnote: if you also lead business development you wear two hats and can influence strategy (subdomains) as well as design; the chapter discusses software engineering only.

Boundaries

  • Ruth Malan:
    • architectural design is system design, contextual design, inherently about boundaries (what is in, out, spans, moves between) and trade-offs
    • it reshapes what is outside as well as inside.

Physical boundaries

  • A bounded context is also the physical boundary of the implementing system: an individual service or project, implemented, evolved and versioned independently
    • Each may use the technology stack that fits it.
  • If a bounded context holds several subdomains, the context is the physical boundary and each subdomain a logical one (namespaces, modules or packages).

Ownership boundaries

  • "Good fences make good neighbours." The division of work between teams is a strategic decision made with this pattern.
  • One bounded context is implemented, evolved and maintained by one team only
    • no two teams share one. This removes implicit assumptions about each other's models: teams must define explicit communication protocols to integrate.
  • One-directional: one team can own several bounded contexts (Figure 3-8: Team 1 owns Marketing and Optimisation, Team 2 owns Sales).

Bounded contexts in real life

  • A class participant said: "There are no bounded contexts in business domains." They are less evident than domains and subdomains, but they are there, like experts' mental models: be conscious of how experts think about entities and processes.

Semantic domains

  • Bounded contexts rest on the lexicographical notion of semantic domains: an area of meaning and the words used to talk about it
    • Monitor, port and processor differ between software and hardware engineering.
  • The tomato:
    • Botany: a fruit (grows from the flower, bears at least one seed); vegetables are other edible parts (roots, stems, leaves).
    • Culinary arts: a vegetable (flavour profile: fruits soft and sweet or sour, eaten raw; vegetables tougher, blander, often cooked).
    • Taxation:
      • an 1883 US 10% tax on imported vegetables, not fruits
      • botanically a fruit, tomatoes entered tax-free
      • in 1893 the US Supreme Court classified the tomato as a vegetable.
    • Theatre (Romeu Moura): a feedback mechanism.

Science

  • Yuval Noah Harari:
    • scientists agree no theory is 100% correct
    • the real test of knowledge is utility, not truth. Newton (absolute space and time) versus Einstein (space and time differ for different observers): contradictory, yet both useful in their bounded contexts.

Buying a refrigerator

  • A piece of cardboard (Figure 3-9) is a model of a Siemens KG86NAI31L fridge
    • It looks nothing like it, which is irrelevant: the question is what problem it solves
    • It is cut to the fridge's width and depth to check whether it fits through the kitchen door (Figure 3-10).
  • A 3D model would be gross over-engineering
    • For height, a tape measure is another simple model
    • So two models of one fridge, each optimised for its task, each with a strict bounded context: the DDD approach
    • No need for a complex jack-of-all-trades model.
  • A reader suggested a LiDAR phone with an AR app: someone has already solved it with complex technology, so in DDD terms checking whether the fridge fits is a generic subdomain.

Conclusion

  • A conflict in experts' mental models means splitting the ubiquitous language into bounded contexts. Consistent inside a context; across contexts the same term can differ.
  • Subdomains are discovered, bounded contexts are designed (strategic decision).
  • A context and its language are implemented and maintained by one team; a team can own many.
  • Contexts decompose a system into physical components (services, subsystems) with decoupled lifecycles that evolve independently, but must work together
    • Some changes will affect other contexts: the next chapter covers integration patterns that protect from cascading changes.

Exercises

  1. Subdomains versus bounded contexts: subdomains are designed and bounded contexts discovered / bounded contexts are designed, subdomains discovered / essentially the same.
  2. A bounded context is a boundary of (select all): a model, a lifecycle, ownership, all of the above.
  3. Size: the smaller the more flexible / always aligned with subdomains / wider is better / it depends on the project's context.
  4. Team ownership (select all): multiple teams can share one / a single team can own multiple contexts / a context can be owned by one team only / "both B and C" / "both A and B".
  5. Find examples of real-life bounded contexts beyond those in the chapter.
Answers (book appendix B)
  1. B: subdomains are identified in analysis, bounded contexts are designed.
  2. D: a context defines where a model is valid, shares one lifecycle and has one owning team.
  3. D: contexts are design decisions that depend on the project's context.
  4. D: owned by one team only, but one team can own several.

Summary points

  • Conflicting mental models: split into bounded contexts, not into one giant model.
  • Discover subdomains, design bounded contexts; size is a trade-off.
  • A context is a model, physical, lifecycle and ownership boundary; one team per context.
Terms to carry forward
  • Bounded context: explicit boundary where one model and ubiquitous language are consistent.
  • Semantic domain: area of meaning and the words used for it.
  • Monolithic bounded context: one model spanning the whole domain.
  • Physical boundary / logical boundary: service or project versus namespace, module, package.
  • Ownership boundary: one team per context.
