Learning DDD 12: EventStorming

Source: Vlad Khononov, Learning Domain-Driven Design (O'Reilly, 2021), ch. 12. Previous: Learning DDD 11: Evolving Design Decisions. Next: Learning DDD 13: Domain-Driven Design in the Real World.

Takeaways
  • EventStorming is a low-tech workshop in which a group models a business process as a timeline of domain events on sticky notes, enriching it step by step.
  • It has 10 steps: unstructured exploration, timelines, pain points, pivotal events, commands, policies, read models, external systems, aggregates, bounded contexts.
  • The real value is the process: shared knowledge, aligned mental models, discovered conflicts, a ubiquitous language; events, aggregates and contexts are bonuses.
  • Use it to build the language, model processes, explore requirements, recover lost knowledge, improve processes and onboard people; skip it for simple, obvious processes.
  • Keep groups to about 10 (about 5 remotely), watch the energy, and keep a visible legend.

What is EventStorming?

  • A low-tech activity where a group brainstorms and rapidly models a business process; in effect a tactical tool for sharing domain knowledge.
  • A session has a scope:
    • the business process being explored
    • Participants model it as a series of domain events (sticky notes) over a timeline, adding concepts (actors, commands, external systems and more) until the elements tell the story of how the process works.

Who should participate?

"Just keep in mind that the goal of the workshop is to learn as much as possible in the shortest time possible. We invite key people to the workshop, and we don't want to waste their valuable time." Alberto Brandolini, creator of EventStorming.

  • A diverse group: engineers, domain experts, product owners, testers, UI/UX designers, support staff, anyone related to the domain. More backgrounds discover more knowledge.
  • Keep it small: everyone should be able to contribute, which is hard beyond 10 participants.

What do you need?

Item Details
Modeling space A wall covered with butcher paper is best (Figure 12-1); a large whiteboard as big as possible
Sticky notes Lots, in several colours, freely addable by everyone; keep the traditional colour conventions to match books and trainings
Markers Enough for everyone: supplies must not bottleneck knowledge sharing
Snacks Sessions last about two to four hours
Room Spacious, no big table in the middle, and no chairs if possible (people should participate, not zone out; leave a few for those who cannot stand long)

The EventStorming process

  • Usually 10 steps; each enriches the model.

# Step What happens Note
1 Unstructured exploration Brainstorm domain events (past tense) Orange
2 Timelines Order events; happy path first, then alternatives Fix, dedupe, add
3 Pain points Mark bottlenecks, manual steps, missing documentation or knowledge Pink diamond
4 Pivotal events Mark events changing context or phase Vertical bar
5 Commands What triggers events, in the imperative, with actors Light blue, actor on small yellow
6 Policies Automation: event triggers command Purple
7 Read models Views an actor uses to decide a command Green
8 External systems Systems outside the domain Pink
9 Aggregates Group commands (left) and events (right) Large yellow
10 Bounded contexts Group related aggregates Natural boundaries

Step 1: unstructured exploration

  • Brainstorm the domain events:
    • something interesting that has happened in the business, in the past tense (Figure 12-2)
    • Everyone grabs orange sticky notes, writes whatever events come to mind and sticks them on the surface.
  • No need for order or avoiding redundancy. Continue until the rate of new events slows significantly.

Step 2: timelines

  • Organise events in the order they occur
    • Start with the happy path (a successful scenario), then add alternatives (errors, different decisions)
      • branching shows as two flows from the preceding event or as arrows (Figure 12-3)
    • Also fix incorrect events, remove duplicates, add missing ones.

Step 3: pain points

  • Using the broad view, find points that need attention:
    • bottlenecks, manual steps to automate, missing documentation, missing domain knowledge
    • Make them explicit to revisit later
    • Mark with rotated (diamond) pink sticky notes (Figure 12-4: missing knowledge on how airfare prices are compared during booking).
  • Other times too: document any issue or concern raised during the session as a pain point.

Step 4: pivotal events

  • Significant events that change context or phase, marked with a vertical bar dividing before and after
    • Example order process: "shopping cart initialized", "order initialized", "order shipped", "order delivered", "order returned" (Figure 12-5).
  • Pivotal events are indicators of potential bounded context boundaries.

Step 5: commands

  • A command describes what triggered the event or flow; it is the system's operation, in the imperative ("Publish campaign", "Roll back transaction", "Submit order").
  • Light blue
    • notes placed before the events they can produce
    • If a role executes it, add the actor (a user persona such as customer, administrator, editor) on a small yellow note (Figure 12-6: "Submit Order" by the customer, followed by "Order initialized", "Shipping cost calculated", "Order shipped")
    • Add actors only where obvious; not all commands have one.

Step 6: policies

  • Some commands have no actor:
    • look for automation policies: an event triggers a command automatically
    • Purple notes connect events to commands (Figure 12-7: "Ship Order" when "Shipment Approved" is observed).
  • If a decision criterion applies, write it on the policy note (escalate after "complaint received" only for VIP customers). Use arrows when events and commands are far apart.

Step 7: read models

  • The view of data the actor uses to decide on a command:
    • a screen, report, notification
    • Green notes with a short description of the information source, placed before the command (Figure 12-8: "Shopping cart" before the customer submits the order).

Step 8: external systems

  • Any system not part of the domain explored
    • it can execute commands (input) or be notified of events (output). Pink notes. Figure 12-9: a CRM triggers "Ship Order"
    • the approved shipment is communicated to the CRM through a policy.
  • By the end, every command is executed by an actor, triggered by a policy, or called by an external system.

Step 9: aggregates

  • Organise related concepts: an aggregate receives commands and produces events. Large yellow notes with commands on the left and events on the right (Figure 12-10).

Step 10: bounded contexts

  • Find aggregates that are related, either by closely related functionality or coupled through policies; such groups are natural bounded context boundaries (Figure 12-11).

Variants

  • Brandolini calls the process guidance, not hard rules; experiment.
  • The author usually starts with the big picture (steps 1 to 4: chaotic exploration through pivotal events): it covers a wide range of the domain, lays a ubiquitous language foundation and outlines context boundaries
    • Then he runs a dedicated session per business process with all steps.
  • The model (events, commands, aggregates, possible contexts) is a nice bonus. The real value is the process: knowledge sharing
    • aligned mental models
    • discovery of conflicting models, and the ubiquitous language.
  • The result can be the basis of an event-sourced domain model (if the domain suits it): you get context boundaries, aggregates and the blueprint of domain events.

When to use EventStorming

Purpose Why
Build a ubiquitous language Cooperating on the model instinctively synchronises terminology
Model the business process DDD-oriented building blocks reveal aggregate and context boundaries
Explore new business requirements Align everyone and reveal edge cases the requirements miss
Recover domain knowledge Knowledge gets lost, especially in legacy systems being modernised; merge participants' knowledge into one picture
Improve an existing process An end-to-end view shows inefficiencies and opportunities
Onboard new team members Expands their domain knowledge
  • When not to: the process is simple or obvious: sequential steps with no interesting logic or complexity.

Facilitation tips

  • With newcomers, start with a quick overview:
    • what you will do, the process to explore and the modelling elements
    • Build a legend (Figure 12-12) with sticky notes and labels for the colour code, visible to all during the workshop.
  • Watch the dynamics:
    • track group energy
    • if it slows, reignite with questions or advance to the next step
      • It is a group activity: give everyone a chance, and draw in those shying away with questions about the model
      • Take breaks, and do not resume until everyone is back
    • restart by walking through the current model.

Remote EventStorming

  • It was invented for co-located people
    • Brandolini often objected to remote sessions (participation, collaboration and knowledge sharing are lower)
    • The 2020 COVID-19 pandemic changed that; tools appeared, the most notable (at the time of writing) being miro.com.
  • Be more patient online (less effective communication)
    • Limit to about five participants remotely (up to 10 in person); for more knowledge, run several sessions and compare and merge the models
    • Return to in-person when possible.

Conclusion

  • A collaboration-based workshop for modelling business processes
    • the main benefit is knowledge sharing: participants synchronise mental models and take first steps towards a ubiquitous language.
  • It is "like riding a bicycle": learn by doing; you need no "black belt" to start.

Summary points

  • Ten steps from events to bounded contexts, one sticky-note colour per concept.
  • Value is in the shared learning and language, not just the model.
  • Small groups, a visible legend, and attention to energy.
Terms to carry forward
  • Domain event (orange), command (light blue), policy (purple), read model (green), external system (pink), aggregate (large yellow), pain point (pink diamond).
  • Pivotal event: marks a phase or context change.
  • Happy path: the successful flow modelled first.
