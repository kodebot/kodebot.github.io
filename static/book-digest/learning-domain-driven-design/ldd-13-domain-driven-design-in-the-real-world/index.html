Learning DDD 13: Domain-Driven Design in the Real World

Source: Vlad Khononov, Learning Domain-Driven Design (O'Reilly, 2021), ch. 13. Previous: Learning DDD 12: EventStorming. Next: Learning DDD 14: Microservices.

Takeaways
  • DDD is not only for greenfield projects with a team of experts, and not all-or-nothing. Brownfield projects benefit most.
  • Start with strategic analysis: understand the business domain, find subdomains (org chart, secret sauce, worst-designed components), then explore the current design (components, tactical choices, a context map) and recover lost knowledge with EventStorming.
  • Modernise gradually: align logical boundaries with subdomains first, then extract contexts where value is highest; tactically fix core subdomains built as scripts or active records. Use the strangler pattern with a facade, or refactor in place; avoid big rewrites.
  • Pragmatic DDD: what matters is letting the business domain drive design decisions, not using aggregates or value objects.
  • Selling DDD: use it undercover as an engineering toolbox, argue from logic and principles, and let domain experts sell event sourcing.

Reality check

  • The "laboratory conditions" (greenfield, everyone a DDD black belt, perfect boundaries, always-clean code) are about as likely as winning the lottery. Now wake up.
  • Misconception 1:
    • DDD is only for greenfield
    • Ironically brownfield projects (proved business viability, need a shake-up against technical debt and design entropy) gain most, and that is where most careers are spent: legacy big balls of mud.
  • Misconception 2: all-or-nothing. You need not apply every pattern, especially on brownfield where it is practically impossible in reasonable time.

Strategic analysis

  • Best start: understand the business strategy and the current state of the architecture.

Understand the business domain

  • Ask:
    • the organisation's business domain(s)
    • its customers
    • the service or value provided
    • competitors
      • and their products
      • This gives a bird's-eye view of high-level goals
      • Then zoom into subdomains
      • A good first heuristic is the org chart (departments and units, and how they cooperate to compete).
  • Core subdomains:
    • what differentiates the company? A "secret sauce" (patents, in-house algorithms)? Remember advantage need not be technical (hiring top personnel, unique artistic design)
    • A powerful but unfortunate heuristic: the worst-designed components, the big balls of mud engineers hate but the business will not rewrite because of risk
    • A ready-made replacement would make it generic, so it is core.
  • Generic subdomains: off-the-shelf solutions, subscription services, open source integrations that competitors can use too without affecting your business.
  • Supporting subdomains:
    • the remaining components that cannot be replaced with ready-made solutions yet give no advantage
    • Rough code triggers less emotional response since it rarely changes.
  • You need not identify all core subdomains; capture the overall structure and focus on those relevant to your systems.

Explore the current design

  • Start with high-level components (not necessarily bounded contexts): boundaries decomposing the domain into subsystems
    • Key property: decoupled lifecycles: even in a monorepo or monolith, which can be evolved, tested and deployed independently?
  • Evaluate tactical design: per component, which subdomains and which patterns for business logic and architecture? Does the solution fit the problem's complexity? Where are more elaborate patterns needed, and where can you cut corners or buy?
  • Evaluate strategic design: chart a context map treating components as contexts, with integration patterns. Look for suboptimal decisions:
    • multiple teams on one component;
    • duplicate implementations of core subdomains;
    • a core subdomain implemented by an outsourced company;
    • friction from frequently failing integration;
    • awkward models spreading from external services and legacy systems.
  • Then look for lost domain knowledge (chapter 11), acute in core subdomains. Use EventStorming to recover it and as the basis for the ubiquitous language.

Modernisation strategy

  • Big rewrites
    • are rarely successful and rarely supported by management
    • Safer: think big, start small
    • Evans: not all of a large system will be well designed; decide where to invest
    • A prerequisite is boundaries between subdomains, not necessarily physical: start with logical boundaries (namespaces, modules, packages) aligned with subdomains (Figure 13-1: reorganise modules to reflect subdomains rather than technical implementation patterns).
  • That is a relatively safe refactoring (types move, logic does not), but check references by full type name (dynamic loading, reflection)
    • Track logic in other platforms (stored procedures, serverless functions): rename procedures to reflect the module, or add a dedicated database schema and move the procedures.

Strategic modernisation

  • Premature decomposition into the smallest contexts is risky (chapter 10; microservices in chapter 14)
    • Look for where turning logical boundaries into physical ones gains most (Figure 13-2)
    • Ask:
    • Multiple teams on one codebase? Define a context per team to decouple lifecycles.
    • Conflicting models across components? Relocate them to separate contexts.
  • Then review integration between contexts: team communication, and especially ad hoc or shared-kernel-like integration: do teams have shared goals and enough collaboration?
Pattern Use when
Customer-supplier A partnership designed for many teams is no longer sustainable (chapter 11): refactor to conformist, ACL or OHS
Anticorruption layer Protect a context from legacy systems with inefficient models that spread downstream, or from frequent changes in an upstream's public interface
Open-host service Changes in one component's implementation often ripple to consumers: decouple the implementation model from the public API
Separate ways Friction between teams co-evolving shared functionality (the "apple of discord"): if it is not core, let teams build their own

Tactical modernisation

  • First, the most painful mismatches: core subdomains implemented with transaction scripts or active records. They change most yet are painful to maintain.

Cultivate a ubiquitous language

  • Domain knowledge and a good model are a prerequisite
    • Use EventStorming with domain experts to explore the legacy code, especially an undocumented mess nobody understands: gather everyone related and explore the domain.
  • Then choose logic patterns (chapter 10 heuristics) and a modernisation strategy: strangler (gradually replace whole components) or refactor in place.

Strangler pattern

  • Strangler figs are tropical trees that seed in a host tree's upper branches, grow roots to the soil and eventually overshadow and kill the host (Figure 13-3).
  • The migration pattern:
    • create a new bounded context (the strangler), implement new requirements in it and gradually migrate legacy functionality
    • Except for hotfixes and emergencies, development of the legacy context stops
    • Eventually all functionality moves and the host dies.
  • Usually paired with a facade:
    • a thin layer that is the public interface and forwards each request to the legacy or the modernised context
    • When migration completes, remove the facade with the legacy system (Figure 13-4).
  • The one database per bounded context rule can be relaxed temporarily: both contexts may share the database to avoid complex integration (often distributed transactions) because both work on the same data (Figure 13-5)
    • Condition: the legacy context will be retired soon, leaving the database to the new implementation.

Refactoring tactical design decisions

  • Two nuances, beyond chapter 11:
    1. Small incremental steps are safer. Do not go from transaction script or active record straight to an event-sourced model: first design state-based aggregates and invest in finding good boundaries with all related logic inside. Moving from state-based to event-sourced is orders of magnitude safer than discovering wrong transactional boundaries in an event-sourced aggregate.
    2. Refactoring to a domain model need not be atomic. Introduce elements gradually: first look for value objects (immutability reduces complexity even without a full domain model). For active records into aggregates: gather related logic, analyse transactional boundaries (strong consistency decisions on eventually consistent data? or strong consistency where eventual would do?). These decisions are business-driven, not technology-driven; only after analysis design aggregate boundaries.
  • When needed, protect the new code from old models with an anticorruption layer, and protect consumers from legacy changes with an open-host service and published language.

Pragmatic DDD

  • Not all-or-nothing:
    • if the tactical patterns do not suit you, use other patterns
    • It is still DDD if you analyse the domain and strategy, look for effective models, and make design decisions based on the business domain's needs.
  • "Domain-driven design is not about aggregates or value objects. It is about letting your business domain drive software design decisions."

Selling domain-driven design

  • Asked at nearly every talk:
    • how to "sell" DDD to team and management
    • The author hates selling but notes that designing software is selling (ideas to team, management, customers)
    • A methodology reaching beyond engineering is hard to sell
    • Management support is essential for big changes, yet DDD is not top of mind and looks like a large process shift
    • That does not stop you using it.

Undercover DDD

  • Make DDD part of your professional toolbox, not an organisational strategy: its patterns are engineering techniques and engineering is your job.
  • Ubiquitous language
    • (borderline common sense): listen to how stakeholders speak and gently steer terminology from technical jargon to business meaning
      • Look for inconsistent terms and ask: multiple names for one thing may mean intertwined models (make contexts explicit)
    • same meaning, ask for one term. Talk to experts a lot, informally (watercoolers and coffee breaks)
    • they are usually happy to work with engineers sincerely interested. Use the language in code and all communication
    • be patient.
  • Bounded contexts: argue from principles:
    • Why problem-oriented models rather than one for all cases? "All-in-one" solutions are rarely effective.
    • Why can't a context host conflicting models? Cognitive load and complexity.
    • Why not several teams on one codebase? Friction and hindered collaboration.
    • Do the same for integration patterns: know the problem each solves.
  • Tactical decisions: do not appeal to authority ("the DDD book says so"); appeal to logic:
Why? Because
Explicit transactional boundaries Protect data consistency
One aggregate instance per transaction Ensure correct consistency boundaries
No external modification of aggregate state Keep the related logic colocated, not duplicated
No offloading aggregate logic to a stored procedure Avoid duplicated logic going out of sync and corrupting data
Small aggregate boundaries A wide scope increases complexity and hurts performance
Events not just in a logfile No long-term data consistency guarantees
  • Event-sourced domain model
    • (hard to sell, sounds radical): let the domain decide
    • Talk to domain experts, show them state-based and event-based models and the advantages (especially the dimension of time)
    • More often than not they will be ecstatic and advocate it themselves
    • While talking, keep building the ubiquitous language.

Conclusion

  • Start as in greenfield:
    • analyse the business domain, goals and strategy
    • use organisation structure and existing design to find subdomains and types
    • plan modernisation, look for pain points and the most business value
    • refactor or replace gradually: "big rewrites entail more risk than business value".
  • You can use DDD even if the organisation has not adopted it: use the right tools and argue from the logic and principles of each pattern.
  • Part IV: DDD's interplay with other methodologies and patterns.

Exercises

  1. First step for a brownfield project: refactor everything to event-sourced / analyse the business domain and strategy / improve components into proper contexts / impossible.
  2. How does the strangler contradict DDD principles during migration (select all)? Several contexts share a database / a core subdomain's implementation is duplicated in old and new / several teams on one context.
  3. Why not refactor active record straight to an event-sourced model (select all)? State-based makes boundary refactoring easier while learning / safer to go gradually.
  4. A colleague asks why an aggregate can't reference all entities and make the whole domain traversable from one place. How do you answer? (open question)
Answers (book appendix B)
  1. B: analyse the business domain and identify subdomains and their types.
  2. A and B: shared database and duplicated core functionality contradict DDD, but the strangler makes them temporary to retire the legacy context.
  3. Both: introducing big changes gradually is safe; state-based aggregates first lets you get the boundaries right, then apply event sourcing.

Summary points

  • DDD works on brownfield; do it incrementally, pragmatically and undercover if needed.
  • Analyse (domain, subdomains, current design, lost knowledge), modernise (logical, then physical boundaries; strangler or refactor), argue from principles.
Terms to carry forward
  • Brownfield / big ball of mud: existing, entropy-laden system.
  • Strangler pattern / facade: grow a new context around the old; thin routing layer.
  • Logical versus physical boundary: namespace or module versus separately deployable.
  • Undercover DDD: using techniques without organisational rollout.
