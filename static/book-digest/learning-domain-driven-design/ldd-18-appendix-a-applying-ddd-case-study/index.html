Learning DDD 18: Appendix A, Applying DDD: A Case Study

Source: Vlad Khononov, Learning Domain-Driven Design (O'Reilly, 2021), appendix A. Previous: Learning DDD 17: Closing Words.

Takeaways
  • The author's own start-up story, Marketnovus (a defunct marketing outsourcing company), told through five bounded contexts: marketing, CRM, event crunchers, bonuses, marketing hub.
  • His DDD understanding evolved from "aggregates everywhere" to "ubiquitous language everywhere".
  • Lessons: the ubiquitous language predicts success; subdomain types change and are easy to misjudge; choose the pattern first and map it back to the subdomain type; never ignore pain; start with wide boundaries and decompose later; never split an aggregate across contexts.

About the case study

  • It shows how the author's DDD journey began
    • Marketnovus used DDD from day one, "committed every possible DDD mistake" and learned from them
    • Part I walks through five bounded contexts, decisions and outcomes; part II relates them to the book.
  • The company no longer exists, so the appendix is not promotional and he can speak honestly.

Business domain

  • Marketnovus let clients outsource all marketing chores (Figure A-1): experts devised a marketing strategy, copywriters and designers produced banners and landing pages, campaigns ran, and sales agents handled the leads by phone
    • An analysis department optimised the whole process (best campaigns, best creatives, most promising leads).
  • Being self-funded, the first system covered the first third of the value chain:
    • contracts and integrations with external publishers;
    • a catalogue of creative materials for designers;
    • campaign management.
  • Overwhelmed, the author turned to Eric Evans's Domain-Driven Design
    • Reading mainly the tactical chapters, he felt he had a strong grasp
    • Guess how the system was designed? It would make a "certain prominent individual" (footnote: @DDDBorat, a parody Twitter account of bad DDD advice) proud.

Bounded context 1: Marketing

  • Style: "aggregates everywhere": agency, campaign, placement, funnel, publisher, every noun an aggregate, all in one huge lone bounded context (a monolith).
  • They were not aggregates:
    • no transactional boundaries, almost no behaviour
    • the logic lived in an enormous service layer. This is the anemic domain model antipattern, a by-the-book case of how not to implement a domain model.
  • Yet the business called it a huge success:
    • working software in an aggressive time to market
    • "A kind of magic": a robust ubiquitous language
    • With no prior marketing experience they could talk to domain experts, who appreciated the effort to learn
    • For two developers in a garage the monolith was good enough.
  • Early DDD understanding (Figure A-2): essentially the tactical patterns.

Bounded context 2: CRM

  • Leads arrived and the sales agents needed a CRM to manage lifecycles: aggregate incoming leads, group them, distribute across sales desks worldwide
    • integrate with clients' systems (notify about lifecycle changes, enrich leads)
    • and optimise (best leads, assign leads by qualification and past performance, flexible agent commission calculation). No off-the-shelf product fit, so they built it.
  • More "aggregates":
    • again every noun, in the same monolith
    • Now something felt wrong: awkward prefixes (CRMLead and MarketingLead, MarketingCampaign and CRMCampaign) that nobody used with domain experts, who understood from context.
  • He recalled bounded contexts, which protect language consistency, and Vaughn Vernon's "Effective Aggregate Design", which exposed their aggregate mistakes: they treated aggregates as data structures, not as protectors of data consistency.

Design, take two

  • Split the monolith into marketing and CRM (bare minimum, not microservices). In CRM, a real domain model, vows:
    • each transaction affects one aggregate instance;
    • each aggregate defines its own transactional scope (no ORM doing it);
    • the service layer goes on a strict diet and logic moves into aggregates.
  • But a proper domain model is hard:
    • everything took far longer
    • getting transactional boundaries right needed several models, with the correct one often the one not yet considered. Deadlines were hopeless, so management offloaded some features to the database administrators' team to implement logic in stored procedures.
  • Tower of Babel 2.0:
    • the real damage was not SQL but an implicit bounded context cutting through the most complex entity, the Lead
    • Two teams on one component with minimal interaction: each had its own vocabulary, inconsistent models, duplicated knowledge and rules, immediately out of sync on changes
    • The project was late and buggy, with production issues that corrupted data for years
    • The fix was a complete rewrite of the Lead aggregate with proper boundaries, a couple of years later.
  • Understanding now (Figure A-3):
    • ubiquitous language, protect it with bounded contexts, and instead of anemic models everywhere, proper domain model everywhere
    • Missing: subdomains and their types
    • They wasted time building domain models for supporting subdomains ("not all of a large system will be well designed", Evans).

Bounded context 3: Event crunchers

  • After CRM they suspected an implicit subdomain spread across marketing and CRM: handling incoming customer events, so each process change hit both
    • They extracted it into event crunchers (Figure A-4).
  • It looked supporting (no money from moving data, nothing to buy), designed accordingly: layered architecture and transaction scripts. It worked, for a while.
  • Then BI asked for flags (new contact, first-time events, business invariants...), which grew into real business logic with complex rules and invariants: a full core subdomain implemented as scripts, giving a very big ball of mud
    • costs rose, quality fell. A year later they rewrote it as an event-sourced domain model, with other contexts subscribing to its events.

Bounded context 4: Bonuses

  • Sales desk managers asked to automate agents' commission calculation: monthly, a percentage of sales, reported to managers
    • Core? No: not inventing, not making money, would buy if possible; not generic either
    • Supporting: active records orchestrated by a "smart" service layer in a layered architecture (Figure A-5).
  • Once automated, everyone got creative:
    • analysts tried different percentages, tied them to amounts and prices, unlocked extra commissions for goals
    • The design broke down, the codebase became a ball of mud, bugs appeared (and in money, even small bugs have big consequences).
  • Take two:
    • rewrite from the ground up as an event-sourced domain model
    • Like event crunchers, it started supporting and mutated into core (they found ways to monetise it)
    • The difference: a ubiquitous language existed even with active records. As the experts' language grew more complex it could no longer be modelled by active records, so they noticed the need to change much earlier than with event crunchers.
  • Understanding now (Figure A-6): the classic DDD, ubiquitous language, bounded contexts and subdomain types, each designed to its needs.

Bounded context 5: The marketing hub

  • Management wanted a new profitable vertical:
    • use the ability to generate massive numbers of leads and sell them to smaller clients
    • Declared core, so they "pulled out the heavy artillery": event-sourced domain model and CQRS, plus the new buzzword, microservices (Figure A-7): small services with own databases, sync and async communication.
  • Micro what?
    • Naively "smaller is better": service boundaries around aggregates (each aggregate its own bounded context)
    • Initially fine (each service fitted to its needs; only one used event sourcing, the rest state-based; independent evolution)
    • But services became chatty: nearly each needed data from all the others, giving a distributed monolith, "an absolute nightmare to maintain".
  • The real problem:
    • despite being "core", it had no technical complexity: the logic was so simple plain active records would do
    • The business profit came from existing relationships with other companies, not clever algorithms
    • Technical complexity far exceeded business complexity: accidental complexity, over-engineered.

Marketing "Aggregates" everywhere, huge service layer (anemic) Strong ubiquitous language compensated Delivered fast; business success
CRM Domain model, but a hidden context split the Lead between two teams Two languages, duplicated rules, corrupt data Late, buggy; Lead rewritten years later

Discussion

Ubiquitous language

  • "The 'core subdomain' of domain-driven design": more effective for sharing knowledge than tests or documents, and a major predictor of project success:
    • Marketing: the robust language compensated for architectural shortcomings.
    • CRM: two languages describing one domain: a huge mess despite a proper design effort.
    • Event crunchers: no investment in language for a "simple" supporting subdomain, regretted big time as complexity grew.
    • Bonuses: logic complexity grew by orders of magnitude, but the language revealed the need to change strategy much earlier.
  • So the ubiquitous language is not optional, whether core, supporting or generic. Invest early: fixing a spoken language is very hard
    • the CRM implementation was fixed, the language was not (for years some people used the conflicting terms).

Subdomains

  • Identify them at the granularity relevant to your software. Marketing hub: the business's extra profit source, but the software was a supporting subdomain
    • the real core was leveraging relationships and contracts.
  • Subdomains also change type. At Marketnovus they saw almost every combination:
    • event crunchers and bonuses: supporting to core once monetised;
    • the creative catalogue built in-house, later replaced by a feature-richer open source project: supporting to generic;
    • the CRM's algorithm to identify the most promising leads, refined over time and finally replaced by a machine learning model in a cloud vendor's managed service: core to generic;
    • the marketing hub: core to supporting, since the competitive edge lay in another dimension.
  • Failing to identify a type is costly (event crunchers, marketing hub).

Mapping design decisions to subdomains

  • The author's trick: reverse the relationship between subdomains and tactical decisions:
    1. Choose the logic pattern that fits the requirements at hand (no speculation or gold plating).
    2. Map the pattern to a subdomain type.
    3. Verify that type against the business vision.
  • This creates an additional dialogue with the business
    • If the business says core but you can hack it in a day, look for finer-grained subdomains or question the business viability
    • If the business says supporting but it needs a domain model or event sourcing:
    • the business may have overcomplicated the requirements (accidental business complexity; simplify them);
    • or the business does not yet realise it has a competitive edge (as with bonuses): you help it find new profit sources faster.

Don't ignore pain

  • Pain
    • when implementing logic signals a need to evolve the domain model or the tactical design
    • If the subdomain evolved, rethink its type and strategy, talk to the experts, and do not fear redesign: once the choice is conscious and you know the options, refactoring to a more elaborate pattern is easier.

Boundaries of bounded contexts

Strategy used Result
Linguistic boundaries (marketing versus CRM) Protected the ubiquitous languages
Subdomain-based (event crunchers, bonuses) Worked
Entity-based (aggregate-sized services) Limited success in the marketing hub, worked elsewhere
Suicidal boundaries (dissecting the Lead aggregate into two contexts) "Never try this at home"
  • None fits everywhere
    • It was safer to extract a service from a bigger one than to start too small, so they began with bigger boundaries and decomposed as knowledge grew: the less you know the domain, the wider the initial boundaries
    • Marketing and CRM each held several subdomains and were gradually split into microservices, staying in the safe range (chapter 14) by refactoring only after gaining knowledge.

Conclusion

  • The evolved approach:
    1. Always start by building a ubiquitous language with domain experts.
    2. For conflicting models, split into bounded contexts along linguistic boundaries.
    3. Identify subdomain boundaries and types in each context.
    4. Choose an implementation strategy per subdomain with tactical heuristics.
    5. Verify the types against those the tactical design implies; discuss mismatches with the business (it sometimes changed requirements).
    6. As knowledge grows, decompose further into narrower contexts.
  • The big difference from the start: from "aggregates everywhere" to "ubiquitous language everywhere".
  • How it ended:
    • the company became profitable quickly and was acquired by its biggest client
    • Success cannot be attributed solely to DDD, but the company ran in "start-up mode" (in Israel; "chaos" elsewhere): constantly changing requirements and priorities, aggressive timelines, a tiny R&D team
    • DDD helped tackle that, so "the bet we placed on domain-driven design paid off in full".

Summary points

  • Five contexts, five lessons: language first, know the subdomain type, let pain trigger redesign, start wide, match design to real complexity.
Terms to carry forward
  • Anemic domain model: aggregates with no behaviour and logic in a service layer.
  • Distributed monolith: over-split, chatty services.
  • Accidental complexity: technical complexity above the business's.
  • Suicidal boundary: splitting an aggregate across contexts.
  • Reverse mapping: pattern first, then subdomain type, then business check.
