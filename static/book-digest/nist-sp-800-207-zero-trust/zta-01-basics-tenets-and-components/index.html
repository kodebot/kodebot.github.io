NIST SP 800-207 Zero Trust Architecture 1: Basics, Tenets and Components

Source: Scott Rose, Oliver Borchert, Stu Mitchell and Sean Connelly, Zero Trust Architecture, NIST Special Publication 800-207, August 2020 (US government publication; authors and date as printed on the PDF). The PDF is saved in the vault as books/NIST_SP_800-207_Zero_Trust_Architecture.pdf (59 pages). Own-words summary, much shorter than the source. Next: NIST SP 800-207 Zero Trust Architecture 2: Approaches, Deployment, Threats and Migration.

Takeaways
  • Zero trust is a set of ideas for making accurate, least-privilege, per-request access decisions in a network treated as already compromised. Trust is never granted implicitly; it is continually evaluated.
  • Its core is authentication and authorisation decided by a policy decision point and enforced by a policy enforcement point close to the resource.
  • Seven tenets: everything is a resource; secure all communication; per-session access; dynamic policy; monitor asset posture; dynamic enforcement; collect data to improve.
  • Logical components: policy engine, policy administrator, policy enforcement point, plus data sources such as CDM, threat intelligence, logs, PKI, identity management and SIEM.

1.1 Why zero trust

  • Traditionally an enterprise defends the perimeter:
    • once authenticated and on the internal network, a subject gets access to a broad set of resources
    • The result is that unauthorised lateral movement has been a major challenge.
  • Perimeter firewalls and internet gateways stop attackers from the internet but are less useful against attacks from inside and cannot protect subjects outside the perimeter (remote workers, cloud services, edge devices).

1.2 Definitions

  • Zero trust (ZT): concepts designed to minimise uncertainty in enforcing accurate, least-privilege, per-request access decisions in information systems and services in the face of a network viewed as compromised.
  • Zero trust architecture (ZTA):
    • an enterprise's cybersecurity plan using ZT concepts: component relationships, workflow planning, and access policies
    • A zero trust enterprise is the network infrastructure and operational policies that result from the plan.
  • The goal is granular access:
    • authorised subjects (a combination of user, application or service, and device) reach the resource to the exclusion of all others
    • "Resource" covers printers, compute and IoT actuators, not just data.
  • Uncertainty cannot be eliminated, so the focus is authentication, authorisation and shrinking implicit trust zones, while keeping availability and minimising delay.

1.3 The basic access model

  • A subject requests a resource. Access is granted through a policy decision point (PDP) and a policy enforcement point (PEP).
  • The questions asked per request:
    • how confident are we in the subject's identity
    • is access allowed at that confidence
    • does the device have the right security posture
    • do other factors (time, location, behaviour) change the confidence?
  • Implicit trust zone:
    • the area beyond a PEP where all entities share the same level of trust
    • The NIST analogy is an airport: after the security checkpoint (PDP/PEP), everyone in the terminal is treated as trusted
    • The zone should be as small as possible, and zero trust moves PDP/PEPs closer to the resource.

1.4 The seven tenets

# Tenet Meaning
1 All data sources and computing services are resources including small devices, SaaS, actuators, and personal devices that reach enterprise resources
2 All communication is secured regardless of network location being on the enterprise network earns no trust; protect confidentiality, integrity and source authentication
3 Access is per session trust in the requester is evaluated before access, with least privilege; access to one resource does not grant access to another
4 Access is determined by dynamic policy identity (user or service account and attributes), application or service, and requesting asset state (software versions, location, time, behaviour), plus environmental attributes (network location, reported attacks)
5 The enterprise monitors the integrity and posture of all assets no asset is inherently trusted; continuous diagnostics and mitigation (CDM) or similar; subverted, vulnerable or unmanaged assets may be denied
6 Authentication and authorisation are dynamic and strictly enforced before access a constant cycle of access, scanning, adaptation and re-evaluation; identity, credential and access management (ICAM) and MFA
7 The enterprise collects as much information as possible about assets, network and communications used to improve policy and give context to requests
  • The tenets are the ideal; not every tenet may be fully implemented. They concern work inside an organisation and with partners, not anonymous public or consumer-facing processes.

1.5 A zero trust view of a network

  1. The entire enterprise private network is not an implicit trust zone; act as if an attacker is present; authenticate all connections and encrypt all traffic.
  2. Devices on the network may not be owned or configurable by the enterprise (visitors, contractors, BYOD).
  3. No resource is inherently trusted: every asset's posture is evaluated by a PEP before a request is granted, continually; credentials alone are not enough to authenticate a device.
  4. Not all resources are on enterprise infrastructure (cloud services, remote subjects).
  5. Remote subjects and assets cannot trust their local network; assume it is hostile and traffic is monitored or modified.
  6. Assets and workflows moving between enterprise and non-enterprise infrastructure keep a consistent security policy and posture.

1.6 Logical components

Component Role
Policy engine (PE) makes the decision to grant, deny or revoke, using enterprise policy and external inputs in a trust algorithm; logs the decision
Policy administrator (PA) executes it: establishes or shuts down the communication path via the PEP, and generates session-specific credentials or tokens
Policy enforcement point (PEP) enables, monitors and eventually terminates connections; may be split into a client-side agent and a resource-side gateway, or be a single portal
  • The components talk over a control plane; application data travels over a data plane. The PE and PA may be treated as one service in some implementations.
  • Data sources that feed the policy engine:
    • CDM system: state of assets (patched OS, integrity of approved software, known vulnerabilities).
    • Industry compliance system: regulatory rules.
    • Threat intelligence feeds: new attacks and flaws.
    • Network and system activity logs.
    • Data access policies: attributes, rules and privileges, based on mission roles.
    • Enterprise PKI: certificates for resources, subjects, services and applications.
    • ID management system: accounts and identity records (such as an LDAP server).
    • SIEM: security-centric data for analysis.

Summary points

  • Never trust by location; decide per request, per session, with least privilege.
  • PE decides, PA configures, PEP enforces; many data sources inform the decision.
  • Keep the implicit trust zone as small as you can.
Terms to carry forward
  • PDP / PEP / PE / PA: policy decision point, enforcement point, policy engine, policy administrator.
  • Implicit trust zone: the area where entities share the trust level of the last enforcement point.
  • CDM: continuous diagnostics and mitigation.
  • ICAM: identity, credential and access management.
  • NPE: non-person entity (a service account or software agent).
