Site Reliability Engineering 1: Being On-Call

Source: Andrea Spadaccini, "Being On-Call" (chapter 11), in Site Reliability Engineering (Copyright 2017 Google, Inc., published by O'Reilly Media; licensed CC BY-NC-ND 4.0). Own-words summary for personal study; published to the website at the user's instruction (2026-11-27). Next: Site Reliability Engineering 2: Emergency Response.

Takeaways
  • Google SREs cap operational work at 50% of their time, and on-call at 25%; the rest goes to engineering that removes toil.
  • Balance on-call by quantity (percentage of time) and quality (incidents per shift, with time to follow up).
  • Stress pushes people towards intuitive, habitual action; escalation paths, an incident process and blameless postmortems keep them rational.
  • Too much load needs fixing (even "giving back the pager"); too little load is also a danger, because people lose touch with production.

1.1 What on-call means

  • On-call engineers are available within minutes to operate production, manage outages and vet production changes, within response times agreed with the service owners: typically 5 minutes for user-facing or time-critical services and 30 minutes for less time-sensitive ones.
  • Response time follows from availability: a 99.99% quarterly target allows about 13 minutes of downtime, so the reaction time must be in minutes.
  • After acknowledging a page, the engineer triages, works towards resolution, involves others and escalates as needed
    • Paging events take priority over almost everything, including project work
    • Non-paging events (lower-priority alerts, releases) can wait for business hours.
  • Teams often run a primary and a secondary rotation:
    • the secondary may be a fall-through for missed pages or may handle non-urgent production work
    • Two related teams can serve as each other's secondary.

1.2 Balanced on-call

Quantity

  • At least 50% of an SRE's time is engineering; of the remainder, no more than 25% on-call, and up to 25% other operational non-project work.
  • From that rule: a single-site team covering 24/7 with a primary and a secondary on week-long shifts needs at least eight engineers (each on-call one week a month).
  • Multi-site "follow the sun" teams avoid night shifts (which harm health) and keep rotations small enough that people stay in touch with production, at the cost of communication and coordination overhead.

Quality

  • An incident is the set of events and alerts sharing one root cause that would be discussed in one postmortem
    • Each shift must leave time to handle incidents and follow-ups such as postmortems
    • If load is exceeded for a time, take corrective action to restore a sustainable level.

Compensation

  • Pay for out-of-hours work, as time off in lieu or cash capped at a proportion of salary. The cap is in effect a limit on how much on-call any one person takes on.

1.3 Feeling safe

  • Research on thinking (Kahneman) contrasts fast, intuitive action with slow, deliberate reasoning
    • For outages in complex systems the deliberate mode works better, but stress hormones push towards habit and heuristics: for example, ignoring an alert that fired three times from an external system.
  • Intuition can be wrong and wastes time chasing a bad line of reasoning; habitual responses can be disastrous.
  • Support that keeps on-call people calm:
    • Clear escalation paths (developer teams also run a 24/7 rotation and can be reached).
    • A formal incident-management protocol when several teams are involved or the end is not in sight (Site Reliability Engineering 3: Managing Incidents).
    • A blameless postmortem culture focused on events, not people (Site Reliability Engineering 4: Postmortem Culture).

1.4 Operational overload

  • Measure it, set quantified goals (the chapter's examples:
    • fewer than 5 tickets a day, fewer than 2 paging events per shift) and put them in quarterly plans
    • A temporarily loaned experienced SRE can give a team room.
  • Bad monitoring
    • is a common cause
    • Paging alerts should match symptoms that threaten the SLOs, and every page must be actionable
    • Hourly low-priority alerts cause fatigue and make serious alerts get less attention
    • Group related alerts to limit fan-out; silence duplicates during an incident.
  • If developers' changes make a system noisier or less reliable, set common goals with them
    • In extreme cases SRE can give back the pager: the developers are on-call exclusively until the system meets SRE's standards
    • It is rare because working with developers almost always works.

1.5 Operational underload

  • Too quiet a system is also a risk: gaps in knowledge and over- or under-confidence show up only during incidents.
  • Size teams so each engineer is on-call once or twice a quarter, and run "Wheel of Misfortune" role-play exercises to sharpen troubleshooting.

Summary points

  • 50% engineering, at most 25% on-call, with enough people to sustain it.
  • Keep pages rare, actionable and grouped.
  • Support calm, deliberate thinking with escalation, process and blamelessness.
Terms to carry forward
  • Primary / secondary on-call: the first responder and the fall-through or non-urgent handler.
  • Follow the sun: a multi-site rotation that avoids night shifts.
  • Giving back the pager: handing on-call to developers until the system is reliable enough.
  • Operational underload / overload: too little or too much production work.
  • Wheel of Misfortune: a role-played incident exercise.
