Site Reliability Engineering 2: Emergency Response

Source: Corey Adam Baye, "Emergency Response" (chapter 13), in Site Reliability Engineering (Copyright 2017 Google, Inc., published by O'Reilly Media; licensed CC BY-NC-ND 4.0). Own-words summary for personal study; published to the website. Previous: Site Reliability Engineering 1: Being On-Call. Next: Site Reliability Engineering 3: Managing Incidents.

Takeaways
  • First: do not panic; pull in more people if you feel overwhelmed and follow the incident process.
  • Three real Google emergencies, each triggered differently (a test, a configuration change, an automation run), were handled with the same habits: stay calm, escalate, communicate, then fix the system.
  • Lessons recur: test your rollbacks, canary even changes that look safe, put sanity checks in automation, keep out-of-band communications and tools.
  • Keep a history of outages, ask "what if" questions, and test failures on purpose.

2.1 What to do when systems break

  • Do not panic:
    • you are trained, and usually only electrons are in peril
    • If overwhelmed, pull in more people, even the whole company if necessary, and use your incident response process (Site Reliability Engineering 3: Managing Incidents).

2.2 Test-induced emergency

  • What happened:
    • SREs wanted to flush out hidden dependencies by blocking access to one test database out of a hundred in a large distributed MySQL system
    • Within minutes, many services reported users unable to reach key systems, some intermittently.
  • Response:
    • SRE assumed the test was responsible and aborted it
    • Rolling back the permissions change failed, so they restored permissions to replicas and failovers by an already tested method, while reaching out to developers to fix the flaw in a database application-layer library
    • Access was fully restored within an hour of the decision.
  • What went well:
    • dependent services escalated immediately
    • the test was aborted at once
    • parallel efforts (some teams reconfigured to avoid the database) helped
    • follow-up fixes were quick and periodic retesting was set up.
  • What was learned:
    • A well-reviewed test still missed an interaction among dependent systems.
    • The incident response process, only weeks old, had not been disseminated and was not followed.
    • Rollback procedures had not been tested, which lengthened the outage. Now they must be tested before such large tests.

2.3 Change-induced emergency

  • What happened:
    • a configuration change to infrastructure that protects services from abuse was pushed globally on a Friday. It triggered a crash-loop bug across essentially all externally facing systems
    • because Google's internal tools depend on its own services, many internal tools broke too.
  • Response:
    • alerts fired within seconds
    • Some on-call engineers moved to dedicated secure rooms with backup production access
    • Within five minutes the pusher rolled the change back and services began to recover; within ten minutes the on-callers declared an incident and notified the company
    • A few services had unrelated bugs triggered by the incident and needed more work.
  • What went well:
    • monitoring detected it immediately (though alerts were so loud they overwhelmed on-callers)
    • incident updates were clear and frequent
    • out-of-band communication and alternative access tools kept people working
    • a rate limit on full updates to new clients throttled the crash loop
    • and some luck (the pusher happened to be watching live complaints).
  • What was learned:
    • An earlier canary of the feature was thorough but did not hit a rare keyword combination
      • this change was judged not risky, so it got a weaker canary. Canarying and automation moved up in priority: canary thoroughly regardless of perceived risk.
    • Alert storms disrupt real work and communication.
    • Debugging tools themselves sat behind crash-looping jobs.

2.4 Process-induced emergency

  • What happened:
    • during routine automation testing, two consecutive turndown requests for the same soon-to-be-decommissioned installation were submitted
    • A subtle bug made the second request send all machines in all such installations worldwide to the queue for disk wiping.
  • Response:
    • on-callers drained traffic from the first location, then saw pages everywhere and disabled all team automation to stop further damage
    • Within an hour traffic was diverted to larger installations (some network links needed mitigation)
    • The first rebuilt installation was back within three hours
      • hand-offs between US and European teams and a streamlined manual reinstall (split into three stages) restored most capacity in three days.
  • What went well: large installations were unaffected; incident response protocols, matured over a year, produced excellent company-wide collaboration.
  • What was learned:
    • The automation lacked sanity checks: an empty rack filter was passed on as "all machines". The chapter notes that sometimes zero does mean all.
    • Reinstallation was slow and unreliable: low-priority TFTP transfers from distant locations, BIOS behaviour on failures, and infrastructure that could not set up thousands of machines at once (a regression limited tasks per worker).

2.5 All problems have solutions

  • A solution exists even if not obvious. If you cannot think of one, widen the net and involve teammates
    • the first priority is resolving the issue quickly, then cleaning up and writing it up.

2.6 Learn from the past

  • Keep a history of outages: be thorough and honest, publish postmortems, and hold people accountable for follow-ups so a near-identical outage does not recur.
  • Ask the big "what if" questions: power failure, flooded racks, primary datacentre dark, a compromised web server. Who do you call, who pays, is there a plan?
  • Test proactively: until a system fails you do not know how it, its dependencies and users will react. Better a planned test with your best people at hand than a failure at 2 a.m.

Summary points

  • Panic costs time; escalate early.
  • Test rollbacks and canaries; automation needs sanity checks.
  • Learn from every outage and rehearse before one happens.
Terms to carry forward
  • Canary: release to a small slice first to catch problems.
  • Out-of-band communication: a path that works when your normal tools do not.
  • Sanity check: a guard that rejects impossible or empty inputs.
  • Test-, change-, process-induced emergency: three triggers named in the chapter.
