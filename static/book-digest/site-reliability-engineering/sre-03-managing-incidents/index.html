Site Reliability Engineering 3: Managing Incidents

Source: Andrew Stribblehill, "Managing Incidents" (chapter 14), in Site Reliability Engineering (Copyright 2017 Google, Inc., published by O'Reilly Media; licensed CC BY-NC-ND 4.0). Own-words summary for personal study; published to the website. Previous: Site Reliability Engineering 2: Emergency Response. Next: Site Reliability Engineering 4: Postmortem Culture.

Takeaways
  • Unmanaged incidents spiral because of tunnel focus on the technical problem, poor communication and freelancing.
  • Google's process is based on the Incident Command System (the chapter points to the US National Incident Management System). Roles: incident commander, operations lead, communications lead, planning lead.
  • Essentials: clear roles, a recognised command post, a live incident document, and an explicit live handoff.
  • Declare early (second team needed, customer-visible, or unsolved after an hour) and practise the process on non-incident changes.

Note on terminology (mine, not the book's): the book's structure is the Incident Command System, not the UK gold, silver and bronze command structure (described at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold–silver–bronze_command_structure); the role names differ.

3.1 An unmanaged incident

  • The chapter's story:
    • Mary, on call, sees one datacentre stop serving, then a second, then a third. She keeps trying fixes alone
    • rollback does not help
    • a developer is woken at 3:30 a.m. her time
    • executives phone Mary's boss and ask for ETAs
    • and a well-meaning engineer deploys a "simple" CPU affinity change without telling anyone, which kills the remaining servers.
  • Three hazards:
    • Sharp focus on the technical problem: Mary was too busy fixing to think about mitigation strategy.
    • Poor communication: nobody knew what colleagues were doing; leaders were angry; other engineers went unused.
    • Freelancing: an uncoordinated change made a bad situation worse. Everyone acted in good faith.

3.2 Elements of the process

  • Process exists to channel enthusiastic people. Google's is based on the Incident Command System, chosen for clarity and scalability.
  • Recursive separation of responsibilities:
    • everyone knows their role and does not stray into others', which paradoxically gives more autonomy because nobody second-guesses colleagues
    • A role holder who is overloaded asks the planning lead for staff and delegates, possibly creating sub-incidents.
Role Responsibility
Incident commander holds the high-level state; builds and assigns the response team by need and priority; holds every role not delegated; can remove roadblocks
Operations lead applies operational tools; the only group modifying the system during the incident
Communications the public face; periodic updates to the response team and stakeholders (usually email); keeps the incident document accurate
Planning supports Ops on longer-term matters: filing bugs, ordering food, arranging handoffs, tracking how the system diverged from normal so it can be reverted
  • A recognised command post:
    • a central "war room" or, for teams working at desks, email and chat. Google found IRC reliable and useful as a log of communications
    • bots recorded incident traffic for postmortems and logged alerts to the channel.
  • Live incident state document:
    • the commander's most important duty
      • Keep it editable by several people at once, on a tool that does not depend on the system being fixed (the chapter says most teams use Google Docs, whereas the Docs SRE team use Sites)
      • It may be messy but must be functional
    • use a template and put the important information at the top
    • retain it for postmortem analysis.
  • Clear, live handoff: hand over the commander role at the end of the day by phone or video, say explicitly "You're now the incident commander, okay?" and do not leave the call until the receiver acknowledges.

3.3 The same incident, managed

  • Mary asks Sabrina to take command and briefs her
    • Sabrina emails a prearranged list, records Mary's impact assessment in a live document, updates executives by email thread (without drowning them in detail), asks an external communications person to draft user messaging, and brings in the developer on call once Mary agrees.
  • Late helpers read the incident document and are told to prioritise Mary's delegated tasks and keep her informed
    • At 5 p.m
    • Sabrina arranges replacements; a 5:45 phone conference and a 6 p.m
    • handoff pass the incident to a sister office, which mitigates and closes it, and starts the postmortem, while Mary plans structural improvements.

3.4 When to declare an incident

  • Declare early, then close it if a simple fix appears; spinning up the framework hours in is worse.
  • The author's team treats an event as an incident if any of these is true:
    • a second team must be involved in the fix;
    • the outage is visible to customers;
    • the issue is unsolved after an hour of concentrated analysis.
  • Skills atrophy without use, so apply the framework to other operational changes that span teams or time zones, and to disaster-recovery exercises.

3.5 Best practices (the chapter's summary)

Practice Meaning
Prioritise stop the bleeding, restore service, preserve evidence for root-causing
Prepare write procedures in advance with the people who take part
Trust give each participant full autonomy within their role
Introspect watch your own emotional state; ask for support if you feel panicky
Consider alternatives periodically re-evaluate whether to continue or change tack
Practise use the process routinely so it becomes second nature
Change it around rotate roles so everyone knows each one
  • Result reported: a planned, scalable and regularly used process reduced mean time to recovery and made emergent work less stressful.

Summary points

  • Separate roles; one person coordinates, a different group changes the system.
  • Keep a live document and hand off explicitly.
  • Declare early and practise.
Terms to carry forward
  • Incident Command System (ICS): the structured emergency-response model the process is based on.
  • Freelancing: making uncoordinated changes during an incident.
  • Live incident document: the running record kept by the commander.
  • Sub-incident: a delegated piece of a larger incident.
