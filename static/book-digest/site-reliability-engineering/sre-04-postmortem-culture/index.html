Site Reliability Engineering 4: Postmortem Culture

Source: John Lunney and Sue Lueder, "Postmortem Culture: Learning from Failure" (chapter 15), in Site Reliability Engineering (Copyright 2017 Google, Inc., published by O'Reilly Media; licensed CC BY-NC-ND 4.0). Own-words summary for personal study; published to the website. Previous: Site Reliability Engineering 3: Managing Incidents.

Takeaways
  • A postmortem is a written record of an incident: impact, mitigation actions, root causes and follow-up actions to prevent recurrence. Its aim is learning, not punishment.
  • Set postmortem criteria in advance (user-visible downtime, any data loss, on-call intervention, long resolution, monitoring failure); any stakeholder may also request one.
  • Blameless: assume everyone acted in good faith with the information they had; fix systems and processes, not people.
  • No postmortem left unreviewed; share widely, reward good postmortems and ask for feedback on the process.

4.1 Why postmortems

  • At Google's scale and velocity of change incidents are inevitable. Without a formal way to learn from them they may recur, multiply or cascade.
  • Primary goals:
    • document the incident, understand all contributing root causes, and put effective preventive actions in place to reduce the likelihood or impact of recurrence
    • Teams choose whatever root-cause technique suits their service.

4.2 When to write one

  • Expected after any significant undesirable event. The chapter's example triggers:
    • user-visible downtime or degradation beyond a threshold;
    • data loss of any kind;
    • on-call intervention (a release rollback, rerouting traffic);
    • resolution time above a threshold;
    • a monitoring failure (usually meaning someone found it manually).
  • Define the criteria before an incident so everyone knows.

4.3 Blameless

  • A blameless postmortem finds contributing causes without indicting any individual or team, assuming good intentions. Fear of punishment makes people hide problems.
  • The culture came from healthcare and aviation, where mistakes can be fatal: each mistake is a chance to strengthen the system
    • You cannot "fix" people, but you can fix systems and processes so people make the right choices.
  • Constructive example from the chapter: instead of complaining that "we need to rewrite the entire backend, I'll rewrite it myself if I get paged again", write that a rewrite action item could end the pages and that the maintenance manual is long and hard to train on, so future on-callers would benefit.
  • Do not stigmatise a person or team for producing many postmortems; blame leads to incidents being swept under the rug.

4.4 Collaboration, review and sharing

  • Use a real-time collaborative document with open comments and email notifications, in a template.
  • Review criteria:
    • was key incident data collected
    • are impact assessments complete
    • was the root cause deep enough
    • is the action plan appropriate and are fixes prioritised
    • was the outcome shared with stakeholders?
  • Share the first draft internally, have senior engineers review, then publish to the widest audience that benefits, with no end-user identifying data.
  • No postmortem left unreviewed:
    • an unreviewed postmortem might as well not exist
    • Hold review sessions to close discussions and finalise action items, then add the document to a repository of past incidents.

4.5 Building the culture

  • Needs continuous cultivation and senior management taking part in review and collaboration; ideally it is driven by engineers' own motivation.
  • Activities:
    • a postmortem of the month in a newsletter
    • a group for sharing and discussing internal and external postmortems
    • reading clubs (often on old incidents)
    • the Wheel of Misfortune, where new SREs replay a past postmortem with the original incident commander attending.
  • Meeting the "too costly" objection:
    • ease postmortems in with a trial period of successful ones
    • reward and celebrate writing good ones, publicly and in performance management
    • get senior leaders to acknowledge them.
  • Visible reward: the chapter describes an engineer whose release briefly took a critical service down for four minutes (because he rolled back immediately) receiving peer bonuses and being featured at the company all-hands session on "The Art of the Postmortem".
  • Survey teams on whether the culture supports their work, whether postmortems are too much toil, and what tools they want.

4.6 Ongoing improvement

  • Google reports fewer outages and a better user experience thanks to the investment
    • A working group pools templates, automates postmortem creation from incident tool data, and extracts data for trend analysis; the template gained metadata fields
    • Future work: use machine learning to predict weaknesses and reduce duplicate incidents
    • Etsy's open-source tool Morgue is named for managing postmortems.

Summary points

  • Criteria first, then blameless writing, review and sharing.
  • Fix the system, not the person.
  • Reward the behaviour you want and keep the repository searchable.
Terms to carry forward
  • Postmortem: written record of an incident and its follow-ups.
  • Blameless: focus on contributing causes, assuming good intent.
  • Postmortem criteria: pre-agreed triggers for writing one.
  • Wheel of Misfortune: a replayed incident exercise.
  • Morgue: Etsy's tool for managing postmortems.
