Software Architecture: The Hard Parts 3: Architectural Modularity

Source: Neal Ford, Mark Richards, Pramod Sadalage and Zhamak Dehghani, Software Architecture: The Hard Parts (O'Reilly, 2021), chapter 3. Previous: Software Architecture: The Hard Parts 2: Discerning Coupling in Software Architecture. Next: Software Architecture: The Hard Parts 4: Architectural Decomposition.

Takeaways
  • "Because nothing else works" is not a business case. Build one by understanding the benefits of modularity, matching them to the issues you face, and analysing the trade-offs.
  • Do not break a system apart without clear business drivers: speed-to-market and competitive advantage.
  • Five architecture characteristics support them: maintainability, testability, deployability, scalability, availability (fault tolerance); the first three make up agility.
  • Modularity does not always mean a distributed architecture: a modular monolith or microkernel also gives maintainability, testability and deployability through component structure.
  • Benefits erode as services communicate more: testing scope, deployment risk, scalability and fault tolerance all suffer when services are chatty or synchronously dependent ("big ball of distributed mud").

3.1 The business case problem

  • Opening scene:
    • the business sponsors say they cannot continue the support line without a working application. Addison and Austen believe breaking the application apart would solve the issues, but cannot justify it
    • Logan asks what assurance they have that it will accomplish more than spending money and time.
  • Logan's advice: to build a good case, understand the benefits of architectural modularity, match them to the current issues, and analyse and document the trade-offs.
  • Business change drivers:
    • mergers and acquisitions, competition, consumer demand, innovation (automation through machine learning and AI)
    • Technical change drivers: containerisation, cloud infrastructure, DevOps, continuous delivery pipelines.
  • Software architecture is meant to be stable like a building's structure, but "unlike the structural architecture of a building, software architecture must constantly change and adapt".
  • Mergers and acquisitions
    • bring more customers and so need scalability, agility and extensibility, all architectural concerns
    • Large monoliths fill up machine resources (threads, memory, CPU) quickly.
  • The water glass analogy
    • (for business stakeholders and C-level executives who pay for the refactoring): the glass is the server or VM, the water the application
    • As a monolith grows the glass fills (Figure 3-1); adding another glass does nothing because it would hold the same amount of water
    • Splitting the water into two parts across two glasses gives 50% more capacity (Figure 3-2).
  • Agility:
    • a Forbes (January 2020, David Benjamin and David Komlos) quote is cited about winners making "bold and decisive course-corrections"
    • Business may change direction fast, but technology staff must be able to implement it fast enough.

3.2 Modularity drivers

  • Do not break a system into smaller parts "unless clear business drivers exist". Primary business drivers: speed-to-market (time-to-market) and competitive advantage.
  • Speed-to-market comes from agility, the ability to respond quickly to change. Agility is a compound characteristic made of maintainability, testability and deployability.
  • Competitive advantage comes from speed-to-market combined with scalability and overall availability and fault tolerance (the ability to fail and continue operating, so other parts keep working).
  • The five key characteristics for agility, speed-to-market and competitive advantage: availability (fault tolerance), scalability, deployability, testability, maintainability (Figure 3-3).
  • Modularity is not always distribution.
    • A modular monolith groups components into well-formed domains, a domain partitioned architecture (Fundamentals of Software Architecture, Chapter 8)
    • A microkernel partitions functionality into plug-in components, giving a smaller testing and deployment scope
    • Both can achieve maintainability, testability and deployability.

3.3 Maintainability

  • Maintainability
    • is the ease of adding, changing or removing features, plus internal changes: maintenance patches, framework upgrades, third-party upgrades
    • As a composite characteristic it is hard to define objectively.
  • Alexander von Zitzewitz
    • (software architect, founder of hello2morrow) wrote an article on a metric for the maintainability level
    • Its initial form, as extracted from the page:
      • ML = 100 * sum of c_i for i = 1..k, where ML is the maintainability level of the system (0 to 100%), k the number of logical components and c_i the coupling level of each component, focusing on incoming coupling
      • the page text may have lost further terms (unverified)
    • The point: the higher the incoming coupling between components, the lower the maintainability.
  • Typical component-based metrics:
Metric Meaning
Component coupling The degree and manner in which components know about one another
Component cohesion The degree and manner in which a component's operations interrelate
Cyclomatic complexity The overall level of indirection and nesting within a component
Component size The number of aggregated statements of code in a component
Technical versus domain partitioning Components aligned by technical usage or by domain purpose (Appendix A)
  • A component here is an architectural building block doing a business or infrastructure function, usually a package (Java), namespace (C#) or directory grouping
    • Order History might be app.business.order.history.
  • Large layered monoliths have low maintainability:
    • technical partitioning, tight coupling, weak domain cohesion
    • Example: adding an expiration date to wish-list items
    • In a layered monolith (Figure 3-4) the change scope is the application level, touching every layer and needing at least three teams:
    1. a user-interface team member to add the expiry field to the screen
    2. a back-end team member for the business rules and contract changes
    3. a database team member to add the expiry column to the Wishlist table
  • In a service-based architecture (Figure 3-5) the change is at domain level within one domain service
    • in microservices (Figure 3-6) it is at function level, isolated to the wish-list service. As modularity increases, maintainability increases.

3.4 Testability

  • Testability is the ease of testing (usually automated) and the completeness of testing; it is essential to agility.
  • Large layered monoliths support low testability: full regression across a large deployment unit is hard, and a small change may force hundreds or thousands of unit tests, with the developer researching dozens of failures unrelated to the change.
  • Modularity reduces the testing scope and makes test suites smaller, more targeted and easier to maintain.
  • Caveat
    • (Figure 3-7): with three self-contained services, a change to Service A limits testing to A
    • But as services communicate more, the testing scope for A grows to include B and C, so testability falls rapidly.

3.5 Deployability

  • Deployability covers ease, frequency and risk of deployment. Deploying every two weeks or less often bundles changes (raising risk) and delays ready features and fixes
    • frequency must be balanced with users' ability to absorb change.
  • Monoliths score low: ceremony (code freezes, mock deployments), higher risk something else breaks, and weeks to months between deployments.
  • Separately deployed units mean less ceremony and risk and more frequent deployment
    • But deployability also declines as services shrink and communicate more, from fear of breaking other services.
  • Matt Stine (article on orchestrating microservices): "If your microservices must be deployed as a complete set in a specific order, please put them back in a monolith and save yourself some pain." The result is the "big ball of distributed mud" where few benefits of modularity are realised.

3.6 Scalability and elasticity

  • Scalability:
    • remaining responsive as user load increases gradually over time
    • Elasticity: remaining responsive during significant instantaneous, erratic spikes (Figure 3-8)
    • Scalability follows normal company growth; elasticity is the immediate reaction to a spike.
  • Concert-ticketing example:
    • light load between events, then 20 to 3,000 concurrent users in seconds when tickets go on sale
    • The system needs peak capacity and the ability to start extra services instantly
    • Elasticity relies on a very small mean time to startup (MTTS), achieved with very small, fine-grained services, further managed by small lightweight platforms and runtimes.
  • Elasticity is more a function of granularity (deployment unit size); scalability more a function of modularity (separate deployment units).
  • Star ratings from Fundamentals of Software Architecture (Figure 3-9), read from the figure:
Architecture Scalability Elasticity Scope
Monolithic (layered) 1 star 1 star Application-level scalability
Service-based 3 stars 2 stars Domain-level scalability, fair MTTS
Microservices 5 stars 5 stars Function-level scalability, excellent MTTS
  • Monoliths are hard and expensive to scale because all functionality scales to the same degree, costly in the cloud
    • Service-based services are coarse-grained, usually a whole domain in one unit (order processing, warehouse management), so MTTS is too long for fast elasticity
    • Microservices maximise both.
  • As with testability and deployability, more inter-service communication per business transaction hurts scalability and elasticity, so keep synchronous communication to a minimum when you need both.

3.7 Availability and fault tolerance

  • Defined here as the ability of some parts of the system to stay responsive as other parts fail
    • Example: a fatal error such as out-of-memory in payment processing should not stop users searching for items and placing orders.
  • All monoliths have low fault tolerance
    • Running several load-balanced instances of the whole application is expensive and ineffective: a programming bug exists in both instances and can take both down.
  • Modularity gives domain-level and function-level fault tolerance by isolating catastrophic failure to one deployment unit.
  • Caveat:
    • if other services are synchronously dependent on a failing service, fault tolerance is not achieved
    • That is why asynchronous communication is essential in distributed systems.

3.8 Sysops Squad saga: creating a business case

Addison and Austen match each business-sponsor complaint to a modularity driver.

Issue Finding Driver
Changes break other things, take too long; large codebase hard to navigate Over 30% of test cases are commented out or obsolete, and critical workflow parts lack tests; the whole suite must run for any change Maintainability and testability
Monthly releases piled with untested changes; mock deployments and code freezes Deployment risk is high so Parker insists on monthly releases; Austen calls it a pipeline issue, Addison says separate services would also shrink scope and risk; Austen still expects pipeline changes Deployability
System unavailable, lost tickets, routing issues, customers cancelling plans Metrics show it is customer survey and reporting, not core ticketing, that bring the system down Availability / fault tolerance: isolate them from ticketing
System freezes Analysis by Sydney: it freezes when more than 25 customers create tickets at once, and when operational reports run during ticket entry Scalability and database load: separate reporting and scale ticketing

ADR: Migrate Sysops Squad application to a distributed architecture

  • Context:
    • the monolithic problem-ticket application supports customer registration, ticket entry and processing, operational and analytical reporting, billing and payment, and administrative maintenance
    • it has scalability, availability and maintainability issues.
  • Decision: migrate to a distributed architecture to:
    • make core ticketing more available for external customers (fault tolerance)
    • scale for customer growth and ticket creation, resolving the freezes
    • separate reporting and its database load, resolving the freezes
    • let teams ship features and fixes faster (agility)
    • reduce bugs when changes occur (testability)
    • deploy weekly or even daily (deployability)
  • Consequences:
    • delays to new features because most developers are needed for the migration
    • additional cost (estimates to be determined)
    • until the deployment pipeline changes, release engineers manage several deployment units
    • the monolithic database must be broken apart
  • The sponsors approved and told them to proceed.

Summary points

  • Justify modularity with business drivers (speed-to-market, competitive advantage) mapped to five characteristics.
  • Modularity is not automatically distribution; chatty or synchronous services erode testability, deployability, scalability and fault tolerance.
  • Scalability follows modularity; elasticity follows granularity and MTTS.
  • Record the decision and its consequences, including the database split to come.
Terms to carry forward
  • Modularity drivers: speed-to-market, competitive advantage.
  • Agility: maintainability + testability + deployability.
  • Scalability vs elasticity: gradual growth vs sudden spikes; modularity vs granularity.
  • MTTS: mean time to startup.
  • Big ball of distributed mud: services that must deploy together in order.
  • Change scope: application, domain or function level.
