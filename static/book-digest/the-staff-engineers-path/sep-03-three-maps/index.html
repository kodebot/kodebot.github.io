The Staff Engineer's Path 3: Three Maps

Source: Tanya Reilly, The Staff Engineer's Path (O'Reilly, 2022), chapter 2. Own-words summary, much shorter than the source.

Takeaways
  • Build three maps of the big picture: a locator map (where you are, for perspective), a topographical map (the organisational terrain and how decisions are made) and a treasure map (where everyone is going and why).
  • The maps already exist but are hidden by "fog of war"; clear it by paying attention, gaining access to decisions and talking to people, including outside engineering.
  • Organisational skills are good engineering, not "politics": being right is only the beginning, and you must convince the right people.
  • If the terrain is hard, be a bridge; if there is no treasure map, draw one (chapter 3).

3.1 Three maps and the fog of war

  • A single map cannot show elevation, voting districts and subways, so use three simple ones, as a mental exercise or drawn with a colleague (compare where you disagree).
  • Fog of war: like a strategy game, you cannot see unexplored parts; starting a job is mostly building context.
  • Maps help others too: the locator map shows teams their purpose and customers, the topographical map exposes friction and gaps, the treasure map aligns everyone on what and why.
  • Skill: paying attention.
    • Reilly's example: on walks in Ireland her friends saw a pine marten's footprint and peppery edible leaves she would have missed, because they knew what to look for.
    • Train yourself to say "that's interesting" and remember facts:
      • an all-hands about a marketing push may mean a traffic spike
      • a director asks you to take a project you have no time for, and you know who is ready to stretch
      • a database disappearing and an email about network maintenance.
    • If your memory is not sticky, note useful facts down as a habit.
  • Opportunity: you also need access to the discussions that matter.

3.2 The locator map: getting perspective

  • The more absorbed you are in a domain, the richer it becomes, and four risks follow:
    • Prioritising badly: your group's concerns look bigger than everyone else's, creating local maxima; check prior art.
    • Losing empathy:
      • a fish-eye lens makes your work huge and others' trivial ("I could solve that in a weekend")
      • it also hampers engineers talking to non-engineers (xkcd 2501) and, in incidents, makes teams forget users are waiting.
    • Tuning out background noise: after months of a mucky configuration file or a broken deploy process you stop seeing it, even when it nears a crisis (the boiling-frog metaphor).
    • Forgetting what the work is for: the goal may have changed or been solved, nobody owns the end result, and you may lose sight of ethics.
  • Seeing bigger (techniques):
    • Outsider view.
      • Reilly's colleague Mark noticed her facial expression when he described the old systems, then two years later she was the insider and needed a newer "new person"
      • Dan Na: a new person can always see the problems
      • Warning: being new is not a licence to be a jerk (Amazon principal engineers' tenet "Respect what came before").
    • Escape the echo chamber.
      • Working with product teams after a decade in infrastructure shook Reilly's beliefs: they moved fast and valued features as much as reliability
      • Befriend other staff engineers (Figures 2-3 and 2-4 show staff engineers scoped to a group, principal engineers to an org, each part of a virtual team) and people beyond engineering.
    • What is actually important?
      • Technology is a means to an end
      • Priorities change with company stage: when customers leave for missing core features, it is not the time to push technical debt
      • Goals include objectives that are always true (Trish Craine): exist, pay everyone, good reputation.
    • What do customers care about?
      • Charity Majors's sticker "Nines don't matter when users aren't happy"
      • Mohit Suley's team tracked down unreliable ISPs where Bing was unreachable, since users do not tell DNS, ISP, CDN or endpoint apart. Internal customers count too.
    • Solved before?
      • Amazon's "many problems are not essentially new"
      • design docs need an "alternatives considered" section (see chapter 5). The "Industry Perspective" box lists LeadDev and SREcon conferences, Rands Leadership Slack, InfoQ, the VOID report, SRE Weekly, Raw Signal and the Thoughtworks Radar.
  • Keep it fresh: all-hands, skip-level one-on-ones, face time with customers. If you cannot explain why your work needs a staff engineer, you may be doing the wrong thing.

3.3 The topographical map: navigating the terrain

  • Team tectonics:
    • domains of responsibility push against each other, forming ridges and chasms
    • reorganisations cut communication, overloaded teams entrench, a new senior leader can cause an earthquake.
  • Without the map: good ideas get no traction, you find hard parts too late, and everything takes longer. "Politics" is really good engineering with humans in the system.

What is the culture?

  • Candidates usually ask "what's the culture?" meaning:
    • autonomy, inclusion, safety to fail, part of decisions, ease of progress, and are people nice. Published values are aspirational
    • real values are what happens every day.

  • Seven sliders
    • (Figure 2-8): secret or open
    • oral or written
    • top-down or bottom-up
    • fast or deliberate change
    • back channels or front doors
    • allocated or available
    • liquid or crystallized. Where you land changes how to frame ideas, whom to convince first and how long things take.
    • Example: in a "liquid, meritocratic" company, success still depends on opportunity and sponsorship and is affected by bias (Reilly points to the Is Tech a Meritocracy? site).
    • In a crystallized team, proposing someone else's earmarked promotion project is "like suggesting stealing the silverware".
    • Culture can be nudged with determined effort.
  • Power, rules or mission (Ron Westrum, 2004):

Pathological Bureaucratic Generative
Orientation power rules performance
Cooperation low modest high
Messengers shot neglected trained
Responsibilities shirked narrow risks shared
Bridging discouraged tolerated encouraged
Failure leads to scapegoating justice inquiry
Novelty crushed creates problems implemented

Points of interest

  • Chasms
    • (for example product engineering versus infrastructure)
    • fortresses (gatekeepers who care about quality; bring sponsorship or satisfy their "password")
    • disputed territory (Reilly's migration with ownership smeared across three teams; the rollback was not pretty)
    • uncrossable deserts (a battle others call unwinnable)
    • paved roads, shortcuts and goat tracks (the new paved road may not lead where people go; teams may ask you not to document the undocumented path).
  • Add your own: guardrails that are missing, rude behaviours, leaders who cause reorganisations, who is at war with whom. Cartography is political: notice what you put at the centre.

How are decisions made?

  • Being technically correct is only the beginning; you must convince the right people. Feeling constantly out of the loop means you do not know how decisions get made.
  • Where is "the room"?
    • Weekly managers' meetings, a director's staff meeting, an architecture Slack channel
    • there may be no room at all (one-on-ones, or bottom-up decisions never made). Ask someone you trust to walk you through a past decision (and say you are not fighting it).
  • Asking to join:
    • argue from impact on the organisation, not your career
    • adding a person is not free (slower, less candour)
    • follow Will Larson's "Getting in the Room": show up prepared, speak concisely, be low friction.
    • If refused, do not take it personally; be friendly and do good work.
    • Some rooms you should stay out of as an individual contributor (compensation, performance); suggest splitting them from technical topics.
    • The room may hold less power than you think: Reilly found directors who felt unable to influence people two levels above.
  • Shadow org chart
    • (Fitzpatrick and Collins-Sussman, Debugging Teams): who the director listens to, such as a ten-year veteran, the "connectors" who know people everywhere, and "old-timers" with informal weight
    • Convince them.

Keeping the map current

  • Automated announcement channels
    • walking the floor (gemba, staying attached to others' work, which also protects your technical credibility)
    • lurking (senior calendars, meeting notes, the newest Slack channels)
    • making time to read RFCs
    • checking in with leaders and sponsors
    • talking with people, including customer support and admin staff.
  • "I don't know how to talk to people":
    • networking can be friendly rather than grubby
    • start by asking a question, showing interest or complimenting something a person chose to do (a well-written RFC, a smooth meeting), never their looks
    • small talk is learnable, and with someone more junior it is your job to make it easy.

Be a bridge

  • Human problems slow organisations most:
    • teams not talking, decisions nobody owns, power struggles
    • Westrum's "bridging" means sending the missing email summary, introducing people who should have met, or writing a document showing how projects connect.
  • Google's DevOps site suggests inviting someone whose work you do not understand (or that frustrates you, such as procurement) to coffee.
  • Where you can, define your scope across the plates so you can catch dropped work, mediate and create a single story.

3.4 The treasure map: remind me where we are going

  • Only short-term goals leave you unable to keep everyone aligned, finish big things, avoid cruft, avoid competing initiatives, or grow engineers.
  • Tech tree:
    • as in the game Civilization (you cannot build a railroad without bridge building and steam engines), you build a service mesh to make the microservices framework easier, to let new services start quickly, to let teams ship faster, to cut time to market
    • Knowing the real goal lets you judge whether proposed work gets closer.
  • Share the map: where you are, where you are going, why each step, a clear definition of success, and progress so far (Figure 2-14); no distractions.
  • If paths compete or none exists, write a short summary of the confusion to force the conversation, and if still no destination, create a new map (chapter 3).
  • Your personal journey: staff impact is slow to show, so keep a narrative of what you did and what you are heading for. The "trail map" comes in chapter 9.

Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the book)

  • Pick one person outside engineering (support, product or an admin) and ask one question about their work. Written questions or a short message first lowers the pressure on live English.
  • Ask your manager to walk you through how one past decision was made (the book's own advice, with the framing that you are learning, not fighting it).
  • Notice one "that's interesting" fact this week and write it in a note.

Summary points

  • Three maps: locator, topographical, treasure.
  • Learn the culture sliders and Westrum's type; find the room and the shadow chart.
  • Be a bridge; if there is no destination, draw one.
Terms to carry forward
  • Fog of war: the part of the organisational map you cannot yet see.
  • Objectives that are always true: implicit goals such as exist, pay people, keep a good reputation.
  • Shadow org chart: informal lines of influence.
  • Westrum typology: pathological, bureaucratic, generative.
  • Gemba: walking the floor to see how work really happens.
  • Bridging: connecting parts of an organisation that otherwise cannot share information.
