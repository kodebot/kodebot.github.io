The Staff Engineer's Path 5: Finite Time

Source: Tanya Reilly, The Staff Engineer's Path (O'Reilly, 2022), chapter 4. Own-words summary, much shorter than the source.

Takeaways
  • You cannot do everything: every commitment has an opportunity cost. Choosing what to work on is part of the job.
  • Beyond "is it important to the company?" ask "is it right for me?" using five resources: energy, quality of life, credibility, social capital, skills.
  • Put focus work, not just meetings, in your calendar, and keep a buffer.
  • If a project is wrong for you: do it anyway (briefly), compensate, let someone else lead, resize it, or say no.

5.1 Time

  • You have 168 hours a week for the rest of your life. Even five minutes spent fixing a dead link means not replying to an email.
  • Senior people see many problems, and when someone points out one more, they add it to a list: you must accept walking past broken things.
  • Put non-meetings in the calendar (advice from coach Fabianna Tassini). Calendars usually show only the interruptions to focus work.
    • Specific items, such as "make slides for the all-hands" or "read Monday's design", not vague "make time" blocks.
    • It shows what a side quest displaces, lets you answer "when can you review my 20-page document?" honestly, and reveals avoided work (if you reschedule it repeatedly, it may not matter).
  • A time graph for a month or quarter: block the fixed overhead first, then fit projects; each new commitment is a block.
  • How busy do you like to be?
    • Know your normal week, your spike limit and where you fall over (Figure 4-3 compares people A to C)
    • Fully allocated time means a surprise forces you to drop something important or work beyond capacity.
  • Not a priority queue: big and small work mix, and engineers differ in fit, so also weigh what suits you.

5.2 Five resources

  • Reilly borrows The Sims need bars: your levels of five resources, affected by each project.
  • Energy: focus ("smartbrain") is finite, and different work drains different people. Outside life, such as a baby that does not sleep or illness, counts.
  • Quality of life:
    • enjoying the work and people, believing in the goals, and money. Work can pay well and still make you unhappy
    • staying on a disliked job as a step toward a future goal is valid.
  • Credibility: others' belief you can do it.
    • Built by solving hard problems, visible competence and consistent judgment
      • lost by absolutism (advocating one technology everywhere), rude or dramatic behaviour, jargon walls, or rambling all-hands questions.
    • Staying too long in an ivory tower makes engineers distrust your judgment. Carla Geisser: "the Giant Maybe Unsolvable Problem will be easier after you've shown you can get things done."
    • Implicit bias affects credibility; if you get extra for free, use it to boost others.
  • Social capital:
    • whether people want to help you (trust, friendship, feeling you are owed a favour)
    • Built by helping and by finished projects; burned by refusing a critical request or not repaying favours
    • Spend it deliberately: "if you waste your 'one unreasonable request' token, you won't get it back."
  • Skills:
    • always slowly eroding
    • Increase by deliberate learning, working with highly skilled people (being the least skilled on a great team teaches more than being the best on a mediocre one), or learning by doing.
  • No formula:
    • optimise different resources at different times
    • some projects need a minimum level
    • sometimes pick a poor fit for another reason. It is a multidimensional bin-packing problem, so do not overthink small tasks.

5.3 Choosing projects

  • Origins:
    • invited to join
    • ask to join (do not hint and wait)
    • your own idea
    • the fire alarm (crisis)
    • claiming a problem (side quests)
    • a grassroots effort
    • "someone needs to..."
    • meddling.
    • A grassroots working group works with organisational buy-in, a clear time commitment, exit criteria and a decision process; otherwise it is a social group.
    • Crises feel good but constant firefighting leaves no narrative.
    • Avoid "lobbing a water balloon": meddling then leaving.
  • What are you signing on for? Understand its shape and growth (some projects ramp up later), estimate honestly (a weekly hour includes prep and actions), set boundaries and exit criteria, and use go/no-go points or timeboxed experiments for risky work.
  • Other people's requests are not always more important than your plan: do not drop everything for a minor outage or an instant Slack reply, because only you watch your own resources.

  • Questions by resource:
    • Energy: how many things you already care about (Reilly: about five at once), whether the work gives or takes energy, whether you are procrastinating by "snacking" (low effort, low impact work; Des Traynor), and whether the fight is worth it (Camille Fournier on "OPP").
    • Quality of life:
      • do you enjoy the kind of work (technical, product, project, people), intensity, collaboration, pairing, on call, travel, public speaking, and whether you feel safe with the team
      • are you comfortable with the goals?
    • Credibility: does it use your technical skills, show leadership (owning outcomes, clear communication), and what is the risk of failure?
    • Social capital: does the work match your company's and manager's expectations at your level (managing up means helping them succeed), will peers respect it, and are you squandering capital?
      • Example from the book: a new senior hire pushed a fix nobody needed, pulled two new grads off their projects for months, then lost esteem.
      • Sponsorship also costs you: if the person you vouch for fails, it reflects on your judgment (watch for sponsoring only people like yourself).
    • Skills: which stories do you want on your future résumé, and will people around you raise your game ("I'll get to learn how CTOs talk to people")?

5.4 What if it is the wrong project?

  • Do it anyway:
    • acceptable for a while (take one for the team), if you know why and the exit. If it is sapping you, tell your manager plainly ("This project is making me really unhappy")
    • good managers listen, and if nothing changes you may be in the wrong team or company, or a big fish that needs a bigger pond.
  • Compensate: add a side project for what is missing, or make room; "torch the back burner" (Grace Vigeant) by deciding to stop caring.
  • Let others lead:
    • Michael Lopp says to "aggressively delegate": you could get an A, they will get a B, which is great for a first time, and you can coach them
    • If you are the most senior, do not take every chance to be publicly competent.
  • Resize: join for the first month, act as a consultant, meet once, recommend someone, or care for the length of one meeting. "The worst they can say is no."
  • Just don't do it:
    • not all problems are yours
    • Saying no is the price of good work
    • Look at how admired colleagues decline; scripts such as "I wish there were two of me", "Unfortunately, now is not a good time", "Thank you so much for thinking of me, but I can't!"
    • Reilly tags emails with #isaidno.

5.5 Worked examples

  • All-hands talk after an 18-month project:
    • it boosts credibility and capital but costs vacation and energy. Ask whether it is a new opportunity for you or someone else needs it more (pick someone who will do the work)
    • reshape (shorter, co-present, postpone).
  • Incident-commander on-call rotation: a skill, credibility and capital boost, but time and out-of-hours energy; decide on current quality of life; try six months.
  • The exciting project you cannot fit: Reilly would take it, discuss with the manager how important the process work is, and block the new project's time in the calendar first.
  • "I want to want to": pay attention to the feeling; look for a variant (coach the lead, join for a month); tell your manager what kind of work you want.

5.6 Defend your time

  • Reilly hesitated to ask a busy VP for advice
    • a friend said, "Ask her. You don't get to that level without knowing how to defend your time." Demands grow while hours stay the same (Will Larson on life filling up), so choose deliberately.

Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the book)

  • Block tomorrow's focus work in your calendar before anyone else books it.
  • Learn two short ways of saying no and say them aloud: the book's "Thank you so much for thinking of me, but I can't!" is short and plain.
  • Ask for one project you want, in writing, rather than waiting to be invited. A written request gives you time to find your words.
  • Remember social capital is built by finishing things: pick one visible commitment this month and finish it well.

Summary points

  • Time is finite; show it in your calendar and keep a buffer.
  • Weigh energy, quality of life, credibility, social capital and skills as well as company impact.
  • Reshape, delegate or decline: focus means sometimes saying no.
Terms to carry forward
  • Opportunity cost, time graph, bin packing.
  • Smartbrain: Reilly's family word for focus energy.
  • Credibility vs social capital: believing you can versus wanting to help you.
  • Snacking: low-effort, low-impact work (Des Traynor).
  • Torch the back burner: decide to stop caring about a task.
