The Staff Engineer's Path 7: Why Have We Stopped?

Source: Tanya Reilly, The Staff Engineer's Path (O'Reilly, 2022), chapter 6. Own-words summary, much shorter than the source.

Takeaways
  • Projects stop because they are blocked, lost, or have "arrived" too early; they also end on purpose (done enough, wrong journey, canceled, or victory).
  • Four techniques unblock most things: understand and explain, make the work easier, get organisational support, make alternative plans.
  • Done means the user can use it ("nobody wants to use software, they want to catch a Pokemon"): define done, market it, and keep the foundations solid.
  • You can restart other people's projects too, but defend your time.

7.1 Why projects stop

  • You are the driver: roadblocks, a lost map, passengers who disagree on the destination, or the realisation you should go elsewhere.
  • As a leader you can help projects you do not lead
    • Will Larson, quoted: many projects are "one small change away from succeeding..
    • or one conversation away from consensus", and a small effort from you can shift the outcome.
    • But seeing a problem does not mean you should jump on it (Figure 6-1: a time graph full of side quests and no room for your own commitment).

7.2 You are stuck in traffic

  • Four techniques:
    • Understand and explain: debug the blockage; make sure everyone shares the same picture.
    • Make the work easier: need less from the people you wait for.
    • Get organisational support: show the work is an objective; escalate when needed.
    • Make alternative plans.
  • Blocked by another team.
    • Three likely causes: misunderstandings
    • misadventure (someone quits or falls sick, they are overloaded)
    • misalignment (Figure 6-2: Team 2's top priority is Team 1's third).
    • Go and find out, by voice if email fails, explaining why it matters, what you need and by when.
    • Ask for something smaller or help unblock them; offering to do part of the work may not help (supporting an untrained person can cost more than doing it yourself).
    • Ask leadership which project should "win"
      • if yours is genuinely more important, escalate with unemotional facts to a mutual leader, and talk to your sponsor first. Escalating is "a polite conversation", not complaining.
    • Otherwise rescope or ship later and tell stakeholders.
  • Blocked by a decision.
    • Do not assume the decider's job is easy ("Why won't product just...?")
    • they may wait on someone else or not understand the question (an engineer asked "X or Y?" and heard "Yes, that would be great!").
    • Be on the same side, explain impact on what they care about.
    • Reframe with pictures, user stories or examples; mediate between conflicting stakeholders; give talking points; say what is hard to reverse and what does not matter.
    • If you must, keep options open or make a best guess:
      • document the trade-offs, tell the decider you are guessing, and mitigate the worst case
      • Lightweight Architectural Decision Records are suggested.
  • Blocked by a single button click.
    • Reilly's load-balancing team asked for a week's notice though each request took half an hour, because it supported four critical services for hundreds of teams
    • the team motto was "lack of planning on your part is not an emergency on mine".
    • Approvers also share accountability when they approve.
    • Ask politely and apologise for skipping the queue; say thank you (peer bonuses, tea, a mention in the launch email).
    • Make your request one of the easy ones (Figure 6-3: all information laid out, little reading).
    • Escalating may build bad blood, so be clear you understand the process and ask once.
  • Blocked by a single person. The excuses may hide the real reason: intimidation, being stuck, oversubscription, stress, or not understanding after two explanations.
    • Reilly's colleague, used to operations firefighting, could not start writing scripts; pairing got him going.
    • Be clear on why it matters, ask them to warn you early, set a go/no-go date, and break the work down.
    • Three Bullets and a Call to Action (Fitzpatrick and Collins-Sussman): at most three bullets and one call to action, easy to forward.
    • Coach without taking over ("Your colleague won't learn to drive if you take the wheel").
    • Eventually it is a people-management issue: talk to their manager.

  • Blocked by unassigned work.
    • Everyone has opinions but nobody commits staff (a plate-tectonics problem)
    • Figure 6-4: investing in which doomed path to take is pointless while the impassable ridge (staffing) remains.
    • Draw conclusions explicitly with a rollup (Denise Yu): "We won't have authentication until at least Q3" is an interpretation of two facts nobody had combined.
    • Offer to mentor the owning team, line up a sponsor with a clear pitch; if staffing is always "next quarter", postpone.
  • Blocked by a huge crowd (a migration). Teams may be willing but busy, opposed, or fatigued by constant changes; a half-migration hurts everyone.
    • Tell both stories (the infrastructure team is not "just playing with new technology", the product team is not ignoring debt).
    • Do as much of the work as you can, automate, specify exact edits, make the new way the default and the old way harder (allow-lists, a config flag such as i_know_this_is_unsupported).
    • Show a progress graph heading to zero ("Everyone else is doing it, why aren't we?").
    • Get it onto organisational goals. As a last resort, with strong backing, withdraw support for the old way or make the last holdouts its owners: "Last out turns out the lights."

7.3 You are lost

  • You do not know where you are going. Forty teams on a legacy architecture produce analysis paralysis ("there's no project to steer").
    • Clarify roles: hear everyone, but you decide, and your sponsor backs you.
    • Choose a strategy: ban implementation details until the problem is agreed; choose a small group to write one (chapter 3).
    • Choose a problem: any real problem, deliberately; wrong is better than vague.
    • Choose a stakeholder: deliver in vertical slices.
  • You do not know how to get there.
    • Articulate the problem; revisit assumptions (Leslie Lamport: "specify the precise problem to be solved independently of the method used in the solution").
    • Give it time (sleep, a vacation); schedule dedicated thinking time and look after your brain.
    • Look for prior art outside software (aviation, civil engineering, medicine)
      • learn from others
      • try a different angle (organisational versus technical, outsource it)
      • start smaller
      • ask for help (you amortise others' learning time).
  • You do not know where you stand. Priorities and leadership change; silence may mean it is going well or you were forgotten.
    • Clarify organisational support with your sponsor; clarify roles (RACI; an "unofficial lead" invites failure); ask for what you need, such as a mention at an all-hands.
    • Refuel: a new kickoff, a "Phase 2", or new energetic team members.

7.4 You have arrived... somewhere?

  • Figure 6-5: the team declared victory and went home but a better treasure remained.
  • It is code complete.
    • "Done" with hardcoded credentials, still in staging, a PR unreviewed
    • Agree a definition of done (Agile Alliance; "done-done"), run user acceptance tests, dogfood, and celebrate landings, not launches.
  • Nobody is using it.
    • "Beware of the Leopard" projects (from The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy): technically documented but undiscoverable
    • Michael R
    • Bernstein: a farmer who grows a crop and leaves it in the field.
    • Keep telling people, make it discoverable (links, shortlinks including misspellings: Google's Sisyphus project had redirects for common misspellings, which is also a security practice).
  • Built on a shaky foundation. "There's no such thing as a temporary solution" (a data centre cable run once stays).
    • Be the person who asks for tests, monitoring and documentation; put cleanup into user stories; negotiate fix-it weeks, 20 percent time, or a rotation.

7.5 The project just stops

  • A better place to stop: local maximum polish may be done enough; check you are not abandoning unhappy customers.
  • Not the right journey:
    • a team of senior engineers killed a new data storage system after discovering it would not scale, and wrote a retrospective
    • That was a success, not a failure
    • The sunk-cost fallacy keeps people on the wrong path; "That didn't work
    • It's OK
    • Here's what we learned" aids psychological safety.
  • Canceled: acknowledge your feelings, talk with a sounding board, tell the team directly (leading with the why), shut down cleanly (turn off, delete, document if restarting is realistic), protect promotions by briefing new managers, and celebrate.
  • Destination reached:
    • verify measurable goals, usability and foundations
    • then celebrate (parties, shout-outs, presentations, blog posts), hold a retrospective of what went right and highlight the behaviours you want to see.

Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the book)

  • Next time you are blocked, write a "Three Bullets and a Call to Action" message (three bullets, one request). A written request is easier to polish than a live conversation.
  • When a colleague is slow, ask once, kindly, "What would make this easier?" before escalating.
  • Write the project's definition of done in one paragraph from the user's point of view and share it.

Summary points

  • Diagnose a blockage by asking the other side's view; use the four techniques.
  • Agree the destination, roles and support when lost.
  • Done is when the user can use it; end projects deliberately and learn from them.
Terms to carry forward
  • Rollup: a summary of facts and interpretations in one place (Denise Yu).
  • Three Bullets and a Call to Action.
  • Definition of done, dogfooding, user acceptance testing.
  • Beware of the Leopard project: a solution built but hidden.
  • Sunk-cost fallacy.
  • Watermelon status (from chapter 5).
