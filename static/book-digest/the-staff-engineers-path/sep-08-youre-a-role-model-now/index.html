The Staff Engineer's Path 8: You're a Role Model Now (Sorry)

Source: Tanya Reilly, The Staff Engineer's Path (O'Reilly, 2022), chapter 7. Own-words summary, much shorter than the source.

Takeaways
  • With a senior title, your words and actions carry more weight: people assume you know what you are talking about and copy how you behave. This is passive influence; chapter 8 covers the deliberate kind.
  • Model four attributes: be competent, be responsible, remember the goal, look ahead.
  • You can do this quietly: many of the best leaders are quiet and thoughtful.
  • The measure of success: do other people want to work with you?

8.1 Why behaviour matters

  • Carla Geisser warned:
    • "Don't think out loud", because a half-baked idea will become a project
    • Ross Donaldson: being staff "does mean you need to be careful when you open your dang mouth"
    • A staff title makes people look to you for guidance.
  • Written values mean little:
    • what gets people promoted shows what the company values. If staff engineers rise through solitary heroics, collaboration messages ring hollow
    • if seniors approve PRs without reading them, everyone rubber-stamps reviews.
  • Being a good engineer includes being a good colleague: mature, constructive and accountable, or condescending and unavailable, shows juniors what "senior" means.
  • "But I don't want to be a role model!"
    • It does not mean being louder or a public figure
    • If leadership terrifies you, start small (compliment a success in a public channel, offer to onboard a new person) and practise as you would learn a language.
  • The four attributes are aspirational, not gatekeeping; if the advice contradicts your judgment, trust yourself.

8.2 Be competent

  • Know things.
    • Technical skill comes from study and practice, not aptitude:
      • Stephanie Van Dyk compares it to weaving
      • Paula Muldoon, an orchestral violinist, wants to be so good that focus goes to others.
    • Experience takes years:
      • the American Society of Civil Engineers' grades ask for 8+, 10+, 15+ and 20+ years for the upper levels
      • staff engineers typically have 10+ and principals 15+ (software is less rigorous)
        • Charity Majors warns against becoming a manager before about five to seven years of writing and shipping code
      • Reilly says the same for staff+ roles that take you away from the technology.
    • "Not technical enough" is vague and dismissive: say which (years? domain? a specific skill?).
    • In a new domain, pattern-match from other experience and learn quickly (trade-offs, constraints, vocabulary, the "famous people").
    • Stay up to date; remain anchored in the technology, not only in how the company operates; show that you are learning and explain how you reached a conclusion.
  • Be self-aware.
    • Admit what you know: do not set the bar at "best in the industry"; volunteer your skills ("it's not a brag, just a statement of fact").
    • Admit what you do not know:
      • bluffing loses learning and sets a bad example
      • ask for an "ELI5" (explain it like I am five) with the social contract that nobody takes offence at basics.
    • Understand your own context; explaining something simply is a real sign of expertise.
  • Have high standards. Write clear documentation, be first to know when your software breaks; use duct tape deliberately, judged by the problem, not by how interesting the work is.
    • Seek constructive criticism: "Your solutions are not you."
    • Own your mistakes:
      • do not get defensive, deflect or fall apart
      • admit what happened, fix it, communicate quickly, clear anyone who might be blamed, apologise for hurt feelings, and hold a retrospective. A leader's openness improves psychological safety.
  • Be reliable: the compliment "Alex will be in that meeting, so I don't need to go" means the right thing will happen. Finish what you start, or stop deliberately and say so.

8.3 Be responsible

  • "Nobody else is coming": you are the "someone" in "someone should do something".
  • Take ownership.
    • Own the whole problem. Avoid "Cover Your Ass Engineering" (John Allspaw): "It's not my fault. They broke it, they used it wrong. I built it to spec."
    • Radiate intent
      • (Elizabeth Ayer): signal what you will do before doing it
      • you give context, invite intervention and keep the responsibility ("it doesn't transfer the blame the way seeking permission does").
    • Make decisions:
      • like an engineer who seals a document and takes liability, choose decisively, explain your reasoning, and make the cost of being wrong low
      • You should be able to vote against your own preference.
    • Ask the "obvious" questions
      • others will not (who is on call for a mission-critical service with two engineers? have you run this by security?)
      • Coming from a senior person they teach the team what a design doc must answer.
    • Do not delegate through neglect:
      • "glue work" (unblocking, onboarding, reminders, mentoring, scheduling; Reilly's viral talk) falls to juniors with a strong sense of ownership, costing them technical growth, and is dismissed when they do it but counted as leadership when a staff engineer does
      • Do it yourself and redirect juniors to technical work.
  • Take charge (a gap and stepping up, not prior authority).
    • Emergency: announce that you are coordinating (the Incident Command System: the commander coordinates rather than fights the fire), keep notes, share context, ask people to radiate intent.
    • Ask for information when everyone is confused:
      • "the FooService has 1% 401 errors" means nothing without a baseline
      • it is safer for seniors to say "I don't know what to do with that information."
    • Feigned surprise
      • ("You've seriously never used Linux?") shuts down questions
      • the Recurse Center named it and made a lightweight social rule against it. Reassure anyone who apologises for a basic question
      • that builds culture.
    • Drive meetings: agenda, purpose, and notes (glue work that is high status when a senior does it; notes are "flow control").
    • If you see something, say something.
      • When someone says something disrespectful in a public channel, respond publicly (praise in public, criticise in private does not apply here)
      • Sarah Milstein: the adrenaline does not go away, speak anyway, even later ("I wish I'd addressed it at the time")
      • Reference the culture you want, give the person a path to your side, appeal to their values in private, and tell a manager if there is a pattern.
  • Create calm.
    • Defuse, do not amplify: make big problems smaller, keep small ones small; ask questions; do not spill your worries onto juniors; be clear when venting versus asking for action.
    • Avoid blame:
      • a colleague said of Reilly's first production mistake, "We've all been there"
      • after an outage ask what happened, what led here, what information was missing, where mental models diverged.
    • Be consistent and predictable, and look after yourself (remember Figure 4-3 and leave space).

8.4 Remember the goal

  • There is a business:
    • the software is a means. Adapt: a hackathon lead found tests a waste because the code was disposable
    • in an outage the fastest fix (turn it off and on) beats the clean rolling restart. Priorities change with the company
    • budgets exist (know the good and lean times)
    • headcount is finite
    • spend "innovation tokens" (Dan McKinley) carefully
    • build the most useful thing, not the most fun.
  • There is a user:
    • a vendor said "not many people use Chrome" in a cafeteria where everyone did. Write requirements, get APIs and mockups reviewed first
    • if the user is not happy you built the wrong thing.
  • There is a team: do not be a single point of failure; your impact is "what wouldn't have happened without you", not what you did personally.

8.5 Look ahead

  • "Software engineering is programming integrated over time" (Titus Winters, Software Engineering at Google). Your code may last 5 or 10 years
    • it is fine to plant seeds you will not see grow.
  • Anticipate what you will wish you had done;
    • telegraph what is coming (announce an intent to deprecate)
    • tidy up (like a tool shed where everything is at arm's reach)
    • keep tools sharp (faster builds, delete flaky tests; speeding recovery shortens every outage)
    • create institutional memory (decision records, diagrams that include what "everyone knows", comments with context, searchable keywords; John Reese curated the history of Google's site reliability organisation).
  • Expect failure:
    • Fran Garcia's retrospective on Hosted Graphite being hit by an AWS outage although it did not use AWS (slow users kept connections open until a load balancer limit was reached)
    • test error paths, plan, drill (game days, chaos engineering)
    • "if you haven't tested restoring your backups, assume you don't have any backups".
  • Optimise for maintenance, not creation:
    • make it understandable (docs for the future reader, one big simple picture with arrows for data flow, observability)
    • keep it simple (find a second solution; Martin Fowler: "Any fool can write code that a computer can understand"; Ryan Harter: complexity is a cost to bear, not a thing to maximise)
    • contain inherent complexity in one component
    • build to decommission (clean interfaces, easy to see which clients remain).
  • Create future leaders:
    • let juniors do hard work
    • John Allspaw: "The degree to which other people want to work with you is a direct indication of how successful you'll be..
    • Be the engineer that everyone wants to work with."

Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the book)

This chapter fits your situation closely, because several of its habits do not depend on fluent or fast English. These are my suggestions, not the book's.

  • Quiet is allowed.
    • The book says many of the best leaders are quiet and thoughtful, and that you influence through good decisions and effective collaboration
    • You do not need to be the loudest in the room.
  • Ask one "obvious" question per meeting. Short and plain: "Who is on call for this?" or "Did security see this?" The book says a senior person asking such questions makes the team write explicit answers.
  • Say "I don't know" or ask for an "ELI5"
    • once this week
    • The book says a senior person admitting this makes everyone else do it too, which also makes it easier for you to ask when you miss a word.
  • Radiate intent in writing: before a change, post two sentences ("I am going to do X by Friday, tell me if you see a problem"). It builds trust without relying on live speech.
  • Own a mistake in one plain sentence: "I got that wrong. Here is what I am doing about it."
  • Pichai study:
    • see sundar-pichai-study
    • The book's quiet-leader habits (own it, listen, make room for others) are the ones his own words show: for example, owning the 2018 walkout plainly.
  • Practise any spoken version in speaking-practice/ (link only).

Summary points

  • Behaviour sets culture; competence, responsibility, goal focus and foresight are the four attributes.
  • Ownership, radiating intent, calm and consistency make you a leader without volume.
  • Plan for failure, simplicity and decommissioning; be someone people want to work with.
Terms to carry forward
  • Glue work: unassigned leadership and administrative tasks.
  • Radiating intent (Elizabeth Ayer).
  • Cover Your Ass Engineering (John Allspaw).
  • ELI5: "explain it like I'm five".
  • Feigned surprise: pretending shock at what others do not know.
  • Incident Command System.
  • Innovation tokens (Dan McKinley).
