The Staff Engineer's Path 10: What's Next?

Source: Tanya Reilly, The Staff Engineer's Path (O'Reilly, 2022), chapter 9. Own-words summary, much shorter than the source.

Takeaways
  • The last chapter levels up you: know what matters to you, draw a trail map, and invest in skills, network, visibility and well-chosen roles.
  • Check your role's health with five signals and decide whether to stay or move.
  • Paths from here include keeping the job, promotion, working less, changing teams, management (the pendulum), finding your own niche, new employers, a startup, independence or a change of career.
  • If you reset, redraw your three maps. And take software seriously: senior people set the industry's culture.

10.1 Your career

  • A friend compared careers to Diablo: clear the dungeon, level up, then enter a new dungeon where the monsters have levelled up too. "What is the point, for you?"
  • The trail map
    • (the fourth map after chapter 2's three): paths marked, overgrown or absent
    • some destinations are only reached by leaving the trail. If you only follow marked paths (Figure 9-1) you may miss where you wanted to go.
  • What is important to you? Cian Synnott's priority list. Common items:
    • financial security
    • supporting your family
    • a flexible schedule
    • learning a lot
    • being visible
    • doing cool things
    • challenging yourself
    • building wealth
    • working for yourself
    • making a difference
    • enabling a vocation.
    • It is personal and changes.
  • Where are you going?
    • Plot milestones ("where do you see yourself in five years?")
    • Do not draw it alone: read, attend conferences, ask people about their paths
    • At staff+ your manager may not know your path, so seek your own advice, teaching, guardrails and opportunities
    • If an ambitious path excites you, consider it: you can take steps toward it.
  • What do you need to invest in? Imagine the successful future you: which skills, résumé lines and contacts?

  • Skills:
    • reframe "I'm bad at X" as "not yet levelled up" (Figure 9-2: ability points as in Final Fantasy)
    • Invest in what you want to be good at; everything in tech is learnable if worth the time, though you cannot be expert in everything
    • Do not make it harder than needed: Charity Majors says to know what makes you happy and spend your time mostly in line with it, since energy-draining work is "antithetical to your success"
    • Dina Levitan: she was not bad at axe throwing, she used the wrong axe.
    • Imposter syndrome is common among staff engineers: put points into what makes you nervous and give yourself a break.
  • Network:
    • a large share of jobs and projects are found through contacts (the book cites estimates of 70 to 85 percent of jobs as unpublished)
    • Contacts also show what "competent" looks like in other roles
    • Yvette Pasqua: reach out about a specific topic, join only energising communities, meet one-on-one at events; "none of us know who to talk to or how to start"
    • Vanessa Van Edwards's Science of People site offers learnable tips.
  • Visibility:
    • skills nobody sees cannot be invited. An external reputation (open source, articles, podcasts, talks) is optional but costs ability points
    • do not waste an opportunity, be visibly competent.
  • Choose roles and projects deliberately:
    • you get better at what you spend time on and are easily typecast (five years of storage systems)
    • Ask people living the life you want which experiences got them there (Mason Jones).

10.2 Your current role

  • Cate Huston's five metrics: are you learning
    • transferable skills or coping with dysfunction
    • how you feel about recruiting others
    • confidence
    • stress. Track them as 0 to 5 over months (Table 9-1) to avoid recency bias.
    • "Sometimes five years of experience is just...the same year of experience, five times over."
    • Your employer buys your time but rents your "brand" (how others perceive you).
    • Captain Awkward's question: if things stayed exactly as they are, how long would you stay?
  • Does it meet your list (Figure 9-3)? Few roles do everything: money versus doing good, learning versus prestige, accomplishments versus energy left.
  • Reasons to stay: feedback loops, depth, relationships, context (you know the shadow org chart), familiarity.
  • Reasons to move: employability, experiences, growth, money, mismatch.

10.3 Paths from here

  • Keep doing what you do: fine; even then your skills and communication norms age.
  • Work toward promotion:
    • ask what you really want (cash, prestige, scope, respect, "the room")
    • understand the company's process
    • you need to be as impactful as the person was when promoted, not as they are now.
  • Work less:
    • Jens Rantil took 80 percent time for 20 percent less pay ("Every Thursday is a Friday!")
    • decide where hours come from, expect lower pay to affect retirement savings, and align with your manager and team.
  • Change teams: keep context and capital, build bridges (Burin Asavesna: a new low-level character who knows the game).
  • Build a new specialty; explore through rotations (a staff engineer tried three teams in two years).
  • Take a management role.
    • Charity Majors's pendulum: switch between manager and IC every few years
    • management is a change of profession, not a promotion
    • "you can only really improve at one of these things at a time". Will Larson: a hybrid role is only wise if you have already been both.
    • First direct reports:
      • to meet a future goal, for leverage, or at the top of the ladder
      • Amanda Walker: spend time as a line manager before managing managers
      • Majors: commit to at least two years
      • Camille Fournier: you must give up some technical focus.
  • Find or invent your niche:
    • "holes shaped like you" (Molly Graham), but beware roles that sound tailor-made yet exhaust you
    • Keavy McMinn crafted a technical adviser role to Stripe's chief product officer by asking "This is what I'm good at and really want to do"
    • Fabianna Tassini: make about 70 percent of the role energising and 30 percent practice.
  • Same job, new employer: avoid the "rebound" job (Molly Graham); ask what staff means there (Amy Unger); ask about the interview slate.
  • Up a level (easier in a domain you know; check levels.fyi) or down a level (Josh Kaderlan: a more senior colleague everywhere to learn from; Stacey Gammon swings between leadership and hands-on roles).
  • Startup (James Kirk: build a cash safety net first), independent (Emily Bache: needs a strong network and constant marketing; Vlad Ionescu: less money, more stress than many expect; Chris Vasselli: learn all the functions before going indie), or change careers (Peter Lyons runs a retreat for programmers).

10.4 Prepare to reset, and your choices matter

  • Reset:
    • in a new role your three maps are out of date
    • Cindy Sridharan: leaders who mould the organisation in the image of their last workplace tend to fail harder than those who learn its ropes and adapt
    • Talk to many people, find the rooms, learn the shadow org chart, assume past decisions had reasons, and enjoy the quiet calendar.
  • Take software seriously:
    • software wastes time and causes stress when it fails
      • AI bias, abuse, privacy leaks and addictive apps hurt people
      • life-critical systems (planes, medicine, nuclear plants) will be built by the engineers you level up
    • Canadian engineers wear a faceted ring to remember their obligations
    • when engineers are called senior after four or five years and move on every two or three, the incentive is to build for the current promotion
      • do not send "shoddy systems and technical debt" to today's teenagers
    • "What the senior people do sets the culture for the industry." Final words: build good software, a good software career, a good software industry.

Use it Monday (suggestions, not from the book)

  • Write your own priority list (five items) and check it against your current role, using the book's five metrics scored 0 to 5. Keep it in a note; repeat monthly.
  • Pick one skill to put "ability points" into this quarter. If it is spoken English, the book frames it as "not yet levelled up", not a fixed trait
    • see HBR Managing Across Cultures 7: Global Business Speaks English and speaking-practice/ (link only).
  • Ask one person you admire (for example a senior person who also leads across cultures) about the experiences that shaped their path: a specific question, as Yvette Pasqua suggests.
  • Pichai's own path (Chennai, Stanford, a Chrome project that began with a team of about ten) is in sundar-pichai-study, with sources; compare it with your trail map.

Summary points

  • Know what matters, plot your trail, invest in skills, network, visibility and the right roles.
  • Track job health; stay or move for clear reasons.
  • Many paths exist; reset your maps when you move; take your responsibility to the industry seriously.
Terms to carry forward
  • Trail map: the career counterpart of the three maps.
  • Ability points: Reilly's metaphor for where you invest learning effort.
  • Engineer/manager pendulum (Charity Majors).
  • Rebound job: a job taken just to escape the last one (Molly Graham).
  • Tech lead manager: a hybrid role.
