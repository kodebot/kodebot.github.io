Understanding Distributed Systems 9: Leader election

Source: Roberto Vitillo, Understanding Distributed Systems, 2nd ed. (v2.0.0, 2022), chapter 9. Previous: Understanding Distributed Systems 8: Time. Next: Understanding Distributed Systems 10: Replication.

[!abstract] The one-minute version Sometimes one process needs special powers (access a shared resource, assign work). The processes elect a leader, which stays in charge until it gives up or becomes unavailable, then they elect another. A correct algorithm gives safety (at most one leader at any time) and liveness (an election eventually completes, even with failures). The book explains Raft's election; in practice you rarely implement it and instead use a fault-tolerant key-value store with linearizable compare-and-swap and a TTL to hold a lease. A lease alone is not enough: pair it with a version number.

Safety and liveness

  • Safety: nothing bad happens (here: never two leaders). Liveness: something good eventually happens (an election finishes). These are general properties of distributed algorithms.

9.1 Raft leader election

Raft ("In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm", https://raft.github.io/raft.pdf) models each process as a state machine with three states: follower (recognises another process as leader), candidate (starts a new election proposing itself), leader.

  • Time is divided into election terms of arbitrary length, numbered with consecutive integers (logical timestamps)

    • A term begins with an election where one or more candidates try to become leader
    • At most one leader per term is guaranteed.

  • What triggers an election?

    • All processes start as followers
    • A follower expects a periodic heartbeat from the leader containing the term the leader was elected in
    • If none arrives within a time, a timeout fires, the leader is presumed dead, and the follower increments the current term, becomes a candidate, votes for itself, and asks all processes to vote for it, stamping the request with the term.

  • The candidate stays a candidate until one of three things happens:

    1. It wins. A majority of processes vote for it. Each process votes for at most one candidate per term, first come first served; the majority rule is what guarantees at most one winner per term. The winner becomes leader and starts sending heartbeats.
    2. Another process wins. The candidate receives a heartbeat from a process claiming leadership with a term greater than or equal to its own: it accepts the leader and returns to follower. (Otherwise it stays a candidate.) How can that happen? E.g. the candidate was stopped by a long garbage-collection pause and by the time it resumed, someone else had won. (The same happens if a leader receives a heartbeat with a greater term.)
    3. No winner. Several followers become candidates at once and nobody gets a majority: a split vote. The candidate eventually times out and starts a new election. The election timeout is picked randomly from a fixed interval to reduce the chance of another split vote.

9.2 Practical considerations

  • Other algorithms exist
    • Raft is simple and widely used, hence its choice
      • In practice you will rarely, if ever, implement leader election yourself. A good reason would be a need for zero external dependencies.
  • Instead use any fault-tolerant key-value store offering a linearizable compare-and-swap (CAS) operation with an expiration time (TTL)
    • (Linearizability is defined in section 10.3.1; CAS: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compare-and-swap.)
  • Compare-and-swap
    • takes K, Vo (old) and Vn (new): atomically, if the current value of K equals Vo, set it to Vn
    • otherwise K is unchanged and the operation fails. The TTL removes a key after it expires unless extended.
  • Lease:
    • each competing process tries to acquire a lease by creating a new key with CAS
    • The first to succeed is the leader until it stops renewing the lease, after which another can take over
    • Expiration can also be implemented client-side (like AWS's DynamoDB lock client: https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/database/building-distributed-locks-with-the-dynamodb-lock-client/) but that is more complex and still needs the store to offer CAS.

Why a lease is not enough

Suppose several processes update a file on a shared file store and a lease guards the critical section:

if lease.acquire():
      try:
          content = store.read(filename)
          new_content = update(content)
          store.write(filename, new_content)
      except:
          lease.release()

By the time the process writes, it may no longer hold the lease: the OS may have preempted it long enough for the lease to expire.

Checking the expiry against the local clock before writing assumes synchronised clocks, which are never exact, and the lease can expire while the write request is in flight because of network delay.

Checking that the lease is "far enough in the future" is a workaround, not foolproof: a lease cannot guarantee mutual exclusion by itself.

  • Fix:
    • give each file a version number, incremented on every update
    • The lease holder reads the file and its version, computes locally, then updates the file conditional on the version being unchanged, atomically with compare-and-swap (many file stores support it).
  • If the store has no conditional writes, design around occasional race conditions. Sometimes that is acceptable: two leaders performing the same idempotent update do no harm.

Costs of having a leader

  • A leader simplifies design (no concurrency) but can become a scalability bottleneck once its operations outgrow it, and it is a single point of failure with a large blast radius: if election stops working or the leader misbehaves, the whole system can go down.
  • Mitigation: partition and assign a different leader per partition, at the cost of complexity; many distributed data stores do this since they must partition data anyway.
  • Rule of thumb: if you must have a leader, minimise the work it performs and be prepared to occasionally have more than one.
  • Key assumption:
    • the store holding the leases is fault-tolerant
    • If it ran on one node and that node failed, no one could acquire leases
    • To survive node failure it must replicate its state: the next chapter.
